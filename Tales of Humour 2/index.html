    Tales of Humour 2 - 1. The Money-Lender Meets His Match

    One sunny afternoon, a poor, hard-working woodcutter was chopping wood deep in the forest. Suddenly, he heard a loud, terrifying snorting sound. Looking up, he saw a massive, angry wild buffalo charging aggressively right towards him!

    At once, he dropped his heavy axe and quickly scrambled up a tall tree, narrowly escaping the beast's sharp horns.

    Furious at losing its prey, the buffalo rammed the thick tree trunk again and again. THUD! THUD!

    "Help! Help!" the woodcutter cried out, clinging tightly to the swaying branches.

    After some time, the stubborn buffalo grew very tired from its useless efforts and finally fell fast asleep right at the base of the tree.

    "Saved!" the woodcutter sighed in immense relief. Now I'll quietly climb down and escape, he thought, looking down carefully at the sleeping beast.

    But just as he began to quietly climb down...

    "...Huh! Here comes Dayaram, the greedy money-lender!" the woodcutter muttered, spotting the cruel, wealthy man walking casually down the forest path.

    Dayaram was known throughout the village for his ruthless, cheating ways. Seeing him, the woodcutter smiled mischievously. I'll play a trick on him... hee hee hee! he thought.

    "Sssh... up here," the woodcutter whispered loudly from the branches.

    Dayaram stopped, looking up in confusion. "?"

    "Oh, it's you..." Dayaram sneered, recognizing the poor woodcutter.

    "Hush! Lower your voice!" the woodcutter warned him urgently, putting a finger to his lips.

    "But, why...?" Dayaram asked loudly.

    "That is the king's royal buffalo sleeping down there. You mustn't wake him up!" the woodcutter lied smoothly. Then he leaned down, whispering conspiringly. "Come up here quickly if you want to make some easy money."

    Dayaram's greedy eyes immediately lit up. "Money? Oh, good!"

    When the greedy money-lender had climbed up and settled uncomfortably on a branch, the woodcutter began his grand tale.

    "Now listen closely," the woodcutter whispered. "For a whole month now, I have been the official keeper of this royal buffalo. My job is merely to sit quietly on this tree and mind it while it rests. When I take it safely back to the palace in the evening, the king gives me a thousand gold coins."

    Dayaram's jaw dropped open in shock. "A thousand gold coins? For only a day's work?"

    "Yes—that's exactly how incredibly precious this buffalo is!" the woodcutter nodded seriously. "Having done this extremely easy job for so long, I have obviously become a very rich man."

    The woodcutter sighed, looking at Dayaram kindly. "Now I feel I should step aside and let somebody else make some easy money too."

    "How incredibly thoughtful and kind you are!" Dayaram quickly grasped his arm, completely blinded by his greed. "I will gladly take up the job! A very wise decision!"

    "The brute is still fast asleep. Good!" the woodcutter whispered, looking down at the snoring buffalo. As quietly as he could, the woodcutter carefully climbed down the tree, leaving the greedy money-lender perched high in the branches.

    "I must simply sit all day on this tree, you said?" Dayaram asked softly from above.

    "Sssh... ssh... yes, yes," the woodcutter nodded solemnly from the ground. "Or you certainly won't get your thousand gold coins."

    And with that, the woodcutter happily hurried home.

    So, all afternoon, the miserable money-lender sat uncomfortably on the hard tree branch, terrified to make a sound.

    "Oooh! How terribly stiff and sore my limbs have become," Dayaram groaned quietly, rubbing his aching back.

    Finally, in the evening, seeing the buffalo still sleeping, he stiffly climbed down. He approached the beast confidently.

    "Get up, you!" Dayaram yelled, kicking the buffalo. "I want my gold coins."

    The wild buffalo awoke with a start. GRRAH...! it bellowed, instantly enraged at being violently kicked.

    "OW!" Dayaram screamed as the massive beast bucked and chased him fiercely.

    "Aaah! He'll gore me to death. Help!" Dayaram shrieked in absolute terror, running wildly through the forest. RAM! The buffalo chased him relentlessly, repeatedly charging at him as he frantically scrambled back up the nearest tree.

    It was a very, very long time before the angry wild buffalo finally gave up and left the forest... and the bruised, terrified money-lender could carefully climb down again.

    "I... I never want to see a buffalo again!" Dayaram whimpered, limping painfully back to the village without a single gold coin.

    Tales of Humour 2 - 2. Sundar and the Seth

    One morning, Sundar saw his friend Sonu sitting idle by his hut, looking absolutely miserable.

    "Hey, Sonu! No work today?" Sundar called out cheerfully.

    "I've stopped working for Sethji," Sonu muttered darkly, hugging his knees. "I'd rather starve to death than work for him."

    "Why? What happened?" Sundar asked, sitting down beside his friend.

    Sonu touched his nose nervously. "He did not pay me a single paisa and... he was about to... c...cut off my nose."

    "How awful! But, why?"

    "Because that was his cruel condition when he hired me," Sonu explained. "He said that if I ever left his service of my own free will, he could legally cut off my nose. And if he sacked me, I could cut off his nose. But he works his servants so incredibly hard that they always quit first... and then he keeps their wages and threatens their noses!"

    "How strange!" Sundar smiled, rubbing his chin thoughtfully. "Your Sethji sounds like an incredibly interesting man. I'll go and offer my services to him today."

    Soon after, Sundar marched up to the wealthy man's large house. "Sethji, do you need a strong, hard-working servant?"

    The Seth looked at him shrewdly. "I do. But... I have certain strict conditions..."

    "I already know all about them, Sethji," Sundar interrupted with a wide smile.

    The Seth looked surprised, but pleased. "You do? Excellent! Then you can start immediately."

    A few days later, the Seth was talking to his wife. "He does the work of three men!" the Seth bragged happily.

    "And he eats as much as three men!" his wife complained.

    "Let him eat!" the Seth chuckled evilly. "Remember, because of our special agreement, he won't be getting a single paisa from us in wages! Eventually, he will beg to leave, and I will keep all his money."

    One day, as Sundar was passing by a deserted spot in the forest, he stumbled upon the rotting carcass of a dead wild horse. "A dead horse..." Sundar smiled. "That gives me a brilliant idea!"

    Sundar quickly cut off the dead horse's tail. Then, running back to the Seth's house, he secretly untied the Seth's beautiful, expensive mare, led it deep into the forest, and tied it securely to a hidden tree there.

    Afterwards, he planted the severed horse's tail firmly in a deep, muddy marsh near the village... and ran home screaming in mock terror.

    "Sethji! Sethji!" Sundar yelled, bursting into the courtyard. "Your expensive mare is sinking in the marsh! Hurry! Hurry!"

    "Oh!" the Seth cried, dropping his ledger and running as fast as he could.

    When they reached the marsh, Sundar pointed to the tail sticking out of the mud. "Look! It's completely gone in!"

    "Oh, the poor mare!" the Seth wailed, grabbing the tail and pulling with all his might.

    SPLOSH!

    The tail came completely loose, and the Seth fell flat on his back into the foul-smelling mud.

    Sundar gasped loudly in fake horror. "Sethji, you pulled too hard! You have tragically killed your own mare. Now you'll have to give a massive religious feast to the entire village to atone for your terrible sin!"

    "A... feast?" the Seth choked, imagining the horrifying cost of feeding hundreds of people. His incredible greed instantly took over.

    "Sundar, let's keep this strictly to ourselves!" the Seth begged, grabbing Sundar's arm. "Don't breathe a single word of this to anybody in the village. Please!"

    "But, Sethji, you brutally killed the poor beast..." Sundar argued loudly.

    "Shh! Sundar, not so loud!" the Seth shushed him frantically. "I'll personally pay you a hundred rupees right now to keep quiet."

    Sundar frowned. "Only one hundred? A proper feast to wash away your sins would have easily cost you..."

    "All right! All right! Take two hundred!" the Seth cried desperately.

    "It's a deal!" Sundar agreed instantly.

    The two returned home, and the terrified Sethji paid Sundar the money in secret.

    The very next day, the Seth had an important meeting. "Sundar, fetch the important legal documents from my desk. I have to take them to the Zamindar immediately," the Seth ordered.

    "Yes, sir," Sundar obeyed. He went into the study, found a pair of dirty old chappals (slippers), carefully wrapped them in a neat piece of cloth, and gave the official-looking parcel to his master.

    At the Zamindar's house, the Seth bowed deeply. "I have brought the very important papers, sir," he said, dramatically opening the cloth parcel before the Zamindar and all his noble guests.

    "HA! HA! HA!" the Zamindar and his guests roared with laughter at the sight of the dirty chappals.

    The Seth returned home, fuming with sheer rage. "You... you...!" he screamed at Sundar.

    "Yes, Sethji?" Sundar asked innocently.

    "You rogue! How dare you publicly insult me!" the Seth roared.

    "I didn't do a thing Sethji..." Sundar shrugged casually. "...Yet you can legally sack me right now if you like!"

    The Seth opened his mouth to fire him—"I will!"—but suddenly remembered the cruel condition. If he sacked Sundar, Sundar got to cut off his nose!

    "You're... you're...!" the Seth sputtered, his face turning red. "Never mind!"

    A few days later, when Sethji had to go visit his father-in-law's house in a neighbouring village, Sundar followed him quietly. Taking a shorter route, Sundar reached the house well ahead of his master.

    "Namaskar, sir!" Sundar greeted the father-in-law. "I am a new servant of your son-in-law. Sethji is on his way here right now."

    "Welcome, my boy," the man smiled.

    "Sethani (the Seth's wife) has urgently sent me to tell you that only raw green chillies and black salt should be given to him as food during his stay," Sundar lied smoothly.

    The father-in-law looked shocked. "Green chillies and salt?"

    "A strictly special diet, sir. He is not well at all, you know," Sundar sighed sadly.

    "What is wrong with him?" the mother-in-law asked, rushing out with deep concern.

    Sundar tapped his head. "He is... tragically suffering... from brain-fever... he behaves very strangely and aggressively at times."

    "Oh, my God!" the mother-in-law gasped in horror.

    Sethji arrived a little later, tired and extremely hungry from his journey. At lunch time, his mother-in-law carefully placed a small plate before him.

    The other two nodded in agreement.

    "So," the third friend continued, "I'm going to respectfully keep a quarter of my earnings for my family, and I will gladly give all the rest to the local temple as an offering."

    "That is very generous of you," the second friend praised him. "And you?" the third friend asked him back.

    "I'm going to keep half of my money," the second friend declared proudly, "and I will give the entire other half to the temple to show my immense gratitude!"

    The two generous friends then turned their eyes expectantly to the first friend, who was notoriously the biggest, greediest miser in their entire village.

    "What about you, brother?" they asked. "How much will you give to God?"

    The first friend looked incredibly uncomfortable. He clutched his heavy purse tightly. "I... er... uh..." he stammered, frantically trying to think of an excuse to keep all his money without sounding like a terrible person.

    Suddenly, his eyes lit up with a miserly, brilliant idea.

    "I have the fairest plan of all!" the first friend declared loudly. "I'll tie up absolutely all my money in a piece of cloth and throw it high up into the air..."

    His friends looked at him in total confusion.

    "...And God will reach down from heaven and simply take exactly as much as he wants!" the miserly friend grinned triumphantly. "...And whatever falls back down to the earth... will naturally be mine!"

    Tales of Humour 2 - 29. Ramu and the Rickshaw

    "Here you are, sir..." Ramu wheezed, dropping the heavy bags on the floor. "... (gasp)... it all cost... (gasp)... exactly 95 rupees!" He proudly handed over the 5 rupees change.

    The master glared at the exhausted boy. "You absolute fool! You had 5 rupees left over! Why didn't you just take a rickshaw home with the change instead of breaking your back carrying all this?"

    Ramu stared at him blankly. "?"

    The next day, the master called for him again.

    "Ramu! I urgently need a simple matchbox," the master ordered. "Here's 25 paise. Go down to the corner shop."

    "I'll get it at once, sir," Ramu said eagerly, taking the tiny coin.

    T-TT-TT-TTT-TT!

    "What on earth is that noise I hear?" the master frowned, going to the window.

    He looked out and his jaw dropped in absolute shock. "!!!"

    An expensive autorickshaw was parked right outside his house. Ramu proudly stepped out, holding a tiny, single matchbox.

    Ramu grinned broadly at his shocked master. "You certainly won't be able to complain today, sir! I followed your strict instructions!" he yelled over the engine noise. "I took a rickshaw! Now, please come out here and pay off the driver!"
