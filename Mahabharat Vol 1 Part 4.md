# The Pandavas at Ekachakra

After escaping the burning mansion of Varanavata, the Pandavas and their mother, Kunti, journeyed through dense forests until they arrived at the peaceful town of Ekachakra. Guided by Sage Vyasa, they adopted the guise of humble Brahmana mendicants and took up residence in the modest home of a local Brahmana family. 

For some time, they lived quietly, sustaining themselves on the alms they collected from the townsfolk. Every evening, they would pool their collected food and divide it into two halves—one half for the perpetually ravenous Bheema, and the other half shared among the rest of the family.

One day, while the four brothers were out seeking alms, Bheema stayed behind with Kunti. Suddenly, the quiet afternoon was pierced by the sound of agonizing wails coming from the inner chambers of their host's house. 

Deeply concerned, Kunti approached the Brahmana and his family. "You have provided us safe shelter," she said gently. "I wish to know the cause of your woe to decide if it can be removed."

The weeping Brahmana explained their dire situation. "A mighty, cannibalistic Rakshasa named Baka rules over the nearby forest," he sighed heavily. "He protects our town from enemies, but exacts a terrible tribute. Every week, a different household must send him a cartload of rice, two buffaloes, and the man who drives the cart. Today, it is my family's turn. I cannot abandon my wife or my innocent children, so we must all perish together."

Kunti smiled reassuringly. "Do not fear for me or my sons," she told the astonished Brahmana. "I have five sons. Let one of them go to the Rakshasa with the quittance."
The Brahmana strongly protested, refusing to send a guest to his death, but Kunti insisted. She knew Bheema's immense strength and was confident he could easily slay the demon. 

When Bheema was informed of the plan, he readily consented. He loaded the cart with the massive feast of rice and drove the two buffaloes into the dark forest. 

Upon reaching the clearing where the demon resided, Bheema decided to thoroughly infuriate the monster. Instead of presenting the food, he calmly sat down on the cart and began to eat the enormous feast himself! 

Smelling the food, the monstrous Baka emerged from the trees. Seeing a mere human devouring his meal, the cannibal let out a terrifying roar. "Who is this villain eating up my meal?" he bellowed, rushing forward with upraised arms.

Bheema completely ignored him. Without so much as a glance, he continued eating the rice cheerfully. Blinded by rage, the Rakshasa brought his massive fists down on Bheema's back. Bheema barely flinched and kept eating. 

Furious, Baka uprooted a massive tree and hurled it at Bheema. Having finally finished his meal, Bheema wiped his hands, caught the tree effortlessly in his left hand, and hurled it right back. 

A brutal fight erupted. The two giants grappled and struggled, tearing apart the surrounding forest as they fought. But the mighty Bheema quickly gained the upper hand. He pressed the demon down with his knees, seized his neck, and bent him double with terrifying force. Baka expelled a horrifying roar of pain and fell dead. 

Terrified by the roar, Baka's monstrous kinsmen emerged from the woods. But when they saw their invincible leader dead on the ground, they immediately surrendered to Bheema, promising never to prey on human flesh again. 

Under the cover of darkness, Bheema carried the lifeless cannibal to the town gates and deposited him there unobserved. The next morning, the citizens of Ekachakra awoke to find their oppressor dead, glutted in gore. Thousands thronged the spot, marveling at the superhuman deed.

When the townsfolk rushed to the Brahmana's house to inquire about his survival, the wise host kept the Pandavas' secret. "A noble Brahmana, a master of occult lore, came to me and offered to deliver the food," he told the crowd. "He must have slain the brute."

The town celebrated their newfound freedom with a grand festival. However, recognizing that the extraordinary deed would soon draw unwanted attention and potentially reveal their true identities to Duryodhana's spies, Yudhishthira gathered his family. 
"We have lived here comfortably," he told Kunti and his brothers, "but it is time for us to move on before we are discovered."

Leaving Ekachakra behind, the Pandavas continued their journey in disguise, silently awaiting the right moment to reclaim their destiny.


# Enter Draupadi

While living in disguise as poor Brahmanas in Ekachakra, the Pandavas welcomed a traveling ascetic into their home. The guest entertained them with a fascinating tale from the kingdom of Panchala. 

King Drupada of Panchala, deeply humiliated by his defeat at the hands of Drona and the Kuru princes, had wandered the land in search of powerful Brahmanas who could help him perform a massive sacrifice. His singular goal was to obtain an invincible son who could slay Drona, and a beautiful daughter to marry Arjuna, whom he secretly admired.

Aided by two brilliant sages, Yaja and Upayaja, Drupada performed a grand *Yagna*. From the roaring sacrificial fire, a fully grown, armor-clad warrior emerged—Dhrishtadyumna. Immediately following him arose a breathtakingly beautiful maiden with dark, curly hair and a complexion like a blue lotus. She was named Krishnaa, but became famous as Draupadi. 
"This girl, dark of skin and the best of women, will be the cause of the fall of the Kshatriyas," a celestial voice prophesied as she emerged from the flames. 

Hearing this wondrous tale, the Pandavas grew restless. With Kunti's permission, they decided to leave Ekachakra and travel toward Panchala, hoping to witness the grand *Swayamvara* (husband-choosing ceremony) that Drupada was organizing for Draupadi. 

Their journey took them through a dense forest along the banks of the sacred river Ganga. Led by Arjuna carrying a blazing torch, the brothers walked through the night. As they approached the riverbank at twilight, a furious voice boomed from the water. 

"Keep your distance! Foolish men who venture out during these hours are slain by us," shouted Angaraparna, the haughty King of the Gandharvas, who was sporting in the river with his wives. "Here, leave alone men, even the divines, the Rakshasas and sorcerers fear to tread. I am Angaraparna, and my strength is my faith!"

Arjuna stepped forward fearlessly. "O rover of the skies, the right to approach Ganga is not barred by time," he retorted. "No one can have any exclusive right to the oceans, the Himalayan ranges, or to Ganga—be it night or day or twilight."

Enraged by the mortal's defiance, the Gandharva drew his bow and unleashed a volley of deadly arrows. Arjuna smoothly deflected them with his torch. 
"I know the Gandharvas to be superhuman," Arjuna declared, drawing his own bow. "So I shall fight you with celestial missiles!" 

Invoking the fiery *Agneya* missile given to him by Drona, Arjuna launched it at the Gandharva. The blinding weapon struck the Gandharva's chariot, burning it to ashes and knocking Angaraparna unconscious. Arjuna dragged the defeated king to Yudhishthira by his hair.

Angaraparna's wife, Kumbheenasi, ran to Yudhishthira, tearfully pleading for her husband's life. Touched by her devotion, Yudhishthira ordered Arjuna to release him. "Release him. We would not slay a foe defeated, humiliated, and in need of the protection of a woman."

Deeply humbled, the Gandharva king befriended the Pandavas. Recognizing their immense strength, he offered Arjuna his most prized possessions: the *Chakshushi* lore, a magical science that enabled the user to perceive anything across the three worlds, and a herd of magnificent Gandharva horses that could change color and speed at will. 

In return, Arjuna taught the Gandharva the secrets of the *Agneya* missile. Before parting ways, Angaraparna advised the brothers that no king should travel without a royal priest to guide and protect him. 

Taking his advice, the Pandavas traveled to a nearby ashrama and humbly requested the wise sage Dhoumya to become their royal priest. Recognizing their true noble nature and hidden royal lineage, Dhoumya gladly accepted. 

Guided and protected by their new priest, the Pandavas finally arrived in the bustling, magnificently decorated capital city of Panchala. Still maintaining their disguise, the mighty princes took up residence in the humble house of a potter, living quietly as mendicants, eagerly awaiting the grand spectacle of Draupadi's Swayamvara.


# Draupadi's Swayamvara

King Drupada of Panchala had secretly harbored a lifelong desire to marry his exceptionally beautiful daughter, Draupadi, to Arjuna, the greatest archer of the age. To ensure only a warrior of Arjuna's caliber could win her hand, Drupada devised an impossibly difficult test for her *Swayamvara*.

He constructed a massive, incredibly stiff bow that no ordinary man could string. High above the arena, he suspended a target, and right below it, he placed a rapidly revolving mechanism with a narrow gap. The successful suitor had to string the heavy bow and shoot five arrows simultaneously through the spinning mechanism to hit the target.

Kings and princes from far and wide—including Jarasandha, Shalya, and the Kauravas led by Duryodhana—flocked to the dazzling arena. The Pandavas, still disguised as simple Brahmanas, quietly slipped into the stadium and took their seats among the priests.

When the contest began, Dhrishtadyumna announced the rules. One by one, the greatest Kshatriya kings stepped forward. And one by one, they failed miserably. Some couldn't even lift the bow; others were thrown to the ground violently when the stiff bow snapped back as they tried to string it. 

As the kings grew disheartened, the magnificent Karna strode into the arena. With effortless grace, he picked up the bow, strung it easily, and prepared to fire. 

Seeing him, Draupadi immediately stood up. "I will not wed a Suta (charioteer's son)!" she declared loudly. 
Insulted and enraged, Karna threw down the bow with a bitter laugh and walked away.

When all the Kshatriya kings had failed, Arjuna—dressed in the simple white robes of a Brahmana—stood up from the priest's enclosure and walked toward the bow. The Brahmanas murmured in doubt, fearing that a priest attempting a warrior's task would bring them ridicule. But as Arjuna effortlessly lifted the heavy bow, a hushed awe fell over the stadium.

With fluid, lightning-fast motions, Arjuna strung the bow and fired five arrows straight through the revolving mechanism, striking the target perfectly and bringing it crashing to the ground. The arena erupted in cheers, and celestial flowers rained down from the heavens. Smiling, Draupadi stepped forward and garlanded the triumphant "Brahmana."

However, the defeated Kshatriya kings were incensed. "This is an insult to our warrior caste!" they roared. "How can a mere Brahmana win a princess?" Led by Karna and Shalya, the furious kings drew their weapons and charged at King Drupada to slay him.

Arjuna and Bheema instantly leaped to Drupada's defense. Bheema simply uprooted a massive tree and wielded it like a giant mace, standing beside his brother like the god of death himself. A fierce battle broke out. Arjuna's arrows effortlessly stopped the charging kings, matching even the great Karna shot for shot until Karna, realizing he was fighting an equal, withdrew in respect. Bheema engaged King Shalya in a brutal wrestling match, finally lifting the powerful king high into the air and tossing him to the ground.

Seeing the terrifying prowess of these two "Brahmanas," the kings admitted defeat and withdrew, leaving Arjuna and Bheema to proudly lead Draupadi away from the arena.

Returning to their humble potter's hut at dusk, Arjuna cheerfully called out to his mother from outside. "Mother, look what we have brought today!"
Without looking up from her prayers, Kunti automatically replied, "Enjoy it, all of you together."

When she turned around and saw the beautiful princess, Kunti gasped in horror. "What have I said? My words cannot be taken back, but how can one woman marry five men?"

Yudhishthira, knowing his mother's word was absolute law, made a solemn decision. "Draupadi shall be the wife of us all."

Meanwhile, Dhrishtadyumna had secretly followed the Brahmanas to the potter's hut. Eavesdropping from the shadows, he realized with joy that these "priests" were actually the mighty Pandava princes, whom everyone believed had perished in the Varanavata fire! He rushed back to the palace and excitedly informed his father. 

Overjoyed, King Drupada invited the Pandavas to his palace the next day. When Yudhishthira formally revealed their identities, Drupada was thrilled, knowing his dream of marrying his daughter to Arjuna had come true. 

But when Yudhishthira informed him that Draupadi would marry all five brothers to honor their mother's accidental command, Drupada was deeply shocked. "This is against all laws of society and morality!" he protested. 

Just then, the great Sage Veda Vyasa arrived. He took King Drupada aside and explained a divine secret. "In her previous life, Draupadi was a devout maiden who performed severe penance to obtain a husband," Vyasa revealed. "When Lord Shiva appeared, she excitedly asked for a husband five times! Shiva granted her wish, saying she would have five husbands in her next life. This is divine destiny."

Satisfied by the sage's explanation, King Drupada happily gave his blessing. In a grand, joyous ceremony, Draupadi was married to all five Pandava brothers, uniting the immensely powerful Kuru princes with the formidable kingdom of Panchala.
