    Bhagawat: The Krishna Avatar - The Darling of Gokul

    Pareekshit, the son of Abhimanyu, was listening with rapt attention to the story of his forefathers narrated by the great sage Shukadeva. "You have told me about the kings of the solar and lunar dynasty," Pareekshit said. "Now tell me, in detail, the story of Lord Krishna, who saved me from the vengeful wrath of Ashwatthama, and with whose grace my forefathers won the Mahabharata war." "Listen, O Pareekshit," Shukadeva began. "The story of Krishna is such that the enquirer, the narrator, and the listener are all completely purified by it."

    "Long, long ago, in the days of yore, there was a king named Shoorasena of the Yadava race, who ruled over the kingdom of Mathura. Vasudeva was his son. In due time, Vasudeva was married to Devaki, with great pomp and celebration. The newlyweds were about to leave in their chariot, when Devaki's powerful cousin, Kamsa, held the reins in his hand and insisted on driving the chariot himself to show his affection.

    Following them, at a little distance, was a massive procession: four hundred caparisoned elephants, fifteen thousand horses, numerous chariots carrying the gifts bestowed on the bridal pair by Devaki's fond father, and two hundred beautifully bedecked maids.

    Suddenly, even as the notes of auspicious music reached a crescendo, a terrible voice rang out from the heavens. "Beware, you fool! The eighth-born of this very woman will slay you!" The cruel Kamsa, ablaze with anger and fright, instantly drew his sword. "I will kill her right away!"

    Vasudeva desperately intervened. "Stop Kamsa! You are the glory of your race. Mighty warriors relate stories of your valour. It does not become you to kill Devaki, a woman, your own cousin and that too on her wedding day. After all, whoever is born is destined to die one day. Spare your innocent little cousin."

    Vasudeva tried his absolute best to dissuade Kamsa with praise and reasoning, but in vain. I must save poor Devaki's life now, Vasudeva thought frantically. I will promise to give my sons to him. Who knows, perhaps my son may really kill him. Perhaps Kamsa himself may be dead soon, before any son is even born.

    Aloud he said, "You have no reason to fear this damsel. According to the celestial voice, it is her sons who pose a threat to you. I promise I will bring every child to you as soon as it is born." Kamsa knew he could trust Vasudeva's words. "I believe you, and spare Devaki's life. But be sure to keep your promise."

    Vasudeva and Devaki returned home under a dark cloud. Months rolled by, and one tragic day, Vasudeva arrived at Kamsa's palace. "Kamsa. Here is my first-born, Kirtiman. As I promised, I have brought him to you." Kamsa looked at the infant. "Oh! Take this little one away. It is your eighth child who is supposed to kill me. Let him live." "As you say," Vasudeva bowed, but he was not fully convinced. "Whatever he may say, I cannot trust Kamsa. He may change his mind any time."

    Meanwhile, the celestial sage Narada visited Kamsa to warn him. "Kamsa. Take care. Devaki, Vasudeva, Nanda, in fact, all the inhabitants of Braja are incarnations of the devas (gods). They have descended to earth to rid it of cruel oppressors like you." Kamsa panicked. "I had better be wary of all the sons of Devaki."

    He immediately ordered his guards, "Capture Vasudeva and Devaki. Throw them into prison, tightly bound in iron chains." Kamsa now began to reveal himself in his true colours. He put his own father Ugrasena in prison, and violently took over the kingdom. Among his closest friends were wicked Asuras (demons) like Pralamba, Baka, Chanura, and Trinavarta.

    Kamsa proved to be a strong but terribly cruel king. He had the backing of Jarasandha, the powerful emperor who ruled from Magadha. Because of his oppressive rule, his subjects began to flee the kingdom to take shelter in other parts of the country.

    Meanwhile, one by one, all the sons born to Devaki in the dark prison were ruthlessly put to death by Kamsa. When her seventh child was due, Devaki's brief happiness was again clouded over by apprehensions. "Oh! How cruel is the fate that awaits our child. Kamsa will surely kill it at birth."

    Shukadeva paused to explain to Pareekshit: "Although she was not aware of it, Devaki's seventh child was none other than Shesha, the divine serpent on whom Lord Vishnu reclines. Lord Vishnu instructed Yogamaya (his power of illusion), to magically transfer the unborn baby from Devaki to Rohini, Vasudeva's second wife who lived safely in Gokul. Later, on the very day that Lord Vishnu would be born as the eighth son of Devaki, Yogamaya was to take birth as Yashoda's daughter in Gokul."

    On divine command, Yogamaya caused the unborn baby to be transferred from Devaki's womb to that of Rohini's. As a result, Rohini safely found herself with child just when Devaki seemingly lost her baby in prison. In due time, Rohini gave birth to a strong son.

    Before long, Devaki found herself expecting her deeply feared eighth child. Her radiant beauty shone through the dark bars of the jail like that of a brilliant lamp concealed in an earthen pot. Even Kamsa noticed the strange, terrifying glow on Devaki's face. "Devaki never looked like this before. Surely it is the presence of Vishnu himself inside her that makes her so radiant. But what can I do? If I kill her now, I will never be able to live down the terrible stigma of killing a woman, my own cousin, and that too when she is with child." Impatiently and fearfully, Kamsa waited for the birth of Devaki's eighth child. "Order the guards to let me know the exact moment the baby is born," he commanded.

    At last, the auspicious day dawned. The entire universe seemed to celebrate the event. Vasudeva was filled with pure wonder on beholding the new-born baby. It miraculously held aloft a mace, a disc, a conchshell, and a lotus in its four tiny hands. His beautiful dusky complexion contrasted vividly with the yellow silk enveloping him.

    With folded hands, Vasudeva fell on his knees and began to pray to his divine son. "Lord! You have taken birth in our house in order to protect the earth. But the cruel Kamsa will hasten to destroy you as soon as he hears the news." Devaki too was filled with awe at the majestic sight of her baby. "I know, Lord, that you will be our saviour. But do not let Kamsa see you in this divine form, I beseech you!"

    In the twinkling of an eye, the baby assumed the form of an ordinary, beautiful new-born boy. "Kamsa must not set eyes on this son of ours. I must take him away. But how?" Vasudeva agonised.

    No sooner had he thought to himself thus, than a miracle occurred. "Oh! My heavy chains and fetters have fallen off!" Vasudeva gasped. "Devaki, give the child to me. Quick!" Tenderly holding the baby in his arms, Vasudeva cautiously stepped out of the room. Miraculously, he found the massive iron prison doors wide open and the guards all blissfully asleep. Undetected, he walked out into the night with the baby.

    It was a dark, stormy night. The river Yamuna was dangerously in spate. "I would like to go to Gokul across the river, to my dear friend Nanda," Vasudeva thought. "But who will take me across these stormy waters?" And, lo and behold, the raging river waters parted smoothly, making a safe path for him.

    On reaching Gokul, he found all the people deep in supernatural slumber. On reaching Nanda's house, he crept inside, placed his baby boy by the side of Yashoda, Nanda's wife, and quietly picked up her new-born daughter. It was only after Vasudeva had gone away that Yashoda became aware of the child next to her. She, like the rest of the people of Gokul, had been under a deep trance since her baby was born.

    Meanwhile, Vasudeva safely returned to the dark prison in Mathura. He placed the baby girl by the side of Devaki. "I'll put the fetters back on my feet. The doors are locked as before. No one will guess I have been away."

    At dawn, the lusty cries of the child finally woke up the guards. "Did you hear that? The child is born! Go at once and inform the king." Kamsa was hastily woken up. He rushed to the prison, drawing his sword. "The eighth-born! My would-be slayer!"

    When Kamsa arrived there, Devaki implored him through tears. "My dear brother, please spare this child. Surely you would not cruelly kill a female? Allow me to keep this last child of mine." But Kamsa was completely unmoved by her pleas. He seized the baby by her tiny legs and viciously dashed her against a stone surface.

    But this was no ordinary baby. The divine infant slipped from his grasp, rose towards the sky, and manifested herself as a magnificent, eight-armed goddess. Addressing the terrified Kamsa, she mocked, "You fool! The one who is destined to destroy you still lives securely! Do not kill other innocent infants." Then she disappeared into the heavens.

    Kamsa was absolutely wonderstruck on hearing this proclamation. In a daze of fear and guilt, he released Devaki and Vasudeva. "My dear sister, what a great sinner I have been to kill my nephews. I really regret my actions."

    But his repentance was incredibly short-lived. The next morning, he called for his evil ministers and cronies to relate the terrifying events of the night. "...so I let Vasudeva and Devaki go, since the divine being specifically said that the one who is destined to kill me is born elsewhere." His wicked Asura friends advised him, "O king, if that is so, we will search everywhere in the kingdom and kill all infants who are ten days old or less. That will solve the problem." Kamsa agreed to the brutal plan. Addressing a terrifying rakshasi called Pootana, he said, "Pootana, go to every village and city, and kill all the new-born babies."

    Meanwhile, at Gokul, Nanda was overjoyed at the birth of a son to him and celebrated it with due, joyous ceremony. The people of Gokul were eager to see the son of their chief. Bedecked in their best garments, the Gopas and Gopikas (cowherds and milkmaids) made their way to Nanda's palace. They crowded around to have a look at the beautiful baby. "God bless this boy." "May he live long!" "What a beautiful face he has!" The Gopas celebrated the event with gay abandon, playfully sprinkling one another with milk and curds, and smearing soft butter on one another's faces. Nanda gave generous gifts to all his visitors.

    After a few days, leaving Gokul in the hands of other capable cowherds, Nanda went to Mathura to pay his annual taxes to Kamsa. When Vasudeva heard of his safe arrival, he hastened to meet him. After embracing him affectionately, Vasudeva said, "How fortunate you two are to get a son after all these years." "It is a pity that we seldom meet," Nanda smiled. "You and your wife look after Rohini and my son. I hope he is well and looks upon you as his parents." "So many of your precious sons were killed by Kamsa. Even your little daughter did not survive. Verily, our life is governed by destiny," Nanda sympathised.

    Vasudeva, knowing the danger, subtly warned him, "Now that you have paid your annual dues and met me too, you must not tarry here any longer. Many calamities are likely to descend on Gokula." On Vasudeva's earnest advice, Nanda hastily made his way home. Why did Vasudeva say such a thing, I wonder? he thought anxiously. But his words can never be false. May God protect us.

    While Nanda was away on the road, the wicked Pootana reached Gokul magically disguised in the form of a beautiful, innocent damsel. She easily found her way into Nanda's home and beheld the softly sleeping Krishna. She lifted him up in her arms. Both Rohini and Yashoda were surprised to see this complete stranger, but were so utterly carried away by her dazzling beauty that they did not ask any questions.

    Pootana took the baby in her lap and offered her breast to suckle him. Ah! The deadly poison that I have heavily smeared on my breasts will soon take effect! she gloated inwardly. But little did she know that it was her own life that was in terrible danger. For the divine infant, Krishna, soon began to suck the very life out of her along with the milk.

    "Aieee!! Let go! Leave me!" she shrieked in absolute agony. She shrieked and shrieked. Unable to bear the excruciating pain, her disguise fell away. She assumed her true, unbelievably ugly rakshasi form, and finally collapsed dead in a massive heap, shaking the ground. But still, Krishna would not let go of her. The petrified gopikas hastily pulled him away from the monstrous corpse and took him to the trembling Yashoda. Yashoda quickly anointed and bathed Krishna with holy water and rocked him to sleep.

    When Nanda and his companions finally returned to Gokul, they were shocked to see Pootana's hideous, supine form blocking the courtyard. "What Vasudeva prophesied has come true. How amazing!" Nanda gasped. He was all the more astonished when he heard that it was his tiny Krishna who had tormented and killed the massive Pootana. He picked up his little son and kissed him on his forehead again and again in immense relief.

    Nanda and Yashoda watched their son grow with complete delight. When he learnt to turn on his side, the milestone event was celebrated with loud song and feasting. All the people of Gokul had happily gathered at Nanda's house. Seeing that the baby was a little tired after all the fuss, Yashoda fed him and made him sleep. Placing his cot safely in the shade of a heavy, unyoked cart in the courtyard, she went back inside to her guests.

    After a brief nap, Krishna woke up and began to cry for his mother. But Yashoda was too far away and too busy to hear him. Louder and louder he cried, and angrily kicked up his baby feet in the air. But the touch of his tiny, divine feet was enough.

    "CRASH!" The noise of the heavy wooden cart overturning and vessels breaking brought the guests rushing to the spot. "See! That incredibly heavy cart has overturned on its own!" they gasped. "All that food stored inside is completely wasted." "Maybe the cart was just dangerously overloaded."

    The small boys who were playing nearby piped up, "It was the little one! He kicked the cart and it flew over!" "We saw it! We saw it!" But no one believed them. "Is it possible for a tiny baby to topple such a heavy cart?" "Don't listen to them. Little boys are always imagining things." Yashoda had meanwhile grabbed the baby away and soothed him. Slowly, the confused people went back to their feasting and the bizarre matter was forgotten for a while.

    A few days later, Yashoda was sitting comfortably with Krishna in her lap. "Why! He suddenly feels incredibly heavy. I can hardly bear his weight," she strained. She placed him down on the ground and was soon happily engrossed in household work. Krishna was happily playing and gurgling on his own in the courtyard.

    Suddenly, the gentle breeze turned into such a terrible, roaring whirlwind that it swooped the baby entirely off the ground and up into the sky. It was Trinavarta, another evil asura friend of Kamsa, who had appeared in the devastating guise of a whirlwind to completely destroy Krishna.

    The entire village of Gokul was filled by choking dust and pitch darkness. No one could see anything. Yashoda came running out in a panic. "Oh! Where is my darling baby? I had left him right here just now!" When the terrifying whirlwind had finally subsided, the women gathered around her. "What happened, Yashoda?" "My son! He has just disappeared into the sky!" she wailed.

    Meanwhile, as the wicked Trinavarta carried the baby sky-wards, he found the infant becoming heavier and heavier, like a mountain of solid iron. Krishna's crushing weight compelled the struggling demon to slow down. Now, little Krishna caught him tightly by the throat and choked him. "AHHH!" The demon suffocated and died mid-air. Down fell the massive demon to his death, crashing into the rocks outside the village.

    The people of Gokul watched the unbelievable scene with open-eyed amazement. Once again, Krishna was miraculously rescued and restored to his desperately anxious mother. "Thank God you are safe," they wept. "How right Vasudeva was!" Nanda marvelled. "Truly strange things are happening here."

    One day, Garga, the highly respected family priest of the Yadavas, visited Gokul at Vasudeva's secret bidding. After welcoming him with due, deep respect, Nanda said, "We are greatly honoured by your visit, Gargacharya. May I humbly request you to conduct the official naming ceremony of our two boys?"

    Garga explained cautiously, "I am the royal priest of the Yadavas. If the news of my performing the ceremony of your boys reaches Kamsa, he will be instantly suspicious. Ever since he heard that his slayer has been born elsewhere, Kamsa has been constantly on the lookout for him. He would surely have your son killed just to be safe." Nanda understood perfectly. "In that case, we will do everything completely on the quiet. Not even my near ones will know of it."

    So, in absolute secrecy, without any fanfare or guests, the naming ceremony of the two boys was performed quietly in a cowshed. "This son of Rohini," Garga predicted, "will be a charming boy, pleasing people with his exceptionally good qualities. Hence he should be called Rama. Because of his immense prowess and strength, he will be called Balarama." He then turned to the other infant. "Your other dusky complexioned son will be officially called Krishna, because of his dark colour. He will bring immense pleasure and prosperity to you. Take very good care of him."

    In a few short weeks, Balarama and Krishna learnt to crawl. Now there was absolutely no stopping the two energetic boys. They would wander all over Gokul, dragging their little feet adorned with tinkling anklets through the muddy cow pastures. Their childish pranks were a constant source of sheer amusement to their fond mothers. "Yashoda! Come and see your Krishna." "What is he up to now?" "See! He's being excitedly dragged away by a frightened calf, hanging onto its tail!" "Just look at his face! He's absolutely enjoying it!"

    Rohini and Yashoda could never be completely sure what the exceptionally naughty pair would do next. "Yashoda dear! The house is far too quiet. Those two must be up to some terrible mischief! Will you go and look?" "Yes, I will! I hope they haven't caught hold of a stray dog in the street as they did the other day." "Or tried to pick up a sharp sword like yesterday!"

    Soon Yashoda was back, exhausted, dragging the two giggling toddlers with her. "You were completely right, Rohini! I caught them just in the nick of time, trying to peep into the deep well on tiptoe." "What will they ever think of next?"

    Soon Krishna and Balarama learnt to walk and run properly. "Krishna! Balarama! I should keep them firmly tied up, I think! Where could they be?" Yashoda sighed. She knew soon enough. Every single day there would be loud, fresh complaints from the neighbours. "Yashoda, you must completely control that naughty son of yours." "What happened now?" "He ate up all the freshly churned butter in my house!" "And he broke the expensive pot also!" "He is all over our house. When we scold him, he just giggles and runs away." "He brazenly steals the butter and then gives it away to his monkey friends!"

    Everyday, the incredibly naughty Krishna would think of entirely new, ingenious ways to steal butter which would be carefully kept out of his reach. He would climb on his friends' shoulders or use sticks to reach high pots hung from the ceiling. Yashoda would make up her mind to severely scold him. "Just let him come home today!" she would fume. But one single look at his wide-eyed, completely innocent face would instantly melt her heart.

    One day, Krishna and Balarama were playing in the fields with their friends. Soon, Balarama came running home. "Ma Yashoda! Krishna has eaten earth!" "Yes! Yes! We saw him scoop it up," his friends tattled. "You naughty boy! Why did you eat earth?" Yashoda scolded. "They are telling lies. I did not eat any," Krishna said innocently. "Let me see. Open your mouth wide," she demanded.

    Krishna opened his tiny mouth. Yashoda looked inside and was instantly gripped by absolute awe. For in Krishna's mouth, she could not see dirt, but rather the limitless sky, the earth, the stars, and in fact, the entire, boundless universe spinning within. "Surely I must be deeply dreaming," she gasped, stepping back. "Is it my own ego that makes me think like this? Help me, God!" But soon, due to divine illusion, she miraculously forgot the whole incident and simply wiped his mouth clean.

    Another day, Yashoda thought, "All the maids are incredibly busy, but I must have fresh, sweet butter made for my Krishna. Hmm, I will churn it myself today." She was busy singing melodiously and working hard at the churn. Krishna heard his mother singing and happily climbed onto her lap demanding attention. Yashoda was lovingly engrossed in the childish prattle of Krishna when suddenly she smelled burning milk. "Oh! The milk on the stove must have boiled over!" she gasped, dropping him to run to the kitchen.

    Krishna did not like being dropped at all. He angrily grabbed a stone and broke the heavy churning pot, spilling buttermilk everywhere. With tears of anger in his eyes and a large container of fresh butter in his hand, he marched off to a safe, quiet corner to eat the stolen butter in peace.

    When Yashoda came back to the mess - "The pot is deliberately broken! It must be Krishna. Just let me catch hold of him." By now Krishna's hunger and anger had both completely gone. She found him standing on an upturned mortar in the courtyard, happily and generously sharing the stolen butter with a large troop of monkeys. As soon as he saw Yashoda, Krishna ran away with his friends. "Run! Run! Ma has a big stick in her hand."

    Yashoda was quite out of breath by the time she finally managed to catch hold of the swift Krishna. "Today, I must properly punish you. You are becoming naughtier every single day!" She dragged him crying back home. "I will firmly bind you up to the same heavy mortar you were standing on." "Ahh! Here is some thick rope! Now at least I will know exactly where you are," she said. But when she tried to tie it, the rope was too short. "Why! This rope is short by just two fingers. Rohini! Get some more rope!" she called. But no matter how much rope they added, it was always too short by exactly two fingers. "Oh dear! Again this is a little short for tying a knot," Yashoda panted in frustration.

    There was a distinct look of mischief on Krishna's tear-stained face, as if he was deeply amused at her finding the rope short. But at last, taking pity on his exhausted mother, he allowed himself to be tied up firmly. "Now you will stay right here till I decide to free you," Yashoda scolded, going back inside.

    But she was mistaken. As soon as her back was turned, Krishna thought, "I'll find someone to untie me." Slowly and powerfully dragging the immensely heavy wooden mortar with him, he trudged on towards the bank of the river.

    On his way were two massive, ancient Yamalarjuna twin trees. Krishna playfully managed to go in between them, but the heavy mortar trailing behind him got stuck horizontally between the trunks. When Krishna casually tugged at the rope to pull himself free, both the giant trees were completely uprooted from the earth and came crashing to the ground with a thunderous roar. "CRASH"

    Shukadeva paused here during his long narration to explain to Pareekshit the deep divinity of Krishna's actions. "Out of the two fallen trees sprang two brilliantly shining beings. They folded their hands in deep reverence before the toddler Krishna. They were in fact, Nalakubera and Manigreeva, arrogant demigods who had been under a terrible curse and were now finally released because of this simple act of Krishna."

    The impossibly loud crash brought all the people of Gokul running to the scene. "What was that terrifying noise?" "Maybe lightning has struck!" "But there is absolutely no thunder!" When they reached the spot, they were stunned. "How did these incredibly huge trees simply fall over?" "And what is tiny Krishna doing here, tied to a mortar right between them?" "Odd, impossible things are always happening when this boy is around."

    The small children ran up. "It was Krishna who made the massive trees fall!" they yelled. "We saw him do so! He just pulled the rope! And two brilliantly shining figures came out of the broken trees." "Don't listen to their childish, impossible talk! Such wild imagination!" the elders dismissed them. Soon Vasudeva too came there in a panic and quickly untied the rope binding Krishna to the mortar.

    But such punishments did not deter little Krishna from his deeply mischievous ways. He would be always out of the house, joyously singing and dancing to amuse the loving gopikas. "All my heavy housework is still to be done but this boy is such absolute fun to watch!" they would laugh, entirely forgetting their chores. Hiding people's clothes or tools was another favourite prank of his. But, whatever naughty thing he did, the people of Gokul would never fail to be completely enchanted and forgiving at the sight of his beautiful, divine face.

    One day, a fruit seller passed by. "Fresh fruit!" "I'll give a fistful of grain in exchange for the fruit," little Krishna offered, running inside to get grain. But by the time Krishna ran back out, almost all the grain had fallen through his tiny fingers. "Oh! Where did it all go?" he looked at his empty hands sadly. "Never mind! Here, hold this fruit properly," the kind woman smiled, giving him a large basket anyway.

    As Krishna ran away happily with his tasty booty, the woman looked into her empty basket. "What is this? My basket is miraculously filled to the brim with priceless gems! What an unbelievable miracle!"

    The older he grew, the more time Krishna spent on the river bank, happily frolicking in the mud and water with his many friends. "Krishna! Balarama! Come home quickly. You have not eaten since morning," Yashoda would yell from the bank. "I can hardly recognize you under all that thick mud! Go and have a bath immediately. See how impeccably clean your friends are!"

    While Yashoda was busy trying to control Krishna's endless little pranks, Nanda and his trusted advisers were discussing a much more serious matter. Upananda, a deeply wise and elderly man, said, "Such terrible calamities have steadily begun to happen in our normally quiet Gokul. I feel it is truly time to leave this place entirely for the welfare of our young ones. See that little darling of Nanda, Krishna. What miraculous, terrifying escapes he has had. First that wicked demoness Pootana, then the heavy cart mysteriously falling on him, that horrible, unnatural whirlwind picking him up and dashing him down. And finally those two massive, ancient trees inexplicably falling! Thank God no one was injured in any of these events."

    "We have been incredibly protected by God's mercy so far," another elder agreed. "But before anything else terrible happens, let us all pack up and go away. There is a wonderful, safe place called Vrindavana deeper in the forest. It has incredibly lush grassfields, plenty of fruit trees, and thick foliage. It will be absolutely ideal for our cattle and safe for our families."

    Nanda agreed completely. "We will pack and leave for Vrindavana as soon as we can. Tell absolutely everyone to be prepared to leave today."

    After quickly collecting their massive herds of cattle, the people of Gokul loaded their heavy carts with all their belongings. The women, children, and the aged all rode safely on chariots and carts. With their bow and arrow firmly in hand, the strong young men walked protectively beside them. With a joyful song on their lips, and deep hope in their hearts, the entire population of Gokul set out in search of a safe new home in Vrindavana.
