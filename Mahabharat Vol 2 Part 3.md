# Arjuna in Indraloka

Having acquired celestial weapons from Lord Shiva and the Lokapalas, Arjuna waited on the mountain peaks for the great vehicle of Lord Indra. Soon, a spectacular golden chariot guided by Matali descended through the clouds, illuminating the heavens. 

"O fortunate son of Indra," Matali addressed him, "ascend the vehicle sent for you by your father. He and the celestials are waiting to behold you."

Arjuna purified himself by bathing in the Ganga and offered prayers to his ancestors. Blazing with a radiant aura, he ascended the celestial vehicle. The chariot coursed through the skies, becoming invisible to the mortals on the earth, passing thousands of extraordinary flying chariots and celestial beings. 

Finally, Arjuna arrived at Amaravati, the resplendent city of Indra. He was guided to the palace, where he paid his respects to the great Lord of the Devas. Indra affectionately took Arjuna by the hand and made him sit beside him on his own divine throne. Arjuna spent the next five years in heaven, living in absolute comfort and luxury. 

One day, Indra addressed his son. "O son of Kunti, learn the art of music and dancing from Chitrasena. This music of the celestials does not exist in the world of men, but you will benefit from it."

Under the expert guidance of Chitrasena, Arjuna mastered the celestial arts. The two became great friends, but the thought of his brothers and mother Kunti always troubled Arjuna. 

To honor Arjuna's visit, Indra convened a large assembly. The Gandharvas played their veenas, and the Apsaras, including the incomparably beautiful Urvashi, danced. As Arjuna watched the performance, his eyes lingered on Urvashi's beauty. 

Noticing this, Indra secretly commanded Chitrasena to approach Urvashi. "Through my grace, he has learnt about weapons and other arts," Indra said. "But now he should be conversant with the art of conducting himself in female company. Tell Urvashi to wait upon Arjuna."

Chitrasena delivered the message. Urvashi, pleased and eager, prepared herself and visited Arjuna's mansion that night. However, upon seeing her, Arjuna closed his eyes out of modesty and offered his worship as if to an elder. 

"O foremost of Apsaras," Arjuna said, bowing in reverence. "I command me and I shall wait upon you as a servant."

Urvashi explained that she had come impelled by Kamadeva and the command of his father. But Arjuna was overcome with embarrassment. Shutting his ears, he replied, "O blessed lady, it causes me great unhappiness to hear such words from you. You are worthy of being the wife of someone superior. I respect you like my mother Kunti or Shachi, the Queen of Indra."

"O son of Indra, we Apsaras are appointed to give pleasure to all," Urvashi argued. "Do not elevate me to the status of a superior. The sons and grandsons of Puru's race have accepted our services without incurring any sin."

Arjuna remained firm. "I bow my head and prostrate myself at your feet. For you deserve to be worshipped as my mother, and it is your duty to protect me as your son."

Urvashi trembled with rage at this rejection. Contracting her brows, she cursed him. "Since you have scorned a woman who came to your mansion at the command of your father and of her own free will, I curse you! You shall have to bide your time among women, unrespected and as a dancer. You shall be regarded as one destitute of manliness and disdained as a eunuch!"

Seething with anger, Urvashi returned to her abode. A distressed Arjuna sought out Chitrasena, who in turn informed Indra of the happenings. 

Indra smiled and consoled his son. "My mighty son, you have surpassed even great rishis by the patience and self-control you have shown. The curse that Urvashi has inflicted upon you will actually be to your benefit."

"How?" Arjuna asked. 

"You have to pass the thirteenth year of your exile incognito," Indra explained. "That is the time when the spell cast on you by Urvashi will work. At the end of that year, you will regain your manhood."

Relieved, Arjuna ceased to worry about the curse and spent the rest of his days in heaven happily. 

Sometime later, the great Rishi Lomasha arrived in Indraloka during his wanderings. To his great surprise, he saw Arjuna seated with Lord Indra on his throne. 

*What acts of merit has he performed to occupy the seat that is worshipped by the gods?* the Rishi wondered. 

Perceiving the Rishi's thoughts, Indra clarified, "O Brahmarshi, Arjuna is no mortal, though he has been born among men. He is born of Kunti with my grace and has come here to acquire weapons to cleanse the earth of all evil. Arjuna and Krishna are none other than Nara and Narayana, who have been born on earth to ensure the prevalence of Dharma."

Indra then gave Lomasha a mission. "Go to the earth and protect the sons of Pandu. Tell Yudhishthira that Arjuna has acquired the celestial weapons and will return when the time is right. But in the meantime, advise Yudhishthira to visit the various places of pilgrimage and distribute charity to Brahmanas."

And so, Sage Lomasha departed for the earth to bring hope and comfort to the exiled Pandavas.


# The Reunion

Without Arjuna, the Kamyaka forest felt devoid of joy for the remaining Pandavas. Yudhishthira and his brothers were filled with profound melancholy. "In Arjuna's absence the earth seems to be forlorn," Draupadi lamented. "Even this forest with its blossoming trees does not seem as delightful as before. I cannot feel any happiness, O King!"

Bheema, Nakula, and Sahadeva echoed her sentiments, their hearts heavy with the absence of their valiant brother. Seeing their grief, Yudhishthira became sadder still. 

Just then, the celestial Rishi Narada appeared. Seeking a distraction from their sorrow, Yudhishthira asked the sage about the merits of pilgrimage. Narada described the various holy Teerthas and the spiritual merit obtained by visiting them. 

Shortly after Narada departed, the great Rishi Lomasha arrived, bearing news directly from Indraloka. "I have seen Arjuna," Lomasha announced to the overjoyed Pandavas. "He has acquired celestial weapons and has mastered the arts of dancing, music, singing, and recitation. He will join you soon, but meanwhile, you must devote yourselves to austerities at the various Teerthas."

"In obedience to Indra and at the request of Arjuna, I will accompany you on your pilgrimage," Lomasha promised. 

The Pandavas, accompanied by Lomasha, Dhaumya, and other ascetics, set out on their grand pilgrimage. They traveled far and wide, bathing in sacred waters, performing rites, and giving away wealth and cows in charity. 

As they marched into the Gandhamadana forests, Draupadi, unaccustomed to such rugged travel on foot, faltered and collapsed in exhaustion. Yudhishthira placed her head in his lap and wept bitterly, cursing his addiction to dice that had brought such suffering to his delicate wife. 

Bheema immediately took charge. "Ghatotkacha!" he called out to his mighty Rakshasa son. 

Ghatotkacha appeared instantly. "Father, here I am, ready to do your bidding."

"My child, carry Draupadi and follow us," Bheema commanded. 

"I have only to think of him and he will be here," Ghatotkacha said, calling upon other benevolent Rakshasas. In moments, strong Rakshasas carried not only Draupadi but also the weary Pandavas, swiftly covering the treacherous terrain. 

They reached the sacred hermitage of Nara and Narayana at Badari. While dwelling there, a strong wind blew a beautiful, incredibly fragrant flower into their camp. 

"Bheema, look at this exquisite flower!" Draupadi exclaimed. "I must have a few of these. If you love me, dear Bheema, I must have these flowers."

Eager to please her, Bheema armed himself with his mace and bow and rushed into the forest in search of the flowers. As he hurried through the thick jungle, animals fled in terror from his thunderous path. 

Suddenly, he was brought to a halt. A monkey with a long tail was lying lazily on the ground, completely blocking the path. 

"I am ill," the monkey said lazily. "I was asleep and you woke me up. Being a man, you are wiser than I. You ought to be more considerate. Who are you?"

"I am Bheema, the son of Pandu, born to Kunti through the grace of Vayu!" Bheema announced proudly. "Now let me pass or I will give you a thrashing."

"O mighty Bheema, I am old and weak," the monkey sighed. "Have pity on me. Lift my tail and place it aside and go across."

Bheema grabbed the monkey's tail with his left hand, intending to casually toss it aside. To his utter shock, he couldn't move it an inch. Grunting with effort, he used both hands, straining until his muscles bulged and sweat poured down his face, but the tail remained immovable as a mountain. 

Defeated and humbled, Bheema folded his hands. "What's the matter with me? I, Bheema am unable to lift the tail of a mere monkey! But is it a mere monkey? If not, who is he?"

The monkey smiled. "O Bheema, I am your brother Hanuman!"

Bheema fell to his knees and embraced his elder brother, drawing immense strength and courage from him. Hanuman then pointed to a hidden path that would lead Bheema to the Saugandhika forest, the favorite playground of Kubera, the God of Wealth. 

Bheema reached the lake in the forest and began plucking the celestial flowers. The fierce Rakshasas and Yakshas guarding the lake attacked him for intruding without Kubera's permission, but Bheema easily defeated them, killing over a hundred. When the surviving guards reported the incident to Kubera, the Lord of Wealth merely smiled. "I know why Bheema is here! Return to your posts. Let him take the flowers."

Sometime later, a demon named Jatasura, disguised as a Brahmana, infiltrated the Pandavas' camp. Finding Bheema absent, the demon abducted Yudhishthira, Draupadi, and the twins, carrying them off. Bheema returned just in time, chased down the demon, and slew him in a brutal wrestling match, rescuing his family. 

After five long years of separation, as the Pandavas waited at their camp, the heavens suddenly lit up. The earth trembled, and a glorious chariot driven by Matali descended from the sky. 

Arjuna had returned. 

Adorned in celestial garlands, wearing a divine diadem, and carrying the formidable Gandeeva bow, Arjuna stepped down and bowed to his elders. The brothers rushed to embrace him, weeping tears of joy. Even Lord Indra descended briefly from heaven to bless Yudhishthira. 

"You will rule the earth," Indra promised before departing. 

That night, Arjuna recounted his incredible adventures. He told them of his battles with the Nivatakavachas, the fierce demons of the ocean whom he had annihilated single-handedly with Lord Indra's weapons. He spoke of destroying the flying city of Hiranyapura and its demonic inhabitants. 

"Now even the celestials cannot conquer you, let alone mortals," Yudhishthira said, his heart swelling with pride. "There is no strength in the world greater than yours, and there is no one to compare with you in valour."

United once more, the invincible brothers felt a renewed surge of hope. With Arjuna's celestial arsenal and Bheema's unmatched strength, the ultimate victory over the wicked Kauravas was now a certainty.


# Duryodhana Humbled

In the twelfth year of their exile, the Pandavas engaged in asceticism and the practice of archery in the Dwaita woods near a beautiful lake. A Brahmana who had visited them traveled to Hastinapura and described their pitiable living conditions to the Kaurava court. 

"Alas, the virtuous Pandavas who formerly slept on soft beds are now being made to sleep on the bare earth," Dhritarashtra lamented.

But Duryodhana, Shakuni, and Karna felt no pity. In fact, Karna was delighted. "O King, let us go to the Dwaita forest and look at the sons of Pandu," Karna suggested maliciously. "Let us see them shorn of their prosperity, the ones who never took orders and who never knew subjection. The happiness that one derives from beholding his foes in grief is great..."

Duryodhana was thrilled by the idea. "I have always wished glory and prosperity for Dhritarashtra and his sons. So, I bow to you and may that happen which is to happen to you! Let us invite them once again to a game of dice. Whoever loses now will be banished to the forest for twelve years..." wait, no, Duryodhana wanted to mock them. He said: "I want to subdue Yudhishthira without endangering my kin."

Shakuni devised a clever pretext. "It is the right season for the princes to go hunting," Shakuni told Dhritarashtra. "I beseech you to grant Duryodhana permission to go to the cattle-station at Dwaitavana to inspect the cattle."

Unaware of their true, malicious intentions, Dhritarashtra granted his permission, though he warned them not to provoke the Pandavas. 

Duryodhana, Karna, Shakuni, and Duhshasana set out with a massive army of soldiers and courtiers. They camped near the Dwaita forest, spending their time hunting and reveling. When Duryodhana ordered his men to construct pleasure houses near the lake, they were stopped by heavily armed guards. 

"Stop! You cannot enter these woods," the guards warned. "Our king has come here to sport and we will not allow any intrusion."

The guards belonged to Chitrasena, the powerful King of the Gandharvas, who had occupied the lake with his Apsaras and attendants. When Duryodhana's soldiers tried to force their way in, the Gandharvas attacked. 

Duryodhana, enraged, ordered his entire army to attack the Gandharvas. A fierce battle erupted. Karna fought valiantly, raining sharp arrows upon the Gandharvas and killing hundreds. But Chitrasena unleashed his magic powers, bewitching the Kaurava soldiers and causing them to scatter in panic. Seeing the tide turn, Karna, unable to withstand the furious onslaught, fled the battlefield in his chariot. 

With his army routed and his greatest warrior gone, Duryodhana was quickly overpowered. Chitrasena seized him, Duhshasana, and the women of the royal household, binding them in heavy chains. 

The fleeing remnants of the Kaurava army rushed to the nearby Pandava camp, begging for help. 

Bheema laughed mockingly. "The Gandharvas have done what we should have done ourselves! The wretch got what he deserved."

But Yudhishthira, ever righteous, was stern. "O Bheema, blood relatives are often divided and disputes arise among them. But any insult to the family from a stranger should not be tolerated. Our family honor is at stake. Furthermore, these men have come to us for refuge. It is our duty as Kshatriyas to protect them."

Obeying their elder brother's command, Bheema, Arjuna, Nakula, and Sahadeva armed themselves and marched against the Gandharvas. A terrible battle ensued. The Pandavas fought furiously, but the Gandharvas took to the skies, fighting from the clouds. 

Arjuna, unleashing his newly acquired celestial weapons, covered the sky with a devastating shower of arrows, causing panic among the Gandharvas. Realizing the immense power of his adversary, Chitrasena rushed down from the sky and revealed himself. 

"Arjuna! Hold your fire!" Chitrasena called out. "It is I, your friend Chitrasena from Indraloka!"

Arjuna immediately lowered his Gandeeva bow and smiled. "O Chitrasena, why are you punishing the Kauravas? What purpose does it serve to capture Duryodhana and his wives?"

"I knew they had come to this forest to mock you and your brothers in your misfortune," Chitrasena explained angrily. "Lord Indra perceived their evil intentions and commanded me to bring Duryodhana in chains to him. I am only fulfilling that command."

"But the benevolent Yudhishthira has requested that they be released," Arjuna pleaded. "If my brothers and I are worthy of receiving your grace, please release him."

Chitrasena, respecting his friend, agreed. They went to Yudhishthira, and the Gandharva king formally released Duryodhana. 

"O child," Yudhishthira addressed the humiliated Duryodhana gently, "never again commit such a rash act. For you can never attain happiness then. Now you and your brothers go back to the capital cheerfully."

Duryodhana returned to his camp, completely shattered. The humiliation of being saved by his hated enemies was too much to bear. "The Pandavas have been severely humiliated by us. They will never forget how we molested Draupadi," he told Duhshasana. "Yet, we owe our lives to them. I cannot live with this shame. I will fast unto death."

Duhshasana, Shakuni, and Karna frantically tried to dissuade him. "You are foolish to take leave of your senses," Karna argued. "The Pandavas are your subjects. It was their duty to rescue their king." 

But Duryodhana remained stubborn, spreading Kusha grass on the earth and sitting down to meditate and fast. 

His desperate actions, however, were noticed by the fierce Daityas and Danavas in the netherworld. Fearing that Duryodhana's death would weaken their cause against the gods, they performed a dark ritual and summoned him to Patala. 

A female deity arose from a fire and carried Duryodhana down to the demons. "O Duryodhana, you are of celestial origin," the Danavas told him. "You are the creation of Maheshwara. We have also settled the means for slaying Arjuna. The soul of the slain Naraka has assumed the form of Karna, and he will slay Arjuna. Do not give up your life. Go, rule the earth."

Consoled and emboldened by the demons, Duryodhana returned to the surface and abandoned his fast. 

Back in Hastinapura, Karna, seeking to restore Duryodhana's pride, took a mighty oath. "With a large army at my disposal, I will conquer the world for you." 

Karna set out on a massive military campaign, subjugating kings in the North, East, South, and West. He returned victorious, having conquered all the kingdoms of the earth. To celebrate, Duryodhana performed the grand Vaishnava Yajna, rivaling Yudhishthira's Rajasooya. 

Following the successful sacrifice, Karna made a chilling, public vow in the royal palace. "And here I take a vow that as long as I don't slay Arjuna, I shall not allow anyone to wash my feet. Nor shall I taste meat or drink wine. If anyone asks for anything, I shall not say 'no'."

When the spies brought news of Karna's terrible oath to the forest, Yudhishthira grew deeply anxious. He knew that the inevitable war was drawing closer, and Karna's unyielding determination posed a grave threat to the Pandavas.
