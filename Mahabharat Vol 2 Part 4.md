# The Twelfth Year

The Pandavas spent the twelfth year of their exile in the Kamyaka forest, devoting themselves to acts of charity and the performance of religious sacrifices. 

One day, the great Sage Vyasa visited them. "O virtuous Yudhishthira," the sage advised, "life has its ups and downs. Neither is it filled with joy nor with grief. When one is blessed with good fortune, one must know how to be happy, and when misery strikes, one should bear it with fortitude. Practice charity, for there is nothing more difficult than parting with hard-earned wealth."

Taking his words to heart, the Pandavas spent their days distributing charity and performing austerities. Draupadi faithfully served the many sages who visited them, using the wondrous copper Thali granted by Surya to ensure the food never ran out until she herself had eaten. 

Meanwhile, in Hastinapura, Duryodhana was filled with envy upon hearing of the Pandavas' spiritual success. When the notoriously ill-tempered Sage Durvasa arrived at his court with hundreds of followers, Duryodhana saw an opportunity. He served the sage with lavish hospitality, earning a boon. 

"O King, I wish to grant you a boon," Durvasa offered.

"Please visit my brothers in the forest," Duryodhana requested with feigned innocence, "but make sure to arrive after Draupadi has finished her meal." He secretly hoped that the hungry sage would curse the Pandavas when they failed to provide food. 

As expected, Durvasa and his hungry disciples arrived at the Pandava hermitage just after Draupadi had eaten. Yudhishthira welcomed the sage with due honor. "We will return shortly," Durvasa said. "Let Draupadi have a good meal ready for us." They went to the river to bathe. 

Draupadi was terrified. The Thali was empty. In her despair, she prayed to Lord Krishna for protection. 

Instantly, Krishna appeared at the door. "Draupadi, I am very hungry. Give me something to eat first."

"O Krishna, you must save me from the wrath of Durvasa!" Draupadi wept. "The Thali remains full until I have eaten. But I have already eaten, and there is no food left."

Krishna asked her to fetch the vessel. Examining it closely, he found a tiny bit of vegetable stuck to the side. Putting that small morsel into his mouth, Krishna said, "May all the souls in the universe be satiated with this."

At that exact moment at the river, Durvasa and his disciples suddenly felt their stomachs stretch as if they had eaten a massive feast. Realizing they were completely full and could not eat another morsel, they panicked. "If provoked, the Pandavas could consume us in their wrath," Durvasa worried. Fearing the insult of refusing a meal, the sage and his disciples quietly fled from the forest. 

Sometime later, while the Pandava brothers were out hunting, King Jayadratha of Sindhu passed by their hermitage. Seeing Draupadi standing alone, he was utterly smitten by her beauty. He approached her, offering her a place as his queen. When Draupadi angrily rejected him, the wicked king forcibly abducted her in his chariot. 

When the Pandavas returned, they discovered the abduction and immediately gave chase. A fierce battle ensued. The Pandavas effortlessly scattered Jayadratha's soldiers. Seeing his men dying, Jayadratha abandoned Draupadi and fled for his life. 

Bheema and Arjuna pursued him relentlessly. Bheema caught the fleeing king by his hair, dashed him to the ground, and beat him mercilessly. "I would kill you if it were not for Yudhishthira's mercifulness!" Bheema roared. To add to the humiliation, Bheema shaved Jayadratha's head, leaving only five tufts of hair, and forced him to publicly declare himself a slave of the Pandavas. 

Only at Yudhishthira's intervention was Jayadratha released. Deeply humiliated, Jayadratha retreated to the banks of the Ganga and performed severe penances to Lord Shiva, eventually securing a boon to defeat all the Pandavas in battle for one day—except for the invincible Arjuna. 

As the twelfth year drew to a close, a minor incident led to a profound test. A deer accidentally tangled its antlers in a Brahmana's fire-sticks and ran away. The Pandavas chased the deer to retrieve the sticks but lost track of it. Exhausted and parched, they sat under a tree. 

Nakula climbed a tree to look for water and spotted a nearby lake. He hurried to quench his thirst, but as he bent down, a mysterious voice echoed: "Wait! This lake belongs to me. First answer my questions and then you may drink."

Disregarding the warning, Nakula drank the water and instantly dropped dead. When he failed to return, Sahadeva went to look for him. He too ignored the voice, drank, and died. Arjuna and Bheema followed sequentially, both fiercely threatening the unseen voice, but they too drank the water and fell lifeless. 

Finally, Yudhishthira approached the lake. Seeing his invincible brothers lying dead, he was stricken with grief. As he approached the water, the voice spoke again. "I am a Yaksha. I have caused the death of your brothers. Answer my questions, or meet the same fate."

Yudhishthira agreed to answer. The Yaksha fired a series of profound questions. 
"What is heavier than the earth, higher than the heavens, faster than the wind, and more numerous than straws?"
"One's mother is heavier than the earth; one's father, higher than the mountains. The mind is faster than the wind, and our worries are more numerous than straws," Yudhishthira answered wisely. 

"What enemy is invincible? What constitutes an incurable disease?"
"Anger is the invincible enemy. Covetousness constitutes an incurable disease."

Pleased with Yudhishthira's immense wisdom and righteousness, the Yaksha revealed his true form: Yama, the Lord of Justice and Yudhishthira's divine father. 

"I am pleased with you," Yama smiled. "I grant you your brothers' lives. Furthermore, ask for another boon."

"We have spent twelve years in the forest," Yudhishthira requested. "Lord, please grant that in the thirteenth year of our exile, we will not be recognized by anyone."

"So be it," Yama granted. "Even if you wander the earth in your true forms, you will remain unrecognized. Through my grace, you will spend this thirteenth year secretly and undiscovered."

With their brothers revived and the critical boon secured, the Pandavas prepared for the most challenging year of their exile: the year of living incognito.


# The Pandavas at Virata's Palace

Having received the boon of anonymity from Lord Yama, the Pandavas discussed where they should spend their thirteenth year of exile. Arjuna suggested the kingdom of the aged Matsya King, Virata, describing him as powerful, virtuous, and charitable. 

The brothers decided to disguise themselves and enter the king's service in different capacities. Yudhishthira would present himself as Kanka, a Brahmana skilled in dice. Bheema chose to become Ballava, a cook who could also entertain the court as a wrestler. Arjuna, honoring the curse given to him by Urvashi, disguised himself as Brihannala, a eunuch skilled in music and dance, to teach the women of the palace. Nakula became Granthika to look after the king's horses, and Sahadeva took the name Tantipala to manage the royal herds. Draupadi resolved to serve Queen Sudeshna as a Sairandhri (maid) under the name Malini. 

Approaching Virata's capital, they decided to hide their celestial weapons. Nakula climbed a massive, sturdy Shami tree near a cremation ground and secured their weapons high in its branches. To deter people from coming near, they hung a foul-smelling animal carcass from the tree, warning locals that it was a cursed corpse. 

Entering the court of King Virata, their majestic bearings impressed the monarch. He gladly employed all of them in their requested roles, completely unaware of their true identities. 

Ten months passed peacefully in the Matsya kingdom. However, trouble arrived in the form of Keechaka, the incredibly powerful commander of Virata's army and Queen Sudeshna's brother. Catching sight of the disguised Draupadi, Keechaka was consumed by lust. He approached her, offering her unimaginable wealth and status if she became his wife. 

"I am a mere maid-servant," Draupadi warned him. "Moreover, I am a married lady. My husbands are five powerful Gandharvas. Anyone who comes near me will be destroyed by them. Rest assured, O Keechaka, if you touch me, you will not live."

Her warnings did not dampen Keechaka's ardor. He colluded with his sister, Queen Sudeshna, to send Draupadi to his chambers under the pretext of fetching wine. Despite Draupadi's fears, the queen ordered her to go. 

Praying to Surya for protection, Draupadi entered Keechaka's room. When he tried to grab her, she violently pushed him away and fled towards the royal court, where King Virata, Yudhishthira (Kanka), and Bheema (Ballava) were present. Keechaka pursued her into the open court, caught her by the hair, and kicked her brutally. 

Bheema, watching from his post, gnashed his teeth and readied himself to tear Keechaka apart. But Yudhishthira, fearing their identities would be exposed prematurely, signaled Bheema to control his anger. He then turned to Draupadi. "Do not interrupt the game of dice," Yudhishthira said coldly. "The Gandharvas will look after you. Go to Sudeshna's apartment."

That night, Draupadi slipped into the kitchens and wept before Bheema. "I cannot endure it when my weak husbands sit back and watch me being kicked!" she sobbed. "I will swallow poison rather than yield to Keechaka."

Wiping her tears, Bheema formulated a plan. "I will kill the wretched Keechaka. Arrange a meeting with him tonight at the dancing hall, and leave the rest to me."

The next day, Draupadi feigned submission and told Keechaka to meet her alone in the dancing hall at night. Overjoyed, Keechaka dressed in his finest robes and sneaked into the dark hall, finding a figure lying on a couch covered with a blanket. 

"O beautiful maiden, I have come," Keechaka whispered, reaching out in the dark. 

Suddenly, a powerful hand gripped his wrist. "You wretch!" Bheema's voice roared. "Today your sister will see you dragged to the ground!"

A terrifying, silent wrestling match ensued in the darkness. The two immense warriors locked arms, hurled each other around, and fought with ferocious strength. Finally, Bheema overpowered Keechaka, wrapping his massive arms around him. With a final, brutal effort, Bheema broke the commander's back, crushing him into a shapeless, lifeless mass. 

The next morning, Keechaka's followers, the Sootas, found his mangled body. Realizing Draupadi was the cause, they decided to burn her alive on his funeral pyre. They seized her and dragged her towards the cremation grounds. 

Hearing her cries, Bheema slipped out of the palace. He rushed ahead to the cremation grounds, uprooted a massive tree, and attacked the Sootas. Terrified by the "Gandharva," the Sootas dropped Draupadi and fled, but Bheema slaughtered all one hundred and five of them. 

The news of Keechaka and his followers being massacred by invisible Gandharvas sent shockwaves through the city. King Virata, terrified, ordered Draupadi to leave the kingdom, fearing her presence would invite destruction. But Draupadi pleaded, "Please wait for thirteen more days. By then, the Gandharvas will have accomplished their task and will repay you for your kindness."

The King reluctantly agreed, unaware that the thirteenth day would mark the exact end of the Pandavas' grueling thirteen-year exile.


# Panic in the Kaurava Camp

In Hastinapura, King Duryodhana's spies returned after searching far and wide for the exiled Pandavas. They had found no trace of them in the deep woods, but they brought an intriguing piece of news: the mighty Keechaka, commander of the Matsya kingdom, had been killed by invisible Gandharvas over a woman. 

Duryodhana and his allies realized the truth instantly. "Keechaka and his brothers have been slain," Duryodhana declared. "I believe this is the work of none other than Bheema, and the woman Sairandhri is Draupadi."

Seeing an opportunity, King Susharma of Trigarta, a bitter enemy of Matsya, proposed a plan. "Let us invade the prosperous Matsya kingdom," he suggested. "We shall plunder its wealth and cattle."

Karna supported the idea enthusiastically, and the Kauravas launched a two-pronged attack. The Trigartas marched towards the Matsya kingdom and seized the cattle at the northern end. When the news reached King Virata, he gathered his army. Taking his trusted servants—Kanka (Yudhishthira), Ballava (Bheema), Tantipala (Sahadeva), and Granthika (Nakula)—he marched to defend his borders. 

A fierce battle ensued. As darkness fell, the Trigartas gained the upper hand, and King Susharma successfully captured King Virata. 

Seeing the king taken, Yudhishthira quickly ordered Bheema to intervene. "Go and rescue King Virata from the enemy's clutches," Yudhishthira commanded. "This is a debt we have to discharge since we have taken shelter in his kingdom."

Bheema rushed forward, easily overpowering Susharma's guards and unhorsing him. He rescued King Virata, turning a crushing defeat into a glorious victory. 

Meanwhile, Duryodhana, accompanied by Bheeshma, Drona, Karna, and a massive Kaurava army, launched their attack on the southern borders of the Matsya kingdom, driving away sixty thousand head of cattle. The chief cowherd rushed to the capital, pleading with young Prince Uttara for help. 

Uttara boasted loudly in the court. "I would have set out right away if I had a skilled charioteer to guide my horses!"

Hearing this, Draupadi suggested that Brihannala (Arjuna in disguise) was an exceptional charioteer who had formerly served the great hero Arjuna. Eager to prove himself, Uttara commanded Brihannala to drive his chariot, and they set out amidst great fanfare. 

However, when they reached the battlefield and Uttara saw the terrifying ocean of the Kaurava army led by the legendary Bheeshma and Drona, his bravado vanished. "Let us turn back," he trembled. "I dare not fight these heroic warriors."

Before Arjuna could stop him, Uttara leaped off the chariot and ran for his life. Arjuna quickly chased him down and dragged him back. "If you do not wish to fight, come, hold the reins of the horses while I fight the enemy," Arjuna commanded. 

Arjuna directed the chariot towards the cremation ground. Reaching the old Shami tree, he ordered Uttara to climb it and bring down the wrapped bundle. When Uttara unwrapped it, he was mesmerized by the glowing, celestial weapons. 

"To whom do these excellent weapons belong?" Uttara asked in awe. 

"I am Arjuna," he finally revealed. "Kanka is Yudhishthira, Ballava is Bheema, Granthika is Nakula, and Tantipala is Sahadeva. The Sairandhri is Draupadi. Our exile is complete."

Uttara, overwhelmed with reverence, joyfully took the reins. Arjuna replaced his bangles with leather finger-guards, took up the mighty Gandeeva bow, and blew a deafening note on his celestial conch, Devadatta. 

The sound resonated across the battlefield. The Kaurava warriors recognized it instantly. 

"It is Arjuna!" Duryodhana exclaimed gleefully. "The Pandavas have failed to keep their pledge. They will have to go back to the forest for another twelve years!"

But Bheeshma intervened, correcting the King's fatal miscalculation. "The thirteenth year ended five months and twelve days ago! According to the lunar calendar, and even according to the solar calendar, the thirteenth year had ended six days earlier."

With his identity legally revealed and his exile fully complete, Arjuna unleashed a terrifying assault on the Kaurava army. He trounced Karna in a duel, forcing him to flee. He defeated Drona, Kripa, Ashwatthama, and even the great Bheeshma in direct combat. The entire Kaurava force scattered in panic. 

Finally, Arjuna used the mystic *Sammohana* weapon. The entire remaining Kaurava army, including Duryodhana, fell unconscious. Arjuna casually asked Uttara to collect the colorful clothes of the unconscious warriors for the princesses' dolls, then rounded up the stolen cattle. 

As they drove back, Arjuna hid his weapons once again and resumed his disguise as Brihannala, instructing Uttara to take credit for the victory. 

In the capital, King Virata rejoiced at his own victory over Susharma, but was anxious about his son fighting the Kauravas. When news arrived that Uttara had triumphed, Virata boasted proudly. "My son has defeated the Kauravas!"

Yudhishthira smiled knowingly. "He who has Brihannala as his charioteer is sure to succeed."

Enraged that a servant would credit a eunuch for his son's victory, Virata threw a handful of dice at Yudhishthira, cutting his nose. Draupadi quickly caught the blood in a golden bowl, preventing Yudhishthira's wrath from burning the kingdom. 

When Uttara returned, he confessed the truth. "I did not fight the battle. A celestial hero defeated the Kauravas and saved our cattle."

A few days later, the five brothers appeared in the royal court, dressed in royal robes and radiating majesty. King Virata was stunned to realize he had employed the legendary Pandavas as servants, and fell at Yudhishthira's feet, apologizing profusely for his arrogance. 

To cement their new alliance, King Virata offered his daughter, Princess Uttaraa, in marriage to Arjuna. Arjuna politely declined her for himself, having lived as her teacher, but accepted her as a bride for his son, Abhimanyu. 

The Pandavas relocated to the Matsya town of Upaplavya. Soon, the mighty Abhimanyu arrived, and a grand wedding ceremony was held, attended by Lord Krishna, Balarama, King Drupada, and numerous allies. The period of exile and hiding was officially over; the preparations for the great Kurukshetra war were about to begin.
