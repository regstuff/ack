# Great Freedom Fighters: Lokamanya Tilak

"Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it" -- this clarion call was given by Bal Gangadhar Tilak. He was a towering figure in the Indian Independence movement. A nationalist to the core, he believed strongly that modern education would inculcate patriotism and self-respect in the people. His inspiring speeches and writing landed Tilak in jail several times. But this did not dampen his spirit or will to cast off the yoke of foreign rule from his motherland. 

On July 23, 1856, Gangadhar Pant Tilak, the headmaster of the local Marathi school in Ratnagiri, was pacing up and down the veranda of his house. His wife was about to have a baby. He had three daughters, but longed for a son. When his aunt finally announced it was a boy, she teased him first by saying it was a girl, but the joyous father soon learned the truth. The child, Bal Gangadhar Tilak, was destined to become great. 

Bal had his early education at home under the strict supervision of his father. He was a brilliant child who easily mastered complex sums and Sanskrit shlokas. When Bal was ten, his father was transferred to Pune, allowing Bal to attend the best of schools. Shortly after they came to Pune, Bal's mother died, and his aunt looked after him. Bal was exceptionally intelligent, often doing math in his head instead of using a slate, but he was equally stubborn. Once, when peanuts were eaten and shells left on the classroom floor, he refused to clean them up, stating firmly, "I didn't eat in class. I didn't make the mess. So why should I take the punishment?" This frankness earned him the nickname "Mr. Blunt". 

When Bal was fifteen, he was married to a ten-year-old girl named Satyabhama, as child marriage was common in those days. When offered a gold ring as a wedding present, he asked for books instead. That same year, his father died, but Bal focused on his studies to fulfill his father's wishes. He joined the Deccan College at seventeen. There, he met Daji Abaji Khare, and they became lifelong friends. Tilak was not just a scholar; he was dedicated to physical fitness, wrestling, and swimming, eventually achieving a robust physique, though it briefly cost him his grades in his First Year Arts exams. 

Even in college, Tilak's patriotism shone through. He once urged a fellow student to tear down a picture of the Queen of England, declaring, "Display your love of freedom in a more worthy manner! Hammer the throne of England, if you can! If not, at least cherish such an ambition."

After graduating, Tilak studied law and befriended Gopal Ganesh Agarkar. They debated how to bring about change. Agarkar believed in social reforms, while Tilak argued, "We must have political freedom first!" They agreed that modern education was the necessary foundation for both. With the help of Vishnushastri Chiplunkar, they founded "The New English School" in Pune. They worked tirelessly, even sleeping in the school on blackboards. The school was a great success, but Tilak realized they needed to reach the adults too. 

To awaken the masses, they started two weeklies - *Kesari* in Marathi and *The Mahratta* in English. Setting up a second-hand printing press themselves, they distributed the first copies on January 4, 1881. The papers boldly exposed corruption, such as the Diwan of Kolhapur's plot to depose the adopted prince. Even when jailed for sedition over these exposures, Tilak and Agarkar remained steadfast, earning a rousing welcome upon their release. They went on to form the Deccan Education Society and started the Fergusson College. 

Tilak firmly believed in respecting Indian customs. When forced by a mischievous acquaintance to drink tea at a Christian mission, orthodox society demanded he perform a purification ceremony. Tilak refused, stating, "Is my religion so fragile that it can be polluted by a cup of tea?" A profound Sanskrit scholar, he delved into the Vedas and, based on astronomical evidence, wrote a book establishing the period of the Vedas as 4000 B.C. 

To unite the people, Tilak initiated the public celebration of the Ganesha festival and the birth anniversary of Shivaji Maharaj at Raigad. He actively promoted Swadeshi (indigenous) goods and called for a boycott of British cloth when the government unfairly raised excise duties. During a severe famine in Maharashtra, he urged farmers to demand their rights and not just passively starve, a stance that got him labelled an "extremist."

When the plague struck Pune, the government appointed Charles Rand as Plague Commissioner. The soldiers enforcing the quarantine were brutal and insensitive to the citizens' religious and social customs. Tilak actively helped the victims, setting up a segregation camp and a free kitchen, proving himself a true leader while others fled. When an angry youth assassinated Rand, the British cracked down, blaming Tilak's writings for inciting violence. 

Tilak was arrested for sedition. Refusing to apologize, he boldly addressed the jury: "...For me it can only be a matter of a few years, but future generations will look to your verdict and see whether you have judged wrong or right." He was sentenced to eighteen months of rigorous imprisonment. In jail, he survived on a sparse diet and lost 21 pounds. A copy of the Rig Veda sent by the famous German scholar Prof. Max Mueller inspired him to write his groundbreaking book, *The Arctic Home in the Vedas*. 

Released in 1898, Tilak continued his work. When Lord Curzon partitioned Bengal in 1905, Tilak predicted it would backfire. The partition spurred the Swadeshi movement, leading to massive bonfires of foreign clothes. Tilak and his allies, known as Nationalists, demanded Swaraj (self-rule). The Moderate faction of the Congress disagreed with their methods, leading to a split at the Surat Congress in 1907. 

As revolutionary violence grew, Tilak was arrested again in 1908 after a bomb was thrown in Muzaffarpur. Despite his eloquent defense that government oppression was the root cause of the violence, he was sentenced to six years of transportation to Mandalay, Burma. His deportation sparked a massive six-day strike in Bombay. 

In Mandalay, his sole companions were books and birds. While in prison, his wife passed away. Bearing the grief with stoicism, he wrote a monumental treatise on the Gita - the *Gita Rahasya*. 

Released in 1914, Tilak returned to a politically suppressed India. Ignoring advice to retire, he took up the cause of Home Rule, touring the country to demand that Indians manage their own domestic affairs. Fully revered as "Lokamanya" (Respected by the people), he attended the reunified Lucknow Congress session in 1916, where he famously declared, "Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it."

He later visited England to present India's case for Home Rule, winning support from the Labour Party. Lokamanya Tilak passed away on August 1, 1920. The cause he championed was taken up by millions, culminating in India's independence on August 15, 1947, fulfilling his lifelong dream.
