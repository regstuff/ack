# The Acrobat and Other Buddhist Tales - 1. The Acrobat

The city of Rajagriha was buzzing with excitement. 

"Have you heard? They are coming!" one man exclaimed. 
"Yes. It's been a year since we saw them last," his friend agreed. 

"They are coming! They are coming! The acrobats are coming!" the children cheered, running happily through the streets. 

A few days later, Rajagriha jubilantly welcomed its visitors. Crowds gathered in the large city square. 

"Let the show begin," the king announced. 

A tall wooden pole was erected in the center of the square. A beautiful young female acrobat nimbly climbed to the very top. 

"Superb! She looks just like a bird!" the crowd gasped as she balanced horizontally on the very tip of the pole. 

A young man in the crowd, staring with absolute fascination at the acrobat, was Ugrasena, the son of the king's wealthy treasurer. 

*Yes. The loveliest bird I ever set eyes on,* Ugrasena thought, his heart fluttering. *How graceful and skilful she is! If I can't make her mine, life is not worth living.*

Ugrasena went straight home and flung himself on his bed. "Father, mother—I want to marry the acrobat we saw today," he declared stubbornly. "If I can't marry her, I shall starve myself to death."

His parents were horrified. "My dear son, what are you saying?" his mother wept. 
"What can you have in common with a lowly acrobat?" his father argued. "We will find you a girl... one worthy of you..."

"It's her or none! I shall not even look at another girl," Ugrasena vowed, refusing to eat. 

The helpless treasurer and his wife finally sent Ugrasena's friend to speak to the acrobat's father. 

"My friend wishes to marry your daughter," the friend said, opening a large chest of gold. "As a marriage portion, here is all the gold you might desire."

The acrobat's father pushed the gold away. "For shame! Are you asking me to sell my daughter?" he frowned. "If your friend is willing to travel with us, wherever we go, he may marry her."

The treasurer and his wife were completely shocked to hear this condition. "Son, surely you will not leave us to go travelling with a poor acrobat!" his mother cried. 

"I will," Ugrasena said firmly, "for she means absolutely everything to me now."

So Ugrasena happily married the beautiful acrobat... and joined the wandering troupe. 

Being the only member of the troupe completely unskilled in acrobatics, he made himself useful in other simple ways. He fetched wood, carried water, and drove the heavy carts. And for a while, he was content with his simple life. 

Soon, a beautiful baby son was born to them. His wife spent all her free time between shows with the baby. 

"I'll rock you in my arms, O son of a wood-fetcher," she crooned to the baby one day, loud enough for Ugrasena to hear. 

Later, she sang, "Close your eyes and sleep, O son of a water-carrier."

And on the road, she rocked the baby, singing, "Let the jolt of the cart rock you to sleep, O son of a cart-driver."

Ugrasena finally lost his temper. "Woman, are you insulting me, when you sing those songs?" he demanded angrily. 

"Yes... I am," she admitted coldly. 

"You are trifling with me," Ugrasena frowned. "I shall leave you, and go away."

"Do as you think best," she replied indifferently. 

*It... it's her skill as an acrobat that makes her so bold... so vain... so indifferent!* Ugrasena thought furiously. 

Ugrasena went directly to his father-in-law. "I want to boldly become an acrobat," he declared. 

"I'll teach you the skills," the old man agreed gladly. 

Ugrasena worked incredibly hard and made rapid progress in his new profession, and soon—

"I proudly present before you a new performer—my own son-in-law," the old man announced to a village crowd. 

Ugrasena flipped rapidly through the air. 

"Look—he has turned seven somersaults! Unbelievable!" the crowd cheered wildly. "His wife can turn only four!"

Soon at Rajagriha, news spread that the acrobats were returning and, with them, the treasurer's son. 

"It seems Ugrasena will perform the incredible feat of turning fourteen somersaults high in the air!" the people gossiped. 
"Impossible! You are joking!"

On the appointed day, the entire city gathered in the square to watch Ugrasena perform. He climbed the tall pole. 

Just as he expertly turned the very first somersault—

"Look! The holy teacher!" someone in the crowd shouted. 

Ugrasena glanced down. Lord Buddha had calmly arrived in the square and was sitting peacefully under a tree. 

When Ugrasena came smoothly back to his balancing position on the pole, he looked down at the Buddha. 

*It... it is Buddha!* Ugrasena thought in awe. 

As Ugrasena gazed peacefully at Buddha... a profound change suddenly came completely over him. All his foolish pride, vanity, and earthly desire melted away. 

He slid quickly down the pole and fell to his knees. 

"Most venerable teacher," Ugrasena bowed humbly, "please admit me into your peaceful fold."


# The Acrobat and Other Buddhist Tales - 2. The Harvest

"Water my field, O cloud with your gentle rain; ripen my crops, O sun, into golden grain..." a farmer sang cheerfully to himself as he worked his land. 

Once a farmer was hard at work in his field when the Buddha happened to come walking by. 

"What are you doing, O farmer?" the Buddha asked gently. 

"I am clearing my field, sir," the sweating farmer replied. 

"Ah! What a fine harvest I will reap!" the farmer dreamed that night, looking out over his freshly cleared land. 

The next day, Buddha came walking that way again. 

"What are you busy with now, O farmer?" Buddha asked. 

"I am ploughing my field, sir," the farmer replied, guiding his two heavy oxen. 

Then one day, as the green shoots began to appear—

"Sir, I find that from the day I began to clear my field, you have been showing a great deal of interest in my work," the farmer told the Buddha happily. "So when my crop is harvested, I am going to share it with you."

The Buddha smiled gently. 

"For, you are now my partner," the farmer declared proudly. 

The days passed quickly and the farmer's crops ripened beautifully in the sun. 

*I shall call in the reapers tomorrow,* the farmer thought happily, looking at his golden field. 

But that very night, thick black clouds gathered... thunder and lightning rent the dark sky... and a raging, terrible storm broke out, flooding the land. 

The next morning, when the frantic farmer hurried to his field—

"Oh, God—no! I am ruined!" he wept, falling to his knees in the muddy water. His entire golden crop was completely destroyed. 

"And I have faithfully promised a share to my partner!" he sobbed into his hands. 

The grief-stricken farmer returned slowly home and took to his bed, refusing to speak or eat. 

Just then, Buddha arrived gently at his door. 

"O farmer, I wish to proudly speak to you," Buddha called. 

"Master, just a mouthful of food..." his worried wife pleaded with the farmer. 

"No—take it away. I will not eat," the farmer refused stubbornly, turning his face to the wall. 

The farmer slowly rose and came to sit respectfully by Buddha. 

"Tell me why you grieve, O farmer," Buddha asked softly. 

"Last night's terrible storm completely destroyed my crop..." the farmer wept. "...And I cannot keep my honest word to you."

Buddha smiled with deep compassion. "You would not grieve thus, if you truly knew what your sorrow springs from. It is strictly from attachment, O farmer."

"Attachment to your crops, to your word, and to me!" Buddha explained gently. "From attachment springs sorrow, from attachment springs fear."

The farmer listened carefully, his tears slowly stopping. 

"He who is truly free from attachment is entirely free of the heavy burden of both sorrow and fear," Buddha concluded. 

The farmer bowed deeply, a profound peace finally settling over his troubled heart. "I have perfectly understood, O venerable one."


# The Acrobat and Other Buddhist Tales - 3. The Golden Maiden

In the busy town of Sravasti lived young Kumara. He was the only son of rich parents and he had recently come of age. 

"Son, you are old enough to be married," his father announced one day. "We shall choose a good, beautiful girl for you and..."

"Father, I don't want to get married," Kumara refused firmly. 

But the old couple did not give up easily. Every once in a while, they put the question to him again and again. 

"They will simply not take 'no' for an answer," Kumara sighed to himself. "The only way out is to agree but make absolutely sure that they won't ever find the right girl for me."

The clever young man hired the most skilled artisans to carve a stunning golden image of matchless, perfect beauty. 

"Mother! Father!" he called, pulling a silk sheet off the statue. "I will gladly marry but... only one such as this!"

His parents were stunned by the statue's flawless beauty. "If you find a woman as perfectly beautiful as this image, I will marry her," Kumara promised, confident that no such human woman existed. 

"We will find her," his father declared stubbornly. "This golden image... will be taken... from town to town... till exactly such a girl is found."

"You'll absolutely never find her," Kumara laughed confidently. 

Months rolled slowly by. Then at long last, one day—

"The maiden is found, sir," a tired messenger reported. "In the distant city of Sagala."

"What? Can it be so?" Kumara gasped in shock. 

"Let her be brought here with all speed," his father ordered excitedly. The delighted old couple sent forth a royal messenger with rich, expensive gifts specifically for the maiden and her parents. 

Kumara received the surprising news with very mixed feelings. 

*When I got the statue perfectly made, I honestly never imagined that exactly such a girl could ever be found,* he worried. *But... now that she is definitely found, I can hardly wait to warmly see her!*

"They say that strictly compared with the golden image... she is far, far lovelier!" he whispered to himself. "I cannot even begin to imagine the incredible extent of her pure beauty!"

He impatiently awaited the arrival of the girl. He paced nervously on his balcony. "Is that her carriage?" he asked every time a cart passed by. 

"No! It is not. Oh, how incredibly slowly the hours crawl by!"

Each passing day, romantic thoughts of her kept him happy company. He dreamed of seeing her face. 

"Oh!" Kumara sighed one dark night. "It is night already, the tenth long night exactly since she bravely set out. Where is she?"

He rushed down to his parents. "Father, Mother—she is still not securely come. What could possibly delay her so?"

"She... she absolutely must be safely on her way, son!" his mother comforted him. 
"Have they refused the proposal?" he panicked. 
"No, my son."

"Then exactly why hasn't she come yet? Why? Why?" Kumara shouted. 

His father sighed heavily. "We must definitively not gracefully keep it secretly from him absolutely any longer," he whispered to his wife. "Yes. He will have to be truthfully told."

"Son..." his father began slowly. 
"Yes?" Kumara asked hopefully. 

"She will absolutely never safely come."
"But... why...?"
"She is sadly dead, son," his mother wept. 

"DEAD?" Kumara screamed. 

"On her way safely here, she tragically fell terribly ill and quietly died," his mother explained. 

Kumara fainted dead away on the floor. 

Just then, there on the doorstep, peacefully stood Lord Buddha. 

"Arise, youthful one," Buddha called gently. 

Kumara's parents helped him up. "What is this terrible sorrow that heavily afflicts you?" Buddha asked. 

"Holy sir, a beautiful woman tragically died on the rough road and... the terrible news has completely filled me with absolutely unbearable grief," Kumara sobbed. 

"She was the absolute most perfectly beautiful woman securely on earth," Kumara wept. "She was the woman I was happily to formally marry."

Buddha smiled gently. "O Kumara, you absolutely do not really grieve simply because a woman died."

"Un...?" Kumara frowned in confusion. 

"You deeply grieve exactly because the specific woman you passionately desired died," Buddha explained calmly. "From blind desire springs heavy sorrow; from desire springs deep fear. He that is perfectly free completely from desire neither sorrows nor fears."

Kumara listened carefully, and the deep truth of the words slowly sank in. And Kumara prostrated himself fully before the Buddha, exactly as the great teacher's profound calmness peacefully enveloped his very own broken heart.


# The Acrobat and Other Buddhist Tales - 4. Buddha and Krisha Gautami

"He has not safely woken up for many long hours," she worried. "He absolutely must be incredibly ill indeed."

Her husband walked quietly into the room. "How is the child, Gautami?" he asked gently. 

He leaned securely over and touched the boy's cold face. He gasped. 

"Oh, God! No! He is dead," the father wept, covering his face. 

But Krisha Gautami refused to cleanly accept the terrible truth. "Go, son, make arrangements to safely cremate the body," a neighbour advised her gently. 

"Cremate the body? Burn my son? No!" Gautami screamed. "Gautami, he's dead," her husband pleaded with her. 

"Then I will surely find a secure remedy for death," Gautami declared stubbornly. 

She picked up the lifeless baby. "Gautami! Wait! Come safely back! Don't you understand? He's dead," her husband yelled from the doorway. 

But she ignored him and walked down the street. "Kind sir, do you happen to know of any cure for my sick son?" she asked every person she met. 

"Poor woman! She has probably never seen a dead person before," one kind man whispered to himself. "I must help her."

He approached her gently. "I'm afraid, I don't know a cure," he said. "...But the holy Buddha definitely can perfectly help you. Go safely to him."

Completely full of pure hope, Krisha Gautami at once went directly to the sacred grove of Buddha. 

"My name is Krisha Gautami, venerable one," she bowed before him. "I was told that you could magically cure my son. So I have safely come directly to you."

Buddha looked peacefully at the poor, dead child and smiled gently. "Then—go, quickly fetch me exactly a few tiny mustard seeds specifically from a quiet house that has absolutely never ever known death."

Krisha Gautami continued to patiently meditate completely with her eyes peacefully fixed safely on the small flame of the lamp... and gracefully attained perfect Nirvana.
