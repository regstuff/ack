# The Adventures of Agad Datta: Jain Tales of Long Ago

## 1. The Adventures of Agad Datta

One day, Dridha Prahari, one of ancient Kaushambi's most renowned teachers, presented his gifted disciple, Agad Datta, at the royal court. The youth performed many astounding feats of archery.
"He certainly is a skillful archer!" the King of Kaushambi exclaimed. "Give him a hundred gold coins for his trouble and send him away."
When the minister brought the coins, Agad Datta respectfully declined. "Please don't misunderstand me, Maharaj, but... I came here to win your appreciation, not your gold."

Just then, a deputation of distressed citizens entered the court. "Maharaj, the dacoits have struck again! I've lost everything but my life," a citizen wept. 
"We've complained to the chief of the guards. But the situation hasn't improved," another complained. 
The king looked sternly at the chief of guards, who mumbled, "Maharaj, I have tried my best, but the thugs are elusive."

The king turned to the talented young archer. "Now tell me, of what use are your feats when dacoits harass my citizens with impunity?"
"Maharaj, give me a chance!" Agad Datta volunteered boldly. "I'll try to capture the leader of the gang."
"If you do that, you will win my appreciation and my gratitude," the king promised. 

As Agad Datta left the palace, he formulated a plan. "The best way to avoid attracting attention would be to change into some old clothes. Thieves usually choose gambling dens, liquor shops, old gardens, and temple ruins as hide-outs. I must first visit these."
He went to a forest on the outskirts of the city and sat under a tree. Just then, a sadhu (ascetic) approached and sat under the same tree. Agad Datta noticed the man looked worried and poor. "He'd make a good disguise!" Agad Datta thought. 

"Son, who are you? Why do you look so worried?" the sadhu asked.
Deciding not to reveal the truth, Agad Datta lied, "I... I'm from Ujjayini. I've lost all my wealth here in Kaushambi."
"Don't worry," the sadhu smiled. "I'll show you how to get it back. And if you are clever enough, you'll get back more than you've lost."

As soon as the sun set, the sadhu led Agad Datta toward the city. Suddenly, they heard the roar of a wild animal. To Agad Datta's amazement, the sadhu swiftly drew a hidden sword from the wooden stem of his trident! Realizing it was a false alarm, the sadhu sheathed the sword. "What! A sadhu armed with a secret weapon! Strange! Very strange!" Agad Datta thought. 

The sadhu led him to the backyard of a large mansion in the city. "I'll have to get into the house," the sadhu whispered. Instead of knocking, he deftly broke a hole through the wall! 
"He's breaking into the house! He... he's a thug!" Agad Datta realized excitedly. "I won't have to go much further in search of my man!"
The sadhu soon emerged with a heavy bundle of stolen loot and led Agad Datta to a secret hideout in the forest. 

At the hideout, several other thugs were waiting. "Ah, my friends!" the sadhu greeted them. "I see that you have made a better haul than I! But I have found a new disciple!" 
The gang decided to rest until dawn. While the other thugs quickly fell asleep, Agad Datta stayed awake, watching the sadhu closely. 
To his horror, he saw the sadhu sprinkling a mysterious powder on the faces of his sleeping gang members! "He plans to kill his own men! The scoundrel!" Agad Datta realized. 

Agad Datta quickly snatched the sadhu's hidden sword. "No you don't, you rascal!" he shouted, striking the sadhu down. 
Mortally wounded, the sadhu gasped, "My son... you shouldn't have done that. Never mind... I forgive you... now... take my sword... Go to the corner of the crematorium where you will find the entrance to an underground passage. Stand near it and call out. My sister will appear. Give her my sword. She will marry you... and you will become... the master of... all my wealth..." With those words, the bandit leader died.

Curious, Agad Datta followed the instructions and found the secret entrance. A beautiful maiden emerged. When Agad Datta handed her the sword, she recognized it immediately. "It's my brother's sword. You are welcome, stranger!" she smiled. But inwardly, she raged, "The villain! He's slain my brother. I must avenge his death!"

She led Agad Datta down into a luxurious underground cellar. "You seem to be tired. Why don't you rest on this bed while I warm up some food for you," she offered sweetly. 
Suspicious that this was a death-trap, Agad Datta pretended to lie down but quickly rolled off the bed. Just in time! A heavy stone ceiling crashed down precisely on the bed, crushing it completely!

"That was quick and clever of me! My poor brother! May your soul rest in peace for I have avenged your death!" the maiden gloated. But when she checked the rubble, she found no body. 
"No, you haven't, wicked one! I'm still alive," Agad Datta announced, stepping out of the shadows. Terrified, the maiden begged for mercy. "You may plead with the Maharaja," Agad Datta replied, arresting her. 

Agad Datta presented the bandit's sister to the king and narrated the entire adventure. "Maharaj, I request you to pardon her. Now that her brother is dead, she has no one to support her," Agad Datta requested compassionately. 
The king agreed, making her a maid to the queen. He then sent his guards to the hideout, where they easily arrested the rest of the gang, who were still deeply asleep from the sadhu's drugged powder. All the stolen wealth was recovered and returned to the joyful citizens. 

The king offered Agad Datta the prestigious post of Chief of Guards in Kaushambi. However, Agad Datta respectfully declined. "My father was charioteer to the King of Ujjayini. It was my mother's wish that I learn the art of warfare here and then return to acquire the office once held by my father." 
Before leaving, Agad Datta married Shyama, a local girl, and together they set out for Ujjayini in a chariot. 

On their journey, they met several travelers who asked to join them for safety. Among them was another sadhu. Agad Datta agreed. But the next morning, as they traveled, the sadhu realized who Agad Datta was. "The villain who killed my friend! My search has ended," the sadhu plotted. He was actually the notorious dacoit Dhanpujak, seeking revenge!

That evening, they camped in a jungle. The sadhu volunteered to fetch food from a nearby cowherd settlement. He returned with a large vessel of sweet *kheer* (rice pudding). Suspicious of the stranger, Agad Datta warned his wife not to eat it. The other hungry travelers, however, greedily ate their portions. Soon, the drugged *kheer* put all the travelers into a deep stupor. 

When it became pitch-dark, the sadhu pulled a hidden sword from his trident and advanced to murder the sleeping Agad Datta. But Agad Datta was wide awake and waiting! "You scoundrel!" he shouted, springing up. In the fierce fight that followed, the wicked dacoit was slain. 
When the travelers awoke, they realized how Agad Datta had saved their lives from the treacherous bandit. 

Agad Datta finally reached Ujjayini, where his proud mother welcomed him. Presenting himself at the royal court, the King of Ujjayini, who had already heard tales of his immense valor, proudly appointed the brilliant Agad Datta as the Royal Charioteer.

## 2. The Royal Priest

The Raja of Simhapur loved hearing tales of adventure from merchants who sailed to distant lands. That was how the successful merchant Bhadramitra of Padminikhet found himself at the Raja's court. 
After sharing his exciting stories, Bhadramitra mentioned, "I will be setting sail again tomorrow. I need your help. I have a casket of gold which I want to keep with someone trustworthy."
"You can safely leave it with my royal priest, Shribhuti," the Raja recommended. "A more honest man would be hard to find!"

Trusting the Raja's word, Bhadramitra left his casket of gold with Shribhuti and set sail. Over the years, he traveled far, buying and selling a variety of goods, amassing a great deal of wealth. Finally, he began his homeward voyage, eager to present gifts to the good king and the honest priest. 
But tragedy struck. A sudden, violent storm mercilessly tossed his ship about, eventually sinking it. Bhadramitra narrowly escaped by clinging to a wooden plank. After floating on the turbulent seas for hours, he was washed ashore, completely destitute, near the city of Simhapur. 

Exhausted, starving, and wearing only rags, Bhadramitra stumbled to Shribhuti's house late at night. "It's me, Bhadramitra. I have come for my casket," he pleaded weakly. 
To his shock, Shribhuti coldly turned him away. "Away with you! I don't know any Bhadramitra. You've come to the wrong place."
"No! You can't do this to me! I've lost everything... I need my gold," Bhadramitra wept, but the greedy priest slammed the door. 

The next day, Bhadramitra went to the palace, confident the Raja would help him. But the palace guards, seeing his beggar-like appearance, chased him away with spears. Desperate, Bhadramitra squatted outside the palace gates, shouting for justice day and night. "The royal priest has swindled me of my wealth! I want justice!" 

Shribhuti confidently ignored him, telling the Raja, "He is a madman. You know that whatever I earn each day, I give away in charity. Would I swindle anyone?"
But Bhadramitra persisted until the Raja could no longer ignore the cries. The Raja asked his Chief Minister to investigate. The minister kindly took Bhadramitra to his home, fed him, and listened to his tragic tale. 
"I believe you," the minister said thoughtfully. "But Shribhuti flatly denies it, and we have no proof! Wait, I have an idea. Why don't you invite Shribhuti to a game of dice?" he suggested to the Raja. 

The minister laid out a clever plot. The next day, the Raja invited Shribhuti for a friendly game of dice. As they played, the Raja complained of the heat. "How clammy my palms are! I'd better remove my signet ring," he said casually, placing it on the floor. 
Shribhuti followed suit, removing his own signet ring. 
A moment later, the Raja clumsily knocked the dice away. "Oh! How clumsy of me!" 
"Don't worry, Maharaj. I'll pick them up," Shribhuti offered, looking away. In that split second, the Raja swiftly swapped their signet rings!

A few minutes later, the minister entered. "Maharaj, pardon me for interrupting, but I have an urgent matter to discuss in private." The Raja excused himself, stepping outside. He secretly handed Shribhuti's ring to the minister. "I did manage to switch them. I leave the rest to you."

The minister immediately sent a royal attendant to Shribhuti's house. The attendant knocked on the door and told Shribhuti's wife, "The priest has asked for the casket of gold coins."
The wife hesitated. "But... but..."
"If you don't trust me, take a look at this. He asked me to show it to you if you were hesitant," the attendant said, holding up Shribhuti's unique signet ring. 
Recognizing her husband's ring, the wife immediately brought out the casket. "Please deliver it safely, and cover it with your cloth. He wouldn't want anyone to see it," she whispered nervously. 

The attendant brought the casket straight to the palace, where the Raja had resumed his game with Shribhuti. The Raja suddenly placed the casket on the board. "Do you recognize that casket, Shribhuti?" 
Shribhuti turned pale, trembling as he struggled to find an excuse. Just then, the minister walked in with Bhadramitra. "Perhaps you do recognize Bhadramitra, the merchant of Padminikhet. Is your memory refreshed, or does it need another shock?" the minister demanded. 

The hypocritical priest was utterly exposed. The Raja handed the casket back to the overjoyed merchant. "Come, take your casket, Bhadramitra. While I'm truly sorry for the misery we have caused you, I am grateful to you for exposing this hypocrite."
The Raja then turned to the disgraced priest. "Shribhuti, I don't know who is more embarrassed—you, the royal priest who could act like a common cheat, or I, the Raja who recommended you for your honesty! Go away and never set foot in my kingdom again!"
