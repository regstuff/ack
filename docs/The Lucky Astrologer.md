# The Lucky Astrologer - 1. Manduka - The Lucky Astrologer

There was once a poor, foolish Brahmana whom everyone ignored. Even his own father used to say, "You are as dull as a *manduka* (frog) in a well."

Tired of being ignored, the Brahmana decided to do something to make people notice him. One evening, noticing a grand wedding at a rich merchant's house, he quietly stole the bridegroom's horse and hid it near a stream. 

The next morning, the merchant was frantic about the missing horse. The Brahmana's wife, acting on his instructions, went to the merchant and said her husband was a great astrologer who could help. 

The merchant sent for him immediately. The Brahmana pretended to study some astrological charts and then announced, "You'll find the horse near the stream. But make haste, he is in danger of being stolen."

The merchant's men rushed to the stream and found the horse. The overjoyed merchant rewarded the Brahmana with a bag of coins and praised his incredible astrological skills. Word spread quickly, and the "astrologer" became famous. 

One day, the King summoned him to the palace. "The Queen's necklace has been stolen," the King said. "You must not leave the palace till the necklace is found."

The terrified Brahmana was locked in a royal chamber. Knowing his trickery would be exposed and he would likely be executed, he began to curse his own tongue for getting him into this mess. 

"What have you done, *Jihvah*? Why did you do it?" he cried out. (*Jihvah* is the Sanskrit word for tongue).

Outside the door, a royal maid was peeping through the keyhole. By sheer coincidence, her name was Jihvah, and she was the one who had stolen the necklace! 

Hearing the astrologer call out her name, she burst into the room, terrified. "O holy sir, have mercy on me! I did it in a moment of weakness. I am Jihvah, the maid. I heard you," she confessed, thinking he had magically discovered her guilt. 

Realizing his incredible luck, the Brahmana promised to forgive her if she told him where she hid the necklace. 

Later, he led the King to the garden and pointed to a spot under a tree. The guards dug there and found the missing necklace! 

The King was amazed but his minister was suspicious. "He must have hidden it there himself," the minister whispered. "Let us test him once more."

The King placed a small creature inside a sealed jar and asked the Brahmana, "Can you tell us what is in that jar?"

The Brahmana realized he was finally trapped. He lamented his fate, muttering his childhood nickname under his breath. "O *Manduka*, you were better off in the well!" 

"Well done!" the King exclaimed, opening the jar. "I should never have doubted your powers. There is indeed a *manduka* (frog) in the jar."

The King rewarded the incredibly lucky Brahmana with costly gifts, and he returned home a wealthy hero.


# The Lucky Astrologer - 2. The Hidden Meaning

Buddhuram was a hard-working but dim-witted farmer with absolutely no ambitions. His wife, however, was just the opposite. She was constantly nagging him to make more money, especially when she saw their neighbours prospering. 

Matters came to a head when their neighbour's husband, a poet, was rewarded with two gold coins by the king. 

"Well, I have had enough!" Buddhuram's wife declared. "If her husband can write poetry, so can you! You are going to sit down and write a poem too!"

"But I don't know how to read and write," Buddhuram protested. 
"I'll teach you! And you won't get any food until you learn!" she threatened. 

Every day, she taught him a letter and sent him to the riverside to practice writing it in the sand. 

One day, while practicing, a passing stranger told Buddhuram, "You need *inspiration* to write poetry, my friend! It is a feeling! When you get inspired, a thrill runs through your body, and you tremble!"

That night, Buddhuram forgot a letter and was denied dinner. Miserable and hungry, he decided to end his life by jumping into the river. 

The river was icy cold, and Buddhuram immediately began to shiver and tremble violently. "What's happening? Why's my whole body trembling?" he thought. "Perhaps this is what the pandit meant by INSPIRATION!"

Thrilled that he finally had "inspiration," he looked around for something to write about. He spotted a crow (named *Kalia* in folklore) dipping its beak in the water and rubbing it against a stone. 

"What a stroke of luck!" Buddhuram thought, and composed a poem on the spot: 
"You dip it in water and sharpen it again and again. O Kalia, I can read your mind."

He ran home and proudly recited his "poem" to his wife. Thinking it might actually be a divine verse, she sent him to the king's palace to recite it. 

When Buddhuram recited his simple verse to the royal court, the entire audience of learned scholars burst out laughing at the nonsense. 

However, the King maintained a discreet silence. "No one would dare to come here and recite such nonsense unless there was some profound hidden meaning," the King reasoned. His ministers agreed, not wanting to appear foolish. 

The King ordered the poem to be written on a huge board in the courtyard, offering ten gold coins to anyone who could interpret it. For days, scholars puzzled over the rhyme but found no meaning. 

Then, one morning, the King was sitting for his daily shave. His royal barber, whose name happened to be Kalia, was acting very nervous. 

Kalia the barber picked up his razor, dipped it into a cup of water, and began to sharpen it on a stone... again and again. 

The King, looking at the barber's actions, subconsciously recited Buddhuram's poem aloud: "You dip it in water and sharpen it... again and again. O Kalia, I can read your mind!"

Hearing this, Kalia the barber dropped his razor in terror and fell to his knees. "Forgive me, Maharaj!" he cried. "It was not my idea at all! The conspirators asked me to cut your throat!"

The King was stunned. He realized that the "meaningless" poem was actually a divine prophecy warning him of the assassination plot! 

The guards arrested the treacherous barber, and the King summoned Buddhuram. 

"He is a true poet! He sees all!" the King declared, rewarding Buddhuram with immense wealth. Buddhuram returned home a rich and famous hero, much to the absolute delight of his ambitious wife.
