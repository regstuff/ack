# Dhruva and Ashtavakra

## Dhruva

Dhruva was the son of Suneeti, the senior queen of King Uttanapada. Unfortunately for Dhruva and Suneeti, Uttanapada loved Suruchi, his second queen, better and was partial to her. 

One day, as Queen Suruchi walked up to her son, Uttama, who was playing near the King, she said, "Come, my son. Sit on your father's lap. It is your rightful place as the future king."
When Dhruva saw Uttama sitting on Uttanapada's lap, he ran towards them. "I too will sit on my father's lap!"
"Certainly not!" Suruchi snapped. "You have no right to be there. Go away."
"Where shall I go, mother?" the little boy asked. 
"Go to Lord Narayana and seek his favour to be reborn as my son. Only then can you enjoy the same rights as Uttama," Suruchi sneered. 

Stung by her cruel words, Dhruva ran crying to his mother Suneeti. "She said only Uttama had the right to sit on the King's lap. Mother, Suruchi told me to go and seek Lord Narayana's favour if I want to sit on my father's lap."
Suneeti hugged him, holding back her tears. "I am a Kshatriya prince, am I not, mother?" Dhruva asked. "Yes, my child."
"Will Narayana really help me, mother?"
"Yes, my child. He never fails those who turn to him."
"Then I shall seek him out," Dhruva resolved. 

"Go, my son," Suneeti blessed him. "He is the only one who can help us. It will not be easy to find him. But if you try hard enough you will succeed."
"I will succeed, mother. I will not return till I meet the Lord. I will become a greater king than my father or his father before him," Dhruva promised as he set off into the forest. 

Meanwhile, Sage Narada came to know about Suruchi's harsh words and Dhruva's resolve. He went to see Dhruva. 
"My child, why don't you accept your fate and go back to your mother?" Narada asked, testing him. 
"O master, I am Dhruva, the son of mighty Uttanapada, a Kshatriya. And I am determined to win the Lord's favour."
Narada was extremely pleased by his determination. "If you meditate in the Madhuvan forest on the banks of the Yamuna, you will find the Lord."
"O saint, teach me how to meditate," Dhruva requested. 
"Repeat with me - 'Om Namo Bhagawate Vasudevaya!' Say that over and over again, and concentrate on that," Narada instructed. 

As Dhruva set out for Madhuvan, Narada went to the palace of King Uttanapada. He noticed that though Uttanapada welcomed him respectfully, his mind was elsewhere. 
"Holy sage, I am the vilest of men," the king wept. "I did not utter a word when Suruchi drove poor Dhruva away with her cruel words. Alas! He has gone away alone into the forest. The beasts will devour him."
"Fear not, O king," Narada comforted him. "The Lord himself will protect your son. He will come back bringing glory to all."

Meanwhile, Dhruva had reached Madhuvan. In the first month, he lived only on the fruits he could find in the forest. Gradually he gave up even that and ate only grass and leaves. In the third month, he stopped eating and lived only on water. In the fourth month, he gave up even that and lived on air. Throughout the period he repeated the mantra over and over again. 

In the fifth and sixth months, his meditation was so intense that he even stopped breathing. Dhruva's concentration stopped the very air from flowing. There was panic in heaven and on earth. 
"O Lord, give us life-giving air! We cannot breathe," the people gasped. 
"Do not despair," Lord Narayana reassured the devas. "I shall descend to earth and grant Dhruva the boon he seeks."

As Dhruva meditated, his mental image of the Lord vanished. He opened his eyes, and there stood Lord Narayana himself. 
"The Lord has come to me!" Dhruva bowed. 
"I know what has brought you to Madhuvan. Your wish will be fulfilled. You shall be a great king," the Lord smiled. "You shall rule for 36,000 years. Then will I give you a place in heaven where you shall stay forever as the Pole Star."

Meanwhile at the palace of King Uttanapada, a messenger brought the good news. "Your majesty, Prince Dhruva is on his way home."
"I cannot wait to see my son. Commander, get the chariots ready. We will all go to receive him!" the overjoyed king declared. 
Queen Suneeti stepped forward and embraced her child. "O my son, my glorious son!"
Amidst great rejoicing, Dhruva was carried back to the palace. As soon as Dhruva was old enough to rule, Uttanapada crowned him king and retired to the forest.

## Ashtavakra

Kahoda was the favourite disciple of Uddalaka, a renowned scholar in the Vedas. Uddalaka had a daughter called Sujata. Kahoda was clever and hard-working and soon mastered the texts. As his guru had promised, he was married to Sujata. 
"You may continue to live here at the ashram, and teach your disciples," Uddalaka offered. 
Though they had to lead an austere life, Kahoda and Sujata were happy. A few months later, Sujata became pregnant. "I must expose my child to the sacred influence of the hymns even while he is in the womb," Kahoda decided. 

So Sujata sat by her husband as he recited verses from the scriptures; and the child in her womb silently absorbed what the learned father recited. 
One day as Kahoda sat among his disciples, instructing them, a voice spoke up. "That's not the way to chant that hymn, father."
Kahoda was startled. "Who was that? Where did that voice come from?"
"It must have been the voice of our unborn child," Sujata realised. 
"Such impertinence! And from one yet unborn!" Kahoda was furious at being put to shame before his disciples. He cursed the unborn child. "O vain soul! May you be born deformed - may your body be twisted in eight places!"

The months rolled on and the time for Sujata's confinement drew near. One day, Sujata said, "Our child will soon be born. We will need money to provide it with the comforts of life."
"I will go to King Janaka of Mithila," Kahoda decided. "He is generous to learned Brahmans. Besides, he is at the moment performing a grand yagna."

When Kahoda reached Mithila, Janaka welcomed him. "O revered sage, you have come at the right moment. A scholar named Bandhi has come to Mithila. No one can defeat him in debate."
"Let me try, O king," Kahoda offered. 
"Think it over carefully. Though you will be well rewarded if you win... you will be drowned in the river if you don't. And many scholars have lost their lives," Janaka warned him. 
"I accept the challenge," Kahoda declared confidently. 

The debate began but did not continue for long. Kahoda was no match for Bandhi. 
"Bandhi has won again. Poor Kahoda will be drowned like all the others," the crowd murmured. Bandhi was triumphant. 
When the tragic news reached Sujata, she broke down. "Alas! My desire for wealth has cost me my husband and my child its father."
"Don't grieve, Sujata. Your child shall not lack a father's love," Uddalaka consoled her. 

A few days later Sujata had a baby. "Alas! His body is deformed in eight places," she wept. 
"Let him be called Ashtavakra (crooked in eight places). My wife too has just been blessed with a son. We have named him Shwetaketu," Uddalaka said. 
Growing up under the tender, loving care of Uddalaka, Ashtavakra soon began to look upon him as his father. The years rolled on. Uddalaka taught Ashtavakra and Shwetaketu. When the boys were twelve years old, they had mastered the Vedas. 

Suddenly one day, Shwetaketu pulled Ashtavakra off Uddalaka's lap. "You are not his son! He is not your father."
Deeply humiliated, Ashtavakra ran to his mother. "Mother, where is my father? Oh! Why do you weep? Tell me!"
"Your father is dead," Sujata confessed, and she told him all that had happened. 
"I must defeat Bandhi and have him drowned," Ashtavakra vowed. 

That night, Ashtavakra went to Shwetaketu. "Come on, Shwetaketu. Let us go to Mithila. It will be a good experience for us. King Janaka has not been able to complete the yagna he started twelve years ago."
Shwetaketu was willing, and they left at once. 
When the boys reached the gates of Janaka's palace at Mithila, the guards stopped them. "Who are you? What do you want?"
"We have come to meet the King. Please take us to him."
"The King is performing a yagna. I have been ordered to permit only old learned Brahmans to see him," the guard refused. 
"To possess true knowledge one need not be grey-haired or bald. It is wrong to judge a man's merit by his age. I may be young but I have observed the sacred vows and have mastered the Vedas," Ashtavakra argued eloquently. 

The king's ministers overheard and were impressed. They brought the boys to King Janaka. 
"O King, I have heard of Bandhi's challenge and the fate of those who failed to meet it. I have come to challenge Bandhi and to see him destroyed," Ashtavakra declared. 
Janaka was astonished. "You speak of defeating Bandhi because you are ignorant of his skill in debate. No one has been able to defeat him."
When Janaka saw that Ashtavakra was adamant, he decided to test him. "What is it that does not move when born?"
"An egg," Ashtavakra answered instantly. 
"Which animal does not close its eyes while it is asleep?"
"The fish."
The king asked many more questions and was impressed by the bold prompt answers. "Let the boy meet Bandhi. Request Bandhi to present himself," the King ordered. 

Bandhi came and the debate began. But in no time Ashtavakra had him in a tight corner and Bandhi had to admit defeat. 
"Bandhi has met his match. Let Ashtavakra decide his fate," Janaka declared. 
"Bandhi had those whom he defeated thrown into the river. Let him meet with the same fate today. Drown him!" Ashtavakra demanded. 

Bandhi stepped forward. "O King! I am the son of Varuna, Lord of the Hydrosphere. The sages I defeated are not dead. My father was performing a yagna for which he needed sages from the earth. The challenge was a means of sending them to him. The yagna must be over by now and they should return. And by Ashtavakra's grace I can go back to my father. Let us go to the river."

At the river, one by one, the sages emerged from the water. As Kahoda came out, Ashtavakra fell at his feet. 
"Ashtavakra, my son! My blessings on you," Kahoda smiled. He turned to the crowd. "We regard Ashtavakra as the most brilliant scholar among us. Sons have often been known to excel fathers in achievements."
Then as they watched, Bandhi jumped into the water and vanished. 

Kahoda, Ashtavakra and Shwetaketu returned to the hermitage. As soon as they arrived there, Kahoda said, "Ashtavakra, take a quick plunge into the river flowing near by and come out."
Ashtavakra did as he was told. A second later, as he emerged from the water, a miracle occurred. 
"Mother! Father! Look! My body! It's no longer deformed!" he shouted joyfully. His twisted limbs had completely straightened out. 
Uddalaka rejoiced to see his grandson's body straight and tall. The hermitage once again became the haven of peace it used to be.
