# The Pandit and The Milkmaid - 1. The Pandit and The Milkmaid

## The Pandit and The Milkmaid
There was once a highly respected, learned Pandit who lived on the banks of a river. Scholars came from far and near to consult him and praise his knowledge. 

On the other side of the river lived a simple milkmaid named Lakshmi, who sold milk to the Pandit. She had a very busy day: waking early to bathe and milk her cows, cooking for her old father, and then catching the boat across the river to deliver the milk. 

One day, the Pandit complained, "I want the milk much earlier from now on, my child. See to it that you come before sunrise tomorrow."

The next morning, Lakshmi reached the riverbank before dawn, but the boatman was nowhere to be seen. She had to wait for him. By the time she finally reached the Pandit's house, the sun was already up. 

The proud Pandit was furious. "Don't give me excuses! How dare you disregard my wishes! Don't you know who I am? Do you know how much I have read?" he shouted. "People cross the ocean of life just by chanting the name of Hari (God). And you can't manage even a small river!"

"Please forgive me, Panditji," Lakshmi apologized. She took his angry words very, very seriously. "I wish Panditji had told me this earlier," she thought innocently. 

The next day, Lakshmi arrived well before sunrise. 
"Good!" the Pandit said. "But... I see no boat! How did you come?"
"The way you told me to, Panditji," Lakshmi smiled. "I crossed over by chanting the name of Hari. And see, here I am!"

"Impossible! You are lying!" the Pandit gasped. 
"Oh no, Panditji. It's easy. Shall I show you how?"

Lakshmi led the Pandit down to the river. With her hands joined in prayer, she chanted, "Hari, Hari, Hari..." and miraculously walked right across the surface of the water! 

"See how easy it is! Now you come. Come, Panditji!" she called from the water. 

The Pandit, amazed and eager to show his own greatness, stepped toward the river. "M... M... Me? My new silk dhoti... I must be careful," he muttered, lifting his clothes high above his knees. 

"Yes, yes... Hari, Hari... I will hold it as high as I can..." he mumbled as he stepped onto the water. 

Instantly, he fell into the river with a huge splash! "OOOOOOH...!"

Lakshmi laughed gently from the bank. "Oh Panditji, you were not thinking of Hari at all! You were thinking of your dhoti. That's why you fell in."

The soaking wet Pandit finally realized his folly. "Lakshmi, I was so proud of my learning! But now I know that it is pure and simple faith like yours that really counts. God bless you, little Lakshmi!"

## The Cobra and the Monk
Near a lush meadow stood a large banyan tree, home to a deadly, wicked cobra. The village cowherds were terrified of it. 

One day, a holy monk passed by. The boys warned him about the dangerous snake, but the monk calmly walked up to the tree. When the cobra sprang forward, hissing with its hood puffed out, the monk gently chanted a mantra. Instantly, the snake lowered its hood and lay as harmless as an earthworm at his feet. 

"O cobra, why do you lead a life of violence?" the monk asked. "Would you not like to learn to love God?"
"I would, holy monk," the snake replied, "but I don't know anything about God."

The monk taught the cobra a holy word and instructed it to repeat it daily and never harm anyone. The cobra became his devoted disciple. 

Days passed. The meditating cobra forgot the world around him. The cowherds, noticing the snake lying peacefully, thought it was dead or sick. They began throwing stones at it. When it still didn't react, one boy boldly picked the cobra up by its tail, whirled it around, and smashed it against the ground. 

The poor snake was left completely senseless and badly injured. 

It dragged itself to its hole and survived by eating only dry leaves and berries, refusing to bite even the rats. 

Much later, the monk returned. He was shocked to find the once-mighty cobra thin, gaunt, and covered in scars. 

"O Guru, you asked me not to harm anyone," the snake explained weakly. "So I did not defend myself against the boys."

"I am glad that you have conquered anger," the monk sighed, shaking his head. "I asked you not to bite, but surely I didn't ask you not to hiss!"

## The Great Saint
One night, a truly great saint lay completely still in a deep spiritual trance on the side of a dusty road. 

A thief came running by. Seeing the man, the thief thought, "He must be a thief who robbed a house and is now sleeping here through sheer exhaustion! The police may arrive any moment. I better run!" And the thief fled. 

Later, a drunkard came staggering along. Seeing the saint on the ground, the drunkard laughed, "Aha! My friend, I think you have had a sip too much and fallen into this ditch! But I can walk far more steadily than you!" And he staggered on his way. 

Finally, another holy sage came walking down the road. Seeing the serene face and hands joined in prayer, the sage immediately realized, "Surely, this is some noble saint, lost in meditation." He gently pressed the saint's feet in reverence. "You lie on a dusty roadside, but your heart is pure and full of love for God. You are indeed a great saint!"

And so was he, for we see in others exactly what we are ourselves.

## The Two Frogs
There was once a frog who lived his entire life in a small well and knew nothing of the outside world. 

One day, a frog from the river fell into the well. They began talking. 

"Where have you come from?" asked the well-frog.
"From the river, my friend."
"The river? What's that? Is it very big?"
"Oh, no! It's much, much bigger!" the river-frog explained. 

The well-frog took a small leap across the water. "Is it as big... as this?"
"No, much bigger!"
The well-frog took a huge leap from one side of the well to the other. "Then, is it as big... as this well of mine?"

"HA, HA, HA!" the river-frog laughed out loud. "My friend, how can you compare the river with this well?"

The well-frog was furious. "What do you mean! There can never be anything bigger than my well. You are either ignorant or a liar! Get out of my well, at once!"

"With pleasure, my friend," the river-frog replied, climbing out. "Who wants to sit in a well and know nothing of the world outside!"

## The Seven Jars of Gold
A poor but cheerful barber lived a happy, care-free life with his wife and children. One day, the King was so pleased by the barber's merry talk and excellent service that he employed him permanently, offering a generous salary of a bag of gold coins every month. 

The barber was overjoyed. But one dark night, as he passed through a forest, a mysterious voice called out. It was a *Yaksha* (a tree spirit). 

"I want to give you seven jars of gold. Will you accept them?" the Yaksha asked. 
"Yes! Yes! Willingly!" the barber cried. 
"Then go home. You'll find them there."

The barber ran home and found seven large clay jars. Six were filled to the brim with glittering gold coins. But the seventh jar was only half-full! 

Obsessed with filling the seventh jar, the barber's life changed. He emptied all his wife's household money into it, but the level didn't rise. He emptied all their life savings into it, but it remained half-full. 

He stopped buying proper food for his hungry children. He borrowed heavily from his friends. He begged the King for a massive raise, which the King kindly granted. He even sold all his wife's jewelry. 

Everything went into the seventh jar. But the mysterious jar swallowed it all, and the level of gold remained exactly the same! 

The barber became a miserable, exhausted, perpetually frowning man. Seeing this change, the wise King asked him, "My good man, have you by any chance got the seven jars?"

"How do you know that, Maharaj?" the shocked barber asked. 
"Because I was offered them too," the King explained. "But I realized that the Yaksha is the cunning tempter of men. The seventh jar is the jar of unquenchable desire! Its thirst for gold can never be filled."

Realizing what a greedy fool he had been, the barber ran to the forest and begged the Yaksha to take the jars back. When he returned home, the jars were gone, taking all his life savings with them. 

But the barber smiled for the first time in months. "Never mind. I am still hale and hearty. We can make a fresh start. Truly, there is nothing better than a simple, happy life!"

## The Man Who Wanted to Dig a Well
A man once set out to dig a well. After digging down fifteen cubits with no sign of water, a passing stranger told him he was digging in the wrong spot and pointed him to a "better" place. 

The man moved and dug twenty cubits deep at the new spot. Still no water. Another passerby claimed to know the "best" spot. The exhausted man moved again and dug thirty cubits deep. Nothing but dust! 

Sick of the whole business, he complained to a friend that he had dug eighty-five cubits in total and found no water. 

"If you had sunk those eighty-five cubits in one place," his friend pointed out, "you would have struck water anywhere in this area, even below the rocky surface!"

The man learned his lesson: focus your efforts on one spot, no matter what anyone else says.

## The Rich Man's Feasts
There was once a rich man who was considered a great, pious devotee of the Goddess Durga. Every year during Durga Puja, he threw incredibly lavish and expensive feasts for the Goddess, sparing no expense on bags of sugar and pots of rich ghee. The whole village admired his devotion. 

Many years passed, and the man grew old. One year, to the village's surprise, he did not celebrate the feast at all. 

When the villagers asked him why he stopped his grand celebrations for the Goddess, the old man replied, "I have lost all my teeth and my digestion is poor. I can no longer eat rich food."

The villagers realized the truth: he had only ever thrown those rich feasts because *he* liked eating the rich food, not out of any true devotion to the Goddess!
