# The Mouse Merchant - 1. The Mouse Merchant

In the city of Varanasi, a young man was desperately looking for a job. He happened to overhear the royal treasurer speaking to a friend as they walked down the street. 

The friend asked the treasurer for the secret to his success. "Initiative and enterprise," the treasurer replied, pointing to a dead mouse lying in the street. "Even if he has no money, a young man with initiative could just pick up that mouse and start a business." 

The young man took the treasurer's words to heart. "He must surely know what he is talking about!" he reasoned. He picked up the dead mouse and sold it to a nearby shopkeeper for his cat, earning a single *paisa* (copper coin). 

With his one coin, he bought some *gur* (jaggery). The next morning, he carried a pot of drinking water and the *gur* to the outskirts of the city and waited for the flower-gatherers returning from the forest. Hot and thirsty, they gladly drank his water and ate the *gur*, giving him bunches of flowers in return. 

He sold the flowers at the city temple, earning more money. He reinvested his profits, buying a bigger water pot and more *gur* to serve the grass-cutters in the fields. Slowly, his wealth began to grow. 

One evening, a massive storm littered the palace gardens with fallen branches. The young man offered to clear the mess for the worried gardener, provided he could keep the wood. He then cleverly "hired" a group of local children to do the work by offering them delicious *gur* as a reward. He sold the large pile of wood to a potter who urgently needed it to fire the king's pots, earning sixteen copper pieces. 

With his growing capital, he learned that a horse-dealer was bringing five hundred horses to the city. He immediately paid the grass-cutters to supply him with five hundred bundles of grass and made them promise not to sell to anyone else that day. When the horse-dealer arrived, he could only buy grass from the young man, paying him a handsome one thousand copper pieces. 

Now a wealthy man, he heard a rumour that merchant boats were arriving the next day. He hired a luxurious carriage, rode to the harbour, and approached the visiting merchant first. He negotiated to buy the merchant's entire cargo, giving his signet ring as an advance. 

When the city's hundred regular merchants arrived at dawn, they found everything had been sold to the young man. To save their trade, they each had to buy a share of the merchandise from him for a thousand gold pieces! 

Having made a vast fortune in just four short months from a single dead mouse, the young man returned to the royal treasurer with half his wealth as *guru-dakshina* (a gift to a teacher). 

Impressed by the young man's incredible enterprise and initiative, the treasurer married him to his lovely daughter and gave him all his family estates.


# The Mouse Merchant - 2. The Invaluable Treasure

In the holy city of Varanasi, there once lived a hardworking water-carrier. He earned barely enough for two meals a day. 

However, one lucky day, he managed to earn a tiny bit of extra money: exactly one *paisa* (a copper coin). 

Thrilled to have some savings, he carefully hid the single coin in a loose brick in the northern wall of the city. "It's the first brick to the left of the northern gate, and the tenth from the ground level. Easy to remember," he thought proudly. "Ah! My treasure is safe there. I am a rich man!"

Years passed. The water-carrier married a woman who shared his trade, and they built a small hut near the southern gate of the city. 

One hot summer afternoon, his wife sighed, "I wish we could go to town this evening to attend the fair! But we don't have enough money."

"Of course we do!" the water-carrier declared proudly. "I have my hidden treasure— one whole paisa! I will go now and bring it to you."

The midday sun was blazing like a furnace, but the water-carrier ran happily through the deserted streets, singing a joyful song. 

King Udaya, who was relaxing on his shaded palace balcony, saw the man running in the extreme heat and ordered his guards to bring him up. 

"The earth is like a furnace. Yet you run and sing. Don't your feet burn?" the King asked in amazement. 
"It is my desire that burns me, not the heat, O King," the man replied cheerfully. "My desire is to retrieve my treasure hidden in the northern wall to make my wife happy."

The King was intrigued. "Does your treasure consist of a hundred thousand gold coins?" 
"No, not that much," the man laughed. 
"Fifty-thousand? Ten-thousand?" 
"No! It is exactly one paisa, my King."

The King laughed out loud. "My friend, you don't have to run all the way to the northern gate in this heat for one paisa. I will give it to you. Here, take this and go home."

"Thank you, I will take your coin," the water-carrier said. "But I will still go and get my hidden coin, too."

The King, amused by the man's stubborn attachment to his tiny savings, began to raise his offer. "I will give you ten paisas to go home... a hundred... a thousand... ten thousand!"

To every offer, the man replied, "Thank you, I will take your money— and the one I have hidden, too!"

Desperate to see how far the man would go, the King finally declared, "I will give you half my entire kingdom if only you will agree to drop the idea of running now for that one paisa!"

"I AGREE!" the water-carrier beamed. 

The King kept his word and held a grand *darbar* (court) to crown the water-carrier as king of one half of the kingdom. 

"Now, friend," the King asked, "tell me which half of the kingdom you choose to have."

The clever water-carrier thought for a second and smiled. "I want the northern half of the kingdom."

The King burst into laughter. "You've won again!" By choosing the northern half, the clever water-carrier not only got half the kingdom, but also officially reclaimed his "invaluable treasure" hidden in the northern wall!


# The Mouse Merchant - 3. The Right Moment

King Suvarnaka was a famously generous monarch. It was said that no supplicant ever left his court without receiving a rich gift from him. 

Except for one man: a pandit named Yashovarman. 

Yashovarman came to the court every single day, asking for alms. And every single day, the King would point up at the bright sun and refuse him. 

"I do wish to give you gifts," the King would say slyly. "But the sun god prevents me from doing so. As long as he is watching, I cannot gratify your wish." 

In truth, the King had a rule: "Only two categories of people deserve gifts— the old and infirm, or the truly wise pandits. Yashovarman is certainly not infirm. So let me see if he has his wits about him to solve my riddle."

Each day, the King would point at the sun, and the defeated Yashovarman would leave empty-handed and disappointed. 

But Yashovarman was indeed a wise pandit. He waited patiently for the right moment. 

One day, he returned to the court. "Maharaj, I have been seeking alms of you for a long time now," he said. 

"I know," the King began his usual routine. "But the sun god..." 

The King pointed up at the sky, but stopped in shock. "OH!" 

There was a total solar eclipse! The sun had completely vanished from the sky, swallowed by darkness. 

Yashovarman smiled a wide, clever smile. "Whatever you wish to give, please give now, Maharaj... when my enemy is still in the grip of his enemy!" (According to ancient Puranas, a solar eclipse occurs when the sun is swallowed by the powerful demons Rahu or Ketu).

The King burst into loud, delighted laughter. "HA! HA!" Yashovarman had finally proven his sharp wits. 

Impressed by the clever timing, the King immediately removed his heavy gold necklace and happily presented it to the wise pandit. 

"Glory be to you, Maharaj," Yashovarman thanked him, finally receiving his long-awaited gift.
