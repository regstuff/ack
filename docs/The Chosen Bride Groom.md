# The Chosen Bride Groom - 2. The Gods as Witnesses

Upakosha, the most beautiful maiden in Pataliputra, was happily betrothed to the handsome Vararuchi. 

When Vararuchi was about to leave town, he told her, "Upakosha, I must go to the Himalayas to propitiate Lord Shiva. I may be away for long. So I've left my wealth with the merchant Hiranyagupta. You may ask him whenever you are in need."

"I'll wait anxiously for your return," she promised. 

As the days passed, Upakosha grew thin and wan. Listlessly she went about her daily chores. 

One day as she was returning home after her evening bath, the minister at the royal court saw her. "Oh, how beautiful she looks!" he gasped. 

Emboldened by her beauty, he accosted her. "Marry me, O beautiful one," he demanded. 

"But I'm betrothed to Vararuchi..." she protested. 

When he persisted, Upakosha quickly thought of a clever plan. 

"Do not approach me thus in public, sir, lest misfortune befall both of us," she whispered. "Come to my house at dusk on the night of Vasant Panchami next week."

"Ah, yes! All the town people will be busy celebrating and will not observe me coming to your house," the minister grinned. 

But she had hardly gone a little further when— 

"Oh, the king's treasurer!" she gasped. 

"All the jewels in the royal treasury seem pale before your beauty. Oh fair one, be mine," the treasurer begged. 

"How can I accept your proposal here? It would not be proper," she smiled. "Come to my house at midnight on the day of the spring festival."

"Indeed I will!" he agreed happily. 

As she was about to reach her house she met the head magistrate, who also detained her with his proposal. 

"Come to my house at dawn after the spring festival," she whispered. 

*I can no longer live here. I'd better leave the town,* she worried at home. *But I need money for the travel expenses.*

She went to Hiranyagupta, the merchant. "I need some money urgently. Vararuchi had told me, he'd deposited his wealth with you."

"With me? Of course not!" the merchant lied. "You say so because Vararuchi did not care to leave a written record. This isn't fair, sir."

"Listen, pretty one, if you consent to be my wife, I'll bestow all my wealth on you. What Vararuchi left with me is nothing in comparison," the wicked merchant grinned. 

"That means you admit Vararuchi left his money with you," she pointed out. 

"I admit nothing. Can you produce a witness to say that I have his money? My proposal is better," he sneered. 

"Hmm. Let me think about it. I'll give my reply after the spring festival," she said calmly. 

Upakosha now began to plan for Vasant Panchami. "Prepare a large vat of lampblack mixed with oil," she told her maids. "Now add this musk and sandal to perfume it."

"Yes! Keep four pieces of cloth dipped in it for applying it to my special guests," she ordered. "Now where's that huge ancestral trunk?"

"In the store room," her maids replied. The enormous trunk was dragged out from the store room. "Empty it out. Check the latch and the lock properly."

Upakosha confided her plan to her maids. As dusk fell— 

"There he comes! The minister! Do as I've told you to," she ordered. 

The minister was received by Upakosha's maids. "Welcome, sir! Our mistress bade us to have you bathed and dressed in new clothes to celebrate the spring festival."

Elated at being thus welcomed, the minister agreed. He was led into a dimly lit inner chamber. 

"Here's a special unguent we've made for you," the maids said, anointing him with the lampblack which he could smell but not see. 

Just then— *KNOCK KNOCK*

"It is the royal treasurer!" a maid whispered loudly. 
"Oh! He is Vararuchi's friend. Hide! Hide!" Upakosha cried. 
"Where?" the panicked minister asked. 
"In this trunk!"

The minister hid in the trunk. The treasurer entered. "Now the next one! The treasurer," Upakosha told her maids. 

They brought the treasurer inside the dimly lit room. "Oh sir! You must be weary from your royal duties. Our mistress has made this special unguent to give you a soothing massage," they told him. 

Unsuspecting, the treasurer succumbed to the massage. "Good! He's all covered with lampblack," the maids giggled. "No one would recognize him now."

Just then— "The head magistrate is here."

"Oh! I'm done for!" the treasurer gasped. 
"Don't worry! We'll hide you. Get in here, quick!" they told him, shoving him into the trunk with the minister. 

Soon it was the magistrate's turn to enjoy the delightful massage. Meanwhile—

"Send word to Hiranyagupta, the merchant, that I'm ready to reply to him," Upakosha ordered. 

Eager to hear her reply, Hiranyagupta hurried to her house. "Will you consent to be mine?" he asked. 

"Yes-yes I might..." Upakosha smiled. "But do you still deny that Vararuchi deposited his money for me with you?"

Hiranyagupta felt safe now. *There's no one to witness what I say. Heh heh!*

"Of course. Vararuchi did leave his wealth with me and asked me to give it when you needed it," he admitted. 

Upakosha raised her hands in mock plea. "Hear, ye gods, and bear witness to these words of the merchant."

"So you agree to be mine," Hiranyagupta smiled. 
"Have a little patience. You'll see me very soon, I promise you," she bade him go. 

Upakosha, accompanied by her maids, went to the court of King Nanda. 

"Your Majesty! The merchant Hiranyagupta is trying to cheat me. Is it true that Vararuchi left his wealth with you to be given to this lady?" she asked. 

The wicked Hiranyagupta pretended ignorance. "No, your majesty, I have nothing in my keeping belonging to this lady."

"Oh! Your majesty, I have witnesses. Before he left, Vararuchi had put the household deities in a box. This merchant admitted the deposit in their presence. You can ask the gods yourself."

"Let the box be brought to the court," the King ordered. 

"It is a rather heavy box. It may need seven or eight men to lift it," Upakosha smiled. 

The heavy trunk was brought to the court by several of the king's men. Dramatically raising her hands skywards Upakosha said— "Relate truly, O gods, what the merchant said."

No sound came from the trunk. "Speak up, O lords, who dwell inside the trunk. Else I'll burn you or open the box in the court," she threatened. 

Suddenly— "IT IS TRUE THAT THE MERCHANT ADMITTED THE DEPOSIT IN OUR PRESENCE. YES! YES, WE CAN VOUCH FOR IT. INDEED HE HAS ADMITTED TO VARARUCHI HAVING DEPOSITED HIS WEALTH WITH HIM."

Hiranyagupta was beside himself with fear— *Oh dear! Her gods are really powerful and protective towards her.*

Quickly he bent before the king— "Forgive me, your majesty. I did indeed accept the money from Vararuchi..."

"Go at once and get her money," the angry King ordered. But King Nanda's curiosity was aroused. 

"Can I have a look at your household deities?" he asked Upakosha. 
"How can I refuse a royal request, your highness?" she smiled. 

They opened the trunk. "Wh-what on earth? Who are these? They look almost human," the King gasped, seeing the three men covered in lampblack. 

"They look familiar. Let them be brought out from the trunk," the King ordered. 

"Oh! It's my treasurer," the King gasped. "See the minister! And the magistrate too!"

"What a sight!" "Ha! Ha!" the court laughed. 

"What is the meaning of all this?" the King asked. 

Upakosha related everything. "Oh, what a tale! Indeed a good woman is protected by her own virtue and wit," the King smiled. "Henceforth, you will be my sister. I'm sure Vararuchi will be proud of you."

When Upakosha returned home laden with presents given by King Nanda, she was greeted with smiles of admiration from the citizens of Pataliputra.
