# The Cowherd of Alawi - 1. The Cowherd of Alawi

It was the eve of the day Buddha and his monks were to pass through the village of Alawi. A poor cowherd of the village was out searching for one of his cows that had strayed from the herd. 

All through the long night he searched the dark forest but could not find her. Soon it was morning. The cowherd was in a quandary. 

"What shall I do? Unless I turn back now... I will not be able to listen to the words of the enlightened one," he thought sadly. "And yet... until Gowri is safe in her shed, I will not be able to pay attention to his words."

"Gowri will have to be found before I go to the teacher," he decided. 

Meanwhile, Buddha came to the village with his disciples. The villagers warmly welcomed them and served them food. Later, they assembled under a large tree to hear Buddha speak. 

"I will begin only when the man for whom I have come is here," Buddha told the waiting crowd. 

"But all of us are present," a villager said. 
"No, not all. That cowherd is not yet here," another villager pointed out. 

So Buddha patiently waited. 

The cowherd, meanwhile, had still not found his cow. "She couldn't have strayed this far. Yet..."

He climbed up a high rock. "Gowri! Oh, Gowri!" he called. He finally spotted her far away. He ran to her. 

"Do you know what you have done? You have robbed me of the opportunity of listening to the words of the enlightened one," he scolded the cow gently. "He must have ended his discourse by now. But I could at least pay my respects to him. Come, let's hurry home."

As soon as they reached the village, the cowherd left the cow in her shed... and without waiting to have even a cup of water, went straight to the meeting. 

"Ah! The teacher is still here. It was a good thing I did not stop to eat something before coming," he thought happily. "Oh! If only I didn't feel so hungry. Well..."

He approached the Buddha and bowed. Buddha looked at him kindly. "Is there any food left?" Buddha asked the villagers. 

"There is, sir," a villager replied. 
"Then please serve this man some," Buddha said. "Please wait."

"Come with me," a monk told the cowherd. 

Only when he began eating did the cowherd realize how incredibly hungry and thirsty he was. He drank deeply from a pot and finished his meal. He washed his hands... and went back to the meeting feeling refreshed. 

As soon as he was seated, Buddha began to speak. The cowherd heard him in rapt attention, drinking in every word of wisdom. 

When the discourse was over, the cowherd approached the teacher. "Master... admit me to the Sangha."

"I will, my child," Buddha smiled. 

As Buddha and his monks took leave of the villagers and walked on— 

"The teacher never asks whether we have had food or not," a monk whispered to another. "But the fuss he made of that cowherd."

Buddha heard them. "I went to Alawi only to bring the cowherd into our fold," he told his monks gently. "When I saw him, I knew he was in no frame of mind to be receptive to my teaching."

"You cannot teach a person who is troubled by hunger," Buddha explained. 

The monks understood the profound wisdom of his words, and they walked on in peace.

# The Cowherd of Alawi - 3. The Witness

Dhananjaya, a wealthy citizen of Shravasti, lay fast asleep in his home. 

Late that night, burglars broke into his house through a tunnel they had dug. After stealing what valuables they could lay their hands on, they left through the same tunnel. 

As soon as they clambered out at the other end, they saw the Kotwal approaching. 

"Jump into that drain. Quick!" the leader whispered. The thieves ducked into the drain. 

They quickly shared out the booty, tied their bags to their waists, and dispersed into the night. But as one of the thieves ran across a paddy field, his bag fell off. In his hurry to get away, he did not notice it. 

Meanwhile, outside Dhananjaya's house, the owner awoke. 

"Thief... thief... I've been robbed!" Dhananjaya yelled. 

The Kotwal came running. "When were you robbed?"
"Barely a few minutes back," Dhananjaya cried. 

"But I did not see anyone about," the Kotwal frowned. "Ah! A drain! The thief must have seen me and ducked into this drain! I'll go after him."

And the Kotwal lowered himself into the dark drain. 

Sometime later, a farmer set out for his field in the early morning light. Just as he entered it, he saw Buddha and his disciples. He approached Buddha and respectfully saluted him. 

As Buddha walked away, he pointed to the ground. "A poisonous snake! Do you see it, Ananda?"

"I do, revered sir," Ananda replied. "A deadly snake!"

The farmer, hearing this, was alarmed. *A snake in my field! I must get rid of it,* he thought. 

The farmer ran to the spot Buddha had pointed out. "I see no snake here!" he muttered. He poked the grass with his stick. "A bag!"

He picked it up. "The teacher must have mistaken it for a snake! It's heavy! I wonder what it contains."

Just then the Kotwal, who had been following the thief's trail, appeared on the scene. 

"What's that in your hand? I'd like to know what's in this bag," the Kotwal demanded, snatching the bag. 

He opened it. "Gold! Just as I suspected! You thief!"

"I... I only..." the farmer stammered in shock. 

"Not a word from you! You can do all the explaining you want to the king," the Kotwal declared, arresting the poor man. 

But the king did not give him a chance to explain either. 

"You shall be put to death for your crime," the king ordered sternly. 

As the farmer was being led away to be executed, he kept muttering something to himself. 

"A poisonous snake! Do you see it, Ananda? I do, revered sir. A deadly snake!" the farmer repeated over and over. 

The guards stopped. "Why are you repeating their words?" a guard asked. "Are you referring to the elder Ananda and the revered teacher?"

"Yes," the farmer replied. "I will explain this only to the king."

So the farmer was taken to the king once again. 

"Why did you repeat the words of the teacher and the elder Ananda?" the king asked in surprise. 

"Because, I am not a thief, and... this man has named Buddha himself as his witness," the farmer explained the events of the morning. 

The king took the farmer to Buddha. 

"Sir, did you visit this man's field in the morning?" the king asked. 
"Yes!" Buddha replied calmly. 
"When you saw it, what did you say?"
"I said: A poisonous snake. Do you see it, Ananda?" Buddha confirmed. "And Ananda replied: I do, revered sir. A deadly snake!"

The king turned to his guards. "So, what this farmer says is true! Yes, it is."

"By repeating your words he has saved his life..." the King bowed to Buddha. "...And he has saved me too... from the sin of shedding an innocent's blood."

"The teacher knew that the bag contained gold and that sooner or later the farmer would find it, and would find himself in trouble," the king realized. "That's why he went to his field in the morning."

"Revered teacher, I am grateful to you for saving the farmer and for saving me," the King said humbly. 

Later the real culprits were found and suitably punished.


# The Cowherd of Alawi - 4. The Three Editions of the Sutras

When the holy sutras were only available in Chinese, Tetsugen, a young Japanese Zen devotee, decided to publish them in Japanese. 

"I will need a great deal of money for the work. I must travel and collect as much as I can," he decided. 

He wandered from village to village accepting whatever the people gave him. It took him ten long years to collect the vast amount he needed for the ambitious project. 

"Ah! Now I can return home and begin work," he thought happily, his sack full of money. 

On his homeward trek, he saw a ruined village. "What has caused this misery, brother?" he asked a weeping man. 

"The river Uji. Ever since she took away our crops and destroyed our homes, it has been like this," the man sobbed. 

Tetsugen dipped his hand into his heavy sack. "Here, brother, you could buy some food for the family with this," he said kindly, handing over coins. 

To another old woman, he gave more money. "Go, buy yourself some rice. You must not starve like this."

And so, in one single day, Tetsugen distributed the immense wealth that he had taken a whole decade to collect. 

*I must begin all over again,* he decided simply. 

He went back on the road, holding out his begging bowl once again. 

Many years later, when he had finally collected enough money for the second time... an epidemic broke out in the land. People were falling sick and dying in the streets. 

Tetsugen did not hesitate. He spent all his money to buy medicines and care for the sick. And once again Tetsugen distributed the amount. 

But he did not give up his noble venture. He simply began collecting money again. 

And twenty years later his lifelong dream of printing the sutras in Japanese characters finally came true. He printed the books. 

The wooden blocks, beautifully made for the purpose, can be seen to this day in the Okaku Monastery. 

"These are the blocks of Tetsugen's third edition of the sutras," a guide explains to visitors. 

"Third? What do you mean?" a visitor asks. 

To the Japanese, Tetsugen's selfless acts of freely distributing to the needy the money he had so laboriously collected, are in themselves holy sutras... and far surpass the last, his beautifully printed work.
