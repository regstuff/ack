# The Dullard - 1. The Dullard

There were once four brahmans who had grown up together in the same village. Three of them spent all their time poring over scholarly texts, but not so the fourth. 

One day, one of the scholars said, "Why don't we travel to the city? We could make use of our learning and collect a fortune."

"Not a bad idea," another agreed. "But we're not taking that dullard with us. He'd only be a burden."
"You're right," the third nodded. "He doesn't know a thing!"

The fourth friend, the dullard, was sad. "How could you be so unkind!" he cried. "He's grown up with us. We can't leave him behind. He'll share what we make," one of the scholars finally said. 

"Oh! All right! Let him come," the others agreed. And so the four set out. 

As they passed through a forest, they found a pile of bones. 

"Oh God! These seem to be the bones of a lion!" one of them exclaimed. 
"Good Lord, my guess was right! It is the skeleton of a lion," another confirmed. 

"Why don't we test our learning on these bones?" the first scholar suggested. "I can put the bones together and make a skeleton." He quickly assembled the bones. 

"I can give the skeleton muscle and skin," the second scholar boasted. He chanted a mantra, and the skeleton was covered in flesh and fur. 

"Now it's my turn. Watch this!" the third scholar declared proudly. "I can breathe life into it."

"No! No! I'm not! It's a lion," the dullard yelled in panic. "If it comes alive it will kill us all! Please don't..."

"Be quiet, you ungrateful fellow," the third scholar scolded him. "It was I who coaxed the others to let you come along. And now you want to deny me the chance to test and prove my scholarship!"

"Ignore him. What does he understand of these things?" the others sneered. 

"All right. Do as you wish," the dullard sighed, quickly climbing up a tall tree. "But let it not be said that I didn't warn you!"

The third scholar chanted his mantra. The lion immediately sprang to life with a fierce roar! 

The lion pounced on the three scholars and killed them all. The dullard watched from the safety of the tree. 

"My poor learned friends!" the dullard thought sadly. "If only you had heeded what my common sense told me."

**Moral: Mere scholarship without common sense is futile.**


# The Dullard - 2. The Greedy Barber

Manibhadra the merchant was virtuous, rich and generous. Men flocked to his home to enjoy his hospitality. 

Then one day, by making a bad investment he lost all his wealth; and consequently his friends. 

"How can I blame them? Even if they did come in, what could I offer them?" he sighed in his empty house. "My kindness? My nobility? My love? What are they worth? Nothing!"

Without money, he felt he was worth nothing. "Why should I live? I'll starve myself to death," he decided. 

As the merchant fell asleep he had an amazing dream. A monk appeared to him. "A good man like you must live. I will come to your door tomorrow in this form. Touch me on my head with a stick and I'll turn into gold; more gold than you could use in this lifetime."

The next morning, Manibhadra woke up. "What a dream that was! Will it come true?" he wondered. "Of course, not. It won't. It's because I worry day and night about money that I dreamt about it too."

Just then, there was a knock at the door. "Someone's knocking at the door! I'll open it. It must be the barber," he said. It was the barber, who got down to work. 

"Just a minute. There's another knock at the door," Manibhadra said. He opened the door and gasped. 

"Impossible! It's the very monk I saw in my dream," he thought. He invited the monk in. 

He picked up a stick and hit the monk on the head. The monk instantly turned into a pile of gold!

The barber watched in shock. "I'll give the fellow some. Let him share our good fortune," Manibhadra decided, handing the barber some gold coins. "That's for you. And don't tell a soul about what you just saw."

"I won't! You can trust me," the barber promised. But the greedy barber thought, "So these monks turn into gold when they are clouted!"

The next morning, the barber stood outside a monastery. "There! This should be good enough!" he said, holding a stout club. 

"Holy sirs, I have some excellent canvas strips to wrap manuscripts in," he told the monks. "And some money for monks who are engaged in pious works."

"Perhaps you could use the money and the canvas," he offered. 
"We certainly could, generous one," the monks agreed. 
"Then please come with me," the barber smiled slyly. 

As soon as they reached the barber's house, the monks asked, "Where is the canvas?"

"I'll first have to make the money to buy it with! And you will help me do it!" the barber yelled, locking the door and attacking them with his club. 

"Help! The fellow has gone mad!" the monks cried as he beat them. "He's a murderer!"

Their cries caught the attention of the city guards. "What's the matter? A lunatic! In there!" the guards shouted, breaking down the door. "What a waste of energy! Not one of them has turned into... gold!" the barber complained as he was arrested. 

"Let me go. Take your hands off me! What's all this?" he yelled. 
"We'd like to know. So would the city judge," the guards replied. 

Later in court, the judge asked the barber, "What harm had those poor monks done you, you wicked fellow?"
"Nothing, sir. But how else could I get rich?" he replied, describing what he had seen at Manibhadra's house. 

When Manibhadra was called to court, the judge asked him, "This barber says he saw you hitting a monk. Did you?"
"Well, I didn't hit him. But it might have appeared so to one who didn't know the whole story!" the merchant explained. 

Manibhadra then told the judge all about himself, his dream and the monk's command. 

"So that's why my monks didn't turn into gold! What a fool I have been!" the barber realized. 

"Not just a fool but a greedy fool!" the judge declared. "You shall pay for your crime by going to prison!"

**Moral: Blind imitation is a dangerous thing.**


# The Dullard - 3. The Mongoose and the Brahman's Wife

A kind brahman once found a baby mongoose whining near its dead mother. 

"You poor thing. I'll take you home with me," he said gently, picking up the small animal. 

He brought it to his wife. "My dear, you'll have to bring up this little baby, too," he smiled, as she held their own infant son. 

"Why not! I love little ones. Bring him here," she agreed happily. 

The wee, helpless mongoose soon grew into a plump, strong creature with sharp teeth. 

One day, the brahman prepared to leave the house. "I'm off to the river. See that the mongoose does not hurt our son," he warned his wife. 

"How can he hurt his own brother? You worry unnecessarily," she laughed. 

A little later, the wife decided she needed to leave as well. "It's time to go begging for alms. I'd better leave," she thought. She left the house, leaving the baby alone with the mongoose. 

As the brahman left, a dangerous snake slithered into the house. The mongoose saw it. 

"A snake! He's out to get my brother!" the mongoose realized. It attacked the snake fiercely. 

After a bloody battle, the mongoose killed the snake. "There! I've finished him. I must let mother know that my brother is safe!" the mongoose thought proudly, running outside to meet her. 

The wife returned and saw the mongoose running towards her, its mouth covered in blood. 

"BLOOD! YOU BRUTE! YOU'VE KILLED MY SON!" she screamed in horror. 

In her panic and rage, she threw her heavy water pot directly at the mongoose. "Take that, you ungrateful beast!"

The heavy pot crushed the mongoose, killing it instantly. The wife ran inside the house, weeping. "My poor baby! Why did I ever agree to..."

She stopped in shock. Her baby was sleeping peacefully in his cradle. On the floor nearby lay the dead, bloody snake. 

"Oh, no! What have I done! Why did I jump to conclusions when I saw the blood on his nose!" she cried in regret, realizing the mongoose had saved her son. 

"Ah! There she is! How proud she'll be when she learns of my bravery," the mongoose had thought. 

**Moral: Act in haste, repent at leisure.**


# The Dullard - 4. The Wheel-Bearer

Four friends once made their way to the remote hermitage of Yogi Bhairavananda. 

"Why have you come here?" the Yogi asked them. 

"We seek your help and guidance. We know that you are gifted with strange powers," they replied. "We have come out of our homes to find some gold or die in the attempt."

"We are certain that what fools call fate or luck is only the fruit of sincere effort," another added. 

"They seem to be courageous and determined. They should make good disciples," the Yogi thought. "I think I'll help them. I hope they don't have to regret it!"

He went into his hut and came out with four cotton wicks. "I'll give each of you one of these wicks," he instructed. "Take them and walk to the mountains. The moment a wick falls, dig the ground and you'll find some treasure. Collect it and go home."

The four friends bought some pickaxes and set out. "Whatever we find, we'll share equally among us," they agreed. 

As they neared the slopes of the mountains— 

"Hey! The wick slipped out of my hand!" the first friend shouted. "Quick! Let's dig up the treasure."

Keeping the wicks in their sling-bags, the other three helped him. 

"Look! Copper ore! Come all of you. Let's carry as much as we can and go home," the first friend cheered. 

"How much can it fetch us?" another asked skeptically. "Fool! It's only copper. Let's go farther."

"You may go farther if you like, I'm content with this. I'm returning home," the first friend declared, loading up his bag. 

A few days later when the second wick fell— 

"It's silver! Why go any farther? Let's take what we can and return home," the second friend suggested excitedly. 

"And not see what we might find ahead? It might be what we came out for— gold!" the third argued. "Let's go!"

"You may do as you please. I'm going home," the second friend said, collecting the silver. 

The two remaining friends trudged on till the third wick fell. 

"GOLD! We've got what we set out to get. We needn't go any farther," the third friend cheered, digging up the shiny metal. 

"But we still have one wick left," the fourth friend pointed out. "It's bound to bring us precious gems. A handful of which would fetch us more than all this gold would. Come. Follow me."

"I'll tell you what. You go ahead. I'll wait here for you," the third friend replied, satisfied with the gold. 

So the fourth friend walked on, alone. 

"It's a hot day indeed! The sun is merciless. But I must not give up," he panted, sweating profusely. "Oh, what I wouldn't give for a drop of water! Right now it seems more precious to me than the most precious of gems."

He walked on and on but the wick refused to fall. 

"I seem to have lost my way! Whatever I do, I seem to be walking in circles. Where am I?" he wondered in confusion. 

Suddenly, he saw a strange sight. "AA-A-AH!"

He approached a man standing alone. "What are you doing here with that wheel on your head? Anyway can you tell me where I could get some water?" he asked desperately. "I'm dying of thirst. Oh, if only I could be relieved of this thirst forever!"

As soon as he spoke, the heavy, spinning wheel suddenly lifted off the stranger's head and magically settled heavily onto the fourth friend's head!

"WH—WHAT'S THE MEANING OF THIS? KEEP THAT WHEEL AWAY!" the fourth friend cried in terror. 

"I can't, good sir," the stranger replied, looking relieved. "It's the work of the god of wealth— to protect his treasures from seekers like us. You will now be free from hunger, thirst, old age and death."

"But how... when do I become free of this torture?" he wept. 

"When another seeker carrying one of those wicks comes and speaks to you," the stranger answered calmly. "I don't know how long that will be. But I set out, with a magic wick, when Rama was king of Ayodhya. Greedy for something more than copper, silver or gold, I reached this place in search of gems. I saw a man with a wheel on his head and... I don't have to tell you the rest. I am grateful to you for setting me free. Farewell."

Meanwhile, the third friend waited. "Why hasn't my friend returned as yet? I'd better follow his footprints and go in search of him. He might be in trouble."

A few hours later, he found his friend with the wheel on his head. "Ah! There you are! But what are you doing to yourself? Throw off that wheel at once and come away with me," the third friend urged. 

"OOO-OH! I CAN'T... A QUIRK OF FATE... IT WAS LIKE THIS..." the fourth friend explained his terrible predicament. 

When he finished his strange tale, the third friend sighed. "What you call a quirk of fate was your own greed. We set out to find gold and we found it. But you were not content. You wanted gems. Now you will have to stay here and pay for your greed."

The third friend turned to leave. "My poor friend! Much as I hate to leave him in this plight, I have no choice. I am powerless to help him!"

**Moral: Be ambitious, but not avaricious.**


# The Dullard - 5. Ekabuddhi

Two fish named Shatabuddhi and Sahasrabuddhi, and a frog named Ekabuddhi and his wife, all lived happily together in a shallow pond. 

One evening, two fishermen happened to pass by the pond. 

"Look! Look at those beauties! Let's get to work and catch them," one fisherman pointed eagerly at the fish. 

"Not now. It's getting dark. We'll come back in the morning," the other fisherman suggested. 

The animals in the pond heard them. 

"Did you hear that? We'd better flee from here," Ekabuddhi the frog warned the fish. 

"Certainly not," Shatabuddhi the fish scoffed. "We'll stay on. Anything can happen. They might not come tomorrow."

"Suppose they do come?" Ekabuddhi asked. 

"If they do, I know a thousand tricks by which I can save all four of us," Sahasrabuddhi boasted confidently. 

"The only trick I know is to foresee danger and act accordingly," Ekabuddhi replied simply. "I am off with my wife to a safer place I know." 

And the frogs left the pond. 

The next morning, the fishermen returned with their large nets. 

"Oh! Oh! This net is impossible! If only I could get out, I would be able to do something," Shatabuddhi struggled desperately. 

"We're trapped! We're finished," Sahasrabuddhi realized in despair. 

Soon, the fishermen had caught all the fish in the pond. 

"Put this lot into the basket, we'll sell the two big fellows separately," the fisherman grinned. 

"They should fetch us a good price," his partner agreed. 

As the fishermen walked away with their heavy catch, Ekabuddhi and his wife watched safely from the bushes. 

"But for my cautious nature we too would have been in that basket," Ekabuddhi noted sadly. 

**Moral: Prevention is better than cure.**


# The Dullard - 6. The Lazy Brahman

There was once a lazy brahman who lived solely on the alms that he collected. One day a kind housewife gave him a large pot of flour, much more than he needed. 

He was very happy to have so much food. He went home and cooked a small meal. He tied the remaining flour in a bag and hung it from a hook in the ceiling to keep it safe from rats. 

He lay down on his charpoy right beneath the hanging bag of flour and began to day-dream. 

He thought, "If I save that flour till there is a famine, I can sell it for a lot of money."

"With that money I will buy a he-goat and a she-goat. They will have kids, and soon I will have a large flock of goats."

"I will sell the goats and buy a strong bull and a cow. They will have calves, and soon I will have a large herd of cattle."

"Then I will sell the cattle and buy a pair of buffaloes. When they breed, I will sell them all and buy horses. I will have many beautiful horses."

"With all the money I get from selling horses, I will buy a grand mansion. A wealthy man will offer his lovely daughter to me in marriage."

"We will have a son and I will call him Soma Sharma," he dreamed happily. 

"Soon the baby will begin to crawl. I will be worried that my horses will trample him."

"I will call out to my wife to take the baby inside. But she will be busy and won't hear me."

"I will get very angry and kick her like this!" 

The lazy brahman kicked his leg up into the air in his dream. He kicked the bag of flour hanging above his bed. The bag shattered, spilling the flour all over him and the floor. His grand dreams were ruined. 

**Moral: Don't count your chickens before they are hatched.**
