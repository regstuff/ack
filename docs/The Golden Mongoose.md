# The Golden Mongoose

The King of Hastinapura, having established his supremacy, once performed a great Ashwamedha Yagna (horse sacrifice). It was an event of unmatched grandeur. The King distributed thousands of gold coins among the priests and gave rich presents to the learned people assembled there. Even the lame, the blind, and the poor were showered with boundless charity. 

"This is the greatest Yagna any king has performed," the people murmured in awe. "He has given away three times the dakshina (fee) ordained for such a sacrifice."

Suddenly, to everyone's astonishment, a mongoose scurried into the sacrificial grounds. What was extraordinary was that exactly one-half of the creature's body shone with pure gold. The golden mongoose rolled around on the floor, stood up, and then spoke in a clear human voice!

"This sacrifice, O learned ones, is not as great as that of the Brahmana's."

The assembled priests and scholars were taken aback. "You belittle this sacrifice without an explanation," an angry priest challenged. "We are an assembly of learned men and wish to be enlightened. What was that other sacrifice, and why was it greater than this one?"

The golden mongoose began its tale. "I will tell you. In Kurukshetra, there once lived a Brahmana. He and the other members of his household—his wife, his son, and his daughter-in-law—lived a simple and austere life. The Brahmana used to eat only once a day, at noon, and his meal was frugal, consisting of only a few grains of corn."

The mongoose paused, its bright eyes scanning the wealthy assembly. "But a time came when even those few grains of corn became difficult to obtain. A great famine had descended on the land. Even the weeds had dried up. The family could find nothing to bring home for food, and the household went hungry for several days."

"Then, one morning," the mongoose continued, "the Brahmana returned with a glimmer of hope. 'I have found some barley,' he said. His family pounded the barley and divided it into four equal portions, one for each member. But just as they were about to begin their long-awaited meal, they heard a voice at the door."

"Father! A guest!" the son announced.

Even in his extreme adversity, the starving Brahmana did not forget his duties as a host. "Welcome, sir! Welcome to our home," he greeted the weary traveler. He offered water to wash the guest's feet and warmly invited him inside. 

Going to his own share of the barley, the Brahmana brought it out. "Here, sir. Forgive me, but this is all I can offer you," he said gently. The starving guest gulped down the small portion without ceremony and began to lick his fingers. 

The Brahmana thought, *He's still hungry. Now what should I do?* 

Seeing her husband's dilemma, the Brahmana's wife brought her own portion. "I know what is troubling you. Don't be worried, give him my share too."

"I cannot do that," the Brahmana refused. "You yourself have not eaten for several days, and your body is worn out with work."

"If you, who have been suffering as long as I, could give away your share, why can't I?" his wife insisted. "Please take this barley and give it to our guest." Unable to refuse her devotion, he placed her share before the guest, who devoured it just as quickly.

"But the guest was still hungry," the mongoose narrated. "Then the son came forward with his share. 'Here, father. I know you will worry yourself to death if you send that man away hungry.' The Brahmana hesitated, but the youth persisted, and his portion too was given to the guest."

"Finally," said the mongoose, "the daughter-in-law brought her share. 'Here, sir. Won't you have some more?' The guest ate the final portion."

Suddenly, a brilliant light filled the humble hut. The guest smiled, his mortal disguise melting away to reveal a radiant Deva (celestial being). 

"I am pleased with all of you," the Deva announced. "You were prepared to sacrifice even your lives for your dharma. Therefore, one who conquers hunger has won heaven for himself. Your sacrifice has impressed even the deities."

A celestial chariot descended, and the Deva took the righteous Brahmana and his family to the abode of the immortals.

The mongoose looked at the silent assembly of Hastinapura. "I was a witness to all those wondrous happenings. Later, I went up to the place where the Brahmana had stood while serving the guest. There, I saw a scattering of barley flour on the floor. I rolled over it... and to my astonishment, one half of my body became golden!"

The mongoose sighed. "Since then, I have rolled over every place where a sacrifice has been conducted... in the hope of turning the other half of my body into gold. But without success."

With a final, meaningful look at the lavish, wealth-filled sacrificial grounds, the mongoose declared, "And I despair of ever succeeding. Great indeed was the Brahmana's sacrifice!"


# The Enlightened Butcher

Kaushika was the only son of his aged parents. One day, he announced a decision that broke their hearts. 

"Mother, I wish to advance on the spiritual path," Kaushika declared. "I have decided to go off into the jungle and devote myself to the study of the Vedas."

His frail mother looked at him with tears in her eyes. "Your father and I are very old. He can hardly move, and I myself am so feeble. If you go away, what will become of us, my son? Who will fetch food and water for us? Who will attend to our needs when you are gone?"

But Kaushika, driven by his selfish spiritual ambitions, turned his back on the very mother who had given birth to him and nurtured him. Turning a deaf ear to their pleas, the young man walked off into the jungle. 

There, he practiced severe austerities and did intense penance. Seasons came and went, and Kaushika acquired great mystical powers. 

Then one afternoon, as he was meditating under a tree, a she-crane flew up and perched herself on a branch directly above him. Suddenly, a wet splat hit Kaushika's bald head. The bird had dropped its waste on him. 

"Ycch!" Kaushika yelled in disgust. Glaring up at the bird, his eyes blazed with intense, arrogant fury. "Oh, you miserable creature!"

Unable to withstand the yogi's fiery, mystical gaze, the she-crane instantly burnt and fell lifeless to the ground with a heavy thud. 

Looking at the dead bird, Kaushika's anger vanished, replaced by sudden guilt. "What have I done!" he whispered. "How could I have allowed my anger to get the better of me!"

Later in the day, he entered a nearby village to beg for food. Standing before a house, he called out, "Please give me something to eat."

The woman of the house came to the door with a vessel. "Wait just a moment, sir," she said politely. 

But just as she was cleaning the vessel to give him alms, her husband suddenly entered the house. Without a second thought, she immediately kept the vessel down and went inside to attend to her husband, serving him food and washing his feet. 

As the woman kept him waiting, Kaushika's famous anger mounted once again. *How dare she keep me waiting like this!* he fumed silently. 

When she finally returned with the food, she apologized. "I am sorry, I kept you waiting for so long. Here is some food."

Kaushika exploded. "Is this the way to treat a Brahmana? After asking me to wait, you ignore me completely. Have you no respect for a Brahmana?"

"Please forgive me," the woman replied calmly. "My first duty is to my husband. When I saw him coming home, tired and hungry, I forgot everything and everyone else. So you put your husband before me, a virtuous Brahmana?"

"Have you no fear?" Kaushika threatened. "Do you not know what power a Brahmana has?"

The woman looked him directly in the eye. "A Brahmana is one who has mastery over anger. So please do not threaten me, O learned one. I am no she-crane whom you can burn with a single glance."

Kaushika stumbled back in shock. *She knows about the crane!* he thought, bewildered. 

"O holy one," the woman continued gently. "You are a learned Brahmana, but you have not understood the truth about virtue. If you wish to be enlightened on the subject, you should look for Dharmavyadha, who resides in Mithila. He will teach you."

Humbled, Kaushika went to Mithila and made inquiries. He was directed to a bustling market. When he finally found Dharmavyadha, he was horrified. The man was sitting in a shop, hacking away at meat! 

*What would the ascetic be doing in a shop?* Kaushika thought. *It's a butcher's shop!*

As he stood there perplexed, the butcher came up to him. "Welcome, holy one. I am Dharmavyadha, the man you seek. The woman knew about the crane, and this man knows... Come, sir. Let's go to my house. This is no place for you."

Once inside the house, Kaushika couldn't contain his disgust. "Such a sinful profession does not befit you. You must be ashamed of it."

"I am not," Dharmavyadha replied calmly. "I am engaged in the family trade, and I work hard and honestly at it. There's no reason why I should be ashamed of my work. If I do injury to other creatures, so do you. There is not a man on earth who is free from the sin of doing injury to animal life. Even as you walk, you trample on numerous creatures crawling on the ground."

The butcher pointed out the window. "Would you not say that farming is an honourable profession? But doesn't the farmer destroy numerous creatures living in the soil when he ploughs the field?"

Dharmavyadha then led Kaushika to another room. "These are my aged parents," he said, bowing deeply before them. "My parents are my gods. My wife and children and I attend upon them with devotion and love. We consider caring for them to be our greatest duty. And in doing one's duty cheerfully lies true virtue. That is what the dutiful wife sent you to learn."

Dharmavyadha looked at the ascetic sternly. "You, O learned one, have run away from your responsibilities. You have deserted your aged father and mother. Who will look after them when you are gone? All one's learning and penances are useless if one has neglected one's dharma (duty)."

Tears of realization filled Kaushika's eyes. "You have shown me the path of true virtue, O pious man. I am indebted to you."

Kaushika immediately abandoned his selfish spiritual quest and returned to his village. Throwing himself at the feet of his overjoyed parents, he served them lovingly till the end of their days, having finally understood the true meaning of enlightenment.


# The Pigeon's Sacrifice

A cruel fowler once prowled the dense jungle. Catching birds had become second nature to him, and he caught them by the hundreds, selling them in the market-place to make a living. 

One evening, as he was going about his harsh business with a cage full of trapped birds, he was overtaken by a sudden and violent storm. The winds howled, and heavy rains battered the jungle. Birds and animals fled in panic, and the fowler, soaked to the bone, despaired for his life. 

"What should I do now?" he cried, running for cover. "I am so far away from the village. I'll die of cold if of nothing else."

Suddenly, amidst the blinding rain, he spotted a white pigeon struggling in a nearby bush. Even when his life was in danger, the fowler could not pass up the chance of catching another bird. He waded into the mud, snatched the helpless bird, and thrust it into his crowded cage. "It's dazed," he muttered, "but it'll survive."

Desperate for shelter, he dashed toward a massive tree nearby. "O tree, I crave your protection," he shivered, huddling under its thick branches.

He did not know it, but the very tree he sought refuge under was the home of the pigeon he had just caught. 

High above, sitting in her warm nest, the pigeon's mate looked out anxiously. "What a fearful night this is! Oh my beloved wife, where have you gone? She should have been home by now. Has some mishap befallen her?"

Down below, shivering in the fowler's cage, the female pigeon heard her husband's worried voice. "I am here, husband," she called out softly. "You... you have been caught!" her mate gasped in despair.

"Yes," she replied calmly, "but please don't hold any grudge against this man."

"Why?" the husband asked, confused. "He is our enemy!"

"Didn't you hear him ask for refuge just now?" she reminded him. "As this tree is our home, he is our guest, and we must show him all the respect due to a guest."

The male pigeon realized the truth of her words. "You are right." He flew down from the branches and landed near the fowler. "Is there anything I can do for you, sir?" the bird asked.

Startled by the talking bird, the fowler replied, "Well, you can see that I'm shivering with cold. I need a fire to warm myself."

"I'll be back soon," the pigeon promised, flying away into the storm. He returned a little later with a burning twig in his beak. He then gathered dry twigs from various hollows in the tree, made a big pile, and set it ablaze with the burning twig. 

"Now you may warm yourself, sir," the pigeon offered. 

"Ah, this is good," the fowler sighed in relief, stretching his frozen hands towards the flames. But as he thawed, his stomach began to rumble loudly. "Now if only I had something to eat."

Perched on a branch, the pigeon heard the fowler's lament. "I am sorry, there's no food in our nest," he thought, deeply troubled. "But how can I refuse food to a guest? It is my sacred duty to feed him. I would never be able to hold my head up again if I send this man away without feeding him."

Realizing there was only one thing to do, the noble pigeon made a heartbreaking decision. "It is better to die an honourable death than to live in dishonour," he concluded.

He flew down and stood before the fire. "Here is food for you, sir: eat me!" And with those words, the brave pigeon plunged straight into the roaring fire.

The fowler leaped up in shock. "What are you doing!" he cried. He stared at the burning bird, utterly horrified. "Oh, my God! He has given up his life to feed me! What a noble-hearted creature!" 

Overcome with profound remorse, the fowler buried his face in his hands. "And what a base wretch I am in comparison! I have brought misery to thousands of birds. Great is my sin! I will become an ascetic and do penance."

Tossing his nets and weapons aside, he grabbed his cage and ripped the door open. "Go! Go!" he shouted to the trapped birds. "Never will I catch birds again."

The female pigeon flew out of the cage, only to see the charred remains of her beloved mate in the fire. Tears filled her eyes. "My husband, you've died for a noble cause... but I cannot bear to live without you. Let me too share your fate." Without hesitation, she threw herself into the flames alongside him.

But the moment she entered the fire, a brilliant golden chariot descended from the heavens, drawn by divine white horses. 

"My wife," the spirit of her husband called out from the chariot, shining with celestial light. "I have come to take you with me to heaven. Come!"

And the two devoted pigeons, having fulfilled the highest dharma, were united once again in the celestial world, remembered forever for their ultimate sacrifice.
