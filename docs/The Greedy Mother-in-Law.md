# The Greedy Mother-in-Law

Kumati, the wife of the head-man of Salagram village, was a tyrant. She derived special pleasure in tormenting her simple, hardworking daughter-in-law, Saumitri. 

Saumitri slogged from morning till night, scrubbing pots, sweeping the floors, and cooking meals, but she could never please her demanding mother-in-law. 

"Hey Saumitri! Clean them well, eh!" Kumati would yell. "Come on, slow poke! Work faster!"

One morning, dressed in a fresh sari, Kumati announced, "I am going to the temple. Keep the food ready. I will eat immediately on my return. And don't waste any food."

"All right, mother," Saumitri nodded obediently and immediately began to cook. *I never remain idle,* she sighed to herself. *Still, she finds fault with everything I do.*

When the fragrant food was ready, there was a sudden knock on the door. *She is back so soon,* Saumitri thought. But when she opened the door, she saw not her mother-in-law, but a weary, hungry sadhu (holy man).

"May God bless you, my child. Will you feed a hungry sadhu?" he asked gently.

Saumitri hesitated, remembering Kumati's strict orders. But her good heart prevailed. *Feeding a sadhu is dharma, not wastage. Let me welcome him. I shall feed him my share.*

She respectfully invited the sadhu inside and served him the delicious food. "Then please have some more, sir!" she offered as he ate heartily.

Just as the sadhu was about to finish his meal, Kumati returned. "What is going on here?" she screeched, her eyes wide with fury. 

She turned her wrath on the poor holy man. "We are not running a free kitchen here. Get out, you beggar!" 

As the sadhu quietly left, Kumati rounded on Saumitri. "And didn't I tell you not to waste food?"

"I did not!" Saumitri cried defensively. "I fed him my share."

"You did not bring your share from your father's house," Kumati retorted cruelly. "Then go to your father's house and run a free kitchen. Out!" 

With a resounding *BANG!*, Kumati slammed the heavy wooden door, throwing her daughter-in-law out into the street.

Having been thrown out, Saumitri had no choice but to begin the long trek to her father's village. The path passed through a dense, dark forest. Suddenly, the sky darkened, lightning flashed with a loud *CRACK*, and a torrential storm broke out. 

Drenched and shivering, Saumitri spotted a massive, ancient tree with a large hollow in its trunk. "I will take shelter in that hollow," she decided, climbing inside and falling into an exhausted sleep.

She didn't know it, but the tree was the home of two terrifying Rakshasis (demonesses). As the storm raged, the two ugly creatures flew back to their tree. 

"This rain is not going to stop," one Rakshasi grumbled. "Let us go to Suvarna Dweep (Golden Island). You just relax. I will make the tree fly us there."

Suddenly, the massive tree uprooted itself and shot up into the stormy sky! *WHEEE!* Saumitri was jolted out of her slumber. Peeking out of the hollow, she saw the two demonesses sitting on the branches above her. "Oh God! Rakshasis! What shall I do now?" she trembled in fear, keeping herself hidden.

Soon, the flying tree landed on a beautiful island. "Here we are in Suvarna Dweep," the Rakshasis cheered, jumping off to hunt for prey. 

Saumitri heaved a sigh of relief. "Thank God! They are gone." She cautiously stepped out of the hollow onto the beach. As the morning sun broke, the sand around her sparkled brilliantly. 

"This sand glitters..." she gasped, scooping up a handful. "Oh, it is... it is sand of gold!" Quickly, she filled her small vessel with the precious golden sand and climbed back into her hiding spot.

At daybreak, the Rakshasis returned, and the magical tree flew them all the way back to the jungle near Salagram. When the coast was clear, Saumitri slipped out and hurried back to her husband's home.

"Mother-in-law will be happy to receive all this gold," she thought hopefully. 

When Saumitri showed her the glittering treasure and narrated her incredible story, Kumati's eyes widened with greed. But instead of being grateful, she complained, "You brought so little gold. It is enough to last us a lifetime, but I have got enough gold to last us for *two* generations!"

Driven by her insatiable greed, Kumati decided to replicate the journey. "I will. I shall go to the Suvarna Dweep with the Rakshasis."

"Please don't, mother," Saumitri warned. "The Rakshasis are very dangerous. They eat human flesh."

But Kumati scoffed at the warning. She marched into the forest and hid herself in the hollow of the same tamarind tree. At night, the two terrifying Rakshasis returned.

"I want to eat sea-fish tonight," one grunted.

"Then let us go to Kadal Dweep," the other agreed. 

The tree uprooted itself and shot into the sky. Hidden in the hollow, Kumati overheard them. *They are going to Kadal Dweep and not Suvarna Dweep!* she thought in outrage.

Unable to control her bossy, demanding nature, she poked her head out of the hollow. "Hey! You must go to Suvarna Dweep!" she yelled at the demonesses.

The Rakshasis jumped in surprise. "Who is that?" one hissed, reaching into the hollow and dragging the terrified Kumati out by her hair. "It must be a thief. She wants to steal gold from our island."

"No! No!" Kumati screamed.

"HE! HEE! HE! HEE!" the Rakshasis cackled wickedly. Without another thought, they tossed the screaming, greedy woman out of the flying tree, sending her plummeting through the clouds to her doom.


# Think Before You Act

On the serene banks of a large lake, a crane and a crab lived as good friends. But the crane was deeply distressed. 

One day, the crane's wife stood by their empty nest, tears in her eyes. "Friend, that sneaking thief, the snake, has eaten up our eggs again," the crane complained bitterly to the crab. "We are helpless."

"Oh, no!" the crab sympathized. "But you are not helpless. Not when you have a friend like me. You must get the snake killed. I have a plan."

The crane listened carefully as the crab unfolded his clever scheme. "I like your idea," the crane agreed. "It must work. I am sure it will."

However, when the crane explained the plan to his wife, she was not very enthusiastic. "Are you sure of the results? Is there any drawback in the plan? Please think."

"We have no time to think," the crane insisted impatiently. "We have to act now."

Ignoring his wife's caution, the crane flew to the seashore. There, he collected a dead fish left behind by the fishermen and dropped it near the burrow where a hungry mongoose lived. He then collected another dead fish and dropped it a little distance away, creating a continuous trail of fish that led straight from the mongoose's burrow to the dark hole where the wicked snake lived. 

"I hope the crab's plan will work," the crane thought, watching from a distance.

After some time, the mongoose poked his head out of his burrow, sniffing the air. "I smell fish," he muttered. 

Following his nose, he found the first dead fish. "Ah, here it is." He gobbled it down. A few steps later, he found another. "Ah, one more fish. What, another! Today is my lucky day," the mongoose rejoiced, eagerly following the tasty trail.

The mongoose followed the track of dead fish all the way across the grassy banks and reached the base of the large tree where the crane lived. Right next to the tree was the snake's hole, and the final piece of fish lay right in front of it.

"Ah, here he comes," the crane whispered from the branches above, watching the mongoose arrive.

Just then, the snake slithered out of its hole and spotted its mortal enemy. "How dare he enter my territory!" the snake hissed furiously. 

A vicious, deadly fight ensued. The two natural enemies clashed with lightning speed, biting and tearing at each other. Finally, much to the relief of the watching cranes, the snake was killed. 

"Thank God! Our eggs are safe," the crane's wife breathed a sigh of relief.

But their hope was terribly short-lived. The very next day, the hungry mongoose emerged from his burrow, expecting another easy meal. He followed the exact same track to the base of the tree. 

"What! No fish today," the disappointed mongoose grumbled. 

Just as he was about to leave, he looked up and saw the two cranes flying back to their nest high in the branches. "Let me climb this tree and try my luck," the mongoose decided, his predatory instincts taking over.

When the cranes returned to their nest, they were horrified to find the mongoose climbing down the tree, licking his chops. They rushed to their nest and found, to their absolute despair, that their newly laid eggs were missing again. 

"We got rid of one enemy only to invite another," the crane wept, realizing his terrible mistake. 

His wife looked at him sadly. "That's why we must think carefully before we act."


# The Foolish Frog King

Once, an old, weak snake came to a pond full of frogs. The pond was teeming with life, and the snake's mouth watered. 

"So many fat frogs..." he hissed to himself, "but I can't catch them. Alas! Old age has made me slow and weak... and I grow weaker day by day for lack of food. I must do something to get food."

The cunning snake came up with a deceptive plan. He slithered to the edge of the pond and lay perfectly still, pretending to be dead. 

Soon, the Frog Prince and his friends came hopping by. "Hey! The snake is not moving," the young prince noticed. "Could it be dead?"

"Let us investigate," the Frog Prince said boldly. 

"No, don't. It will eat you," his mother warned, trying to dissuade them. 

But the foolhardy Frog Prince did not listen to his mother. He confidently hopped right up to the terrifying predator and even threw a small pebble at it. "Oh! It didn't even move," he laughed, growing bolder. "Let me try again." He hopped onto the snake's tail. Still, there was no reaction. 

Finally, the snake slowly opened its eyes to a mere slit. "Whatever you do, I am not going to be angry..." it hissed mournfully in a pitiful voice. 

The frogs paused in surprise. "Frogs used to be my favourite food," the snake continued his sorrowful tale. "But once I accidentally bit the son of a powerful sage. The infuriated sage cursed me... to carry frogs on my back for the rest of my life. Then, and only then, I shall ride you."

The snake looked at the young prince humbly. "Please do, O Prince!"

Thrilled at the idea of such a grand and terrifying steed, the Frog Prince immediately climbed up the back of the snake. His friends eagerly followed. 

The snake smoothly glided across the pond. "Look, father!" the proud Prince called out to the Frog King. "I am riding a snake!"

"Oh! My brave son," the foolish Frog King swelled with pride. "Let us also ride on the snake."

Soon, the King, the Queen, the Prince, and several other frogs were joyfully riding the old snake. "Whee!! Hooray!" they cheered as the snake carried them around. 

The snake thought to itself, *I can eat them right now, but patience brings greater rewards.*

A few days later, the royal frog family was enjoying another ride. "How exciting!" they cheered. 

"Yes, but he moves too slow," the Frog Prince complained. "Can you move faster, friend?"

The cunning snake sighed heavily and stopped moving. "I can't, O King! I have not eaten for several days. I have no strength left."

"Why didn't you? The royal vehicle must be well fed," the Frog King demanded haughtily. 

"I can't eat without my master's permission," the snake replied submissively. "But I can't eat without *your* permission."

Flattered by the snake's absolute obedience, the foolish King granted his fatal consent. "Well, my faithful mount, you have my permission. But my food is frogs."

"Not royal frogs, O King! Ordinary frogs will do," the sly snake assured him. 

"Oh!" the King agreed. "I know it is a difficult decision. But kings have to make difficult decisions. I am sure you realise that if I don't eat, I will die."

"Hmm... then you will lose your unique mount..." the Frog King pondered, placing his own vanity above the lives of his subjects. "We will have to. He wants frogs to eat."

So, with the King's explicit permission, the snake started eating the ordinary frogs of the pond. Every day, the snake feasted, growing larger, faster, and stronger, while the frog population rapidly dwindled. The royal frogs, however, enjoyed increasingly fast and thrilling rides, oblivious to the tragedy unfolding around them.

Soon, the inevitable happened. "I am hungry, O King," the snake hissed one day. 

"Then eat some frogs," the King replied casually. 

"But none are left," the snake said, licking its lips. "So I am now a King without subjects. And since no more frogs are left, I will eat *you*."

Before the horrified Frog King could even react, the powerful snake struck. Without hesitation, the snake devoured the Queen, the Prince, and finally, the foolish Frog King himself, completely wiping out the entire frog kingdom.


# The Pretentious Owl

Kanakaksha, the golden-eyed owl, had an extraordinary and unusual friend in Sumitra, the elegant King of Swans. The two spent many hours talking by the serene lake where the swans lived. 

Kanakaksha, however, harbored a deep insecurity. He desperately wanted to be seen as an equal to his royal friend. "I must do something to prove that I too am a king," the pretentious owl thought to himself. "He will shun me if he knew that I am a poor owl."

One day, Kanakaksha hatched a deceptive plan. "Ah! The King of Varanasi's army is going to camp near my forest tonight," he observed. "I shall invite Sumitra and pretend that these are my men! He will be impressed with my status."

That evening, Kanakaksha flew down to the lake. "Hello, Sumitra! I want you to visit my home today. I'd love you to come some day."

"I'd love to come some day," Sumitra agreed politely. 

"Not some day, today," Kanakaksha insisted eagerly. "No buts, I visit you every day. You should visit me too."

"All right, tomorrow," the Swan King agreed reluctantly.

The next day, true to his word, Sumitra flew into the dark, dense forest to visit Kanakaksha. "Ah! Magnificent," Sumitra admired the massive ancient trees. 

"This is my domain, and these are my men," Kanakaksha boasted grandly, pointing a wing towards the large military camp set up below them. Hundreds of soldiers, horses, and tents dotted the forest floor. 

"Good. Are they proceeding on a march?" Sumitra asked, impressed by the massive army.

"No," Kanakaksha replied quickly, maintaining his lie. "But they are leaving."

"How can they leave without my orders!" Kanakaksha puffed out his chest and loudly hooted, "HO OO HO OO!" 

Down below, the superstitious human army commander heard the call. "Owl hooting! It is a bad omen. Let us postpone our departure," he ordered. 

The army immediately halted their preparations and unpacked their gear. 

Kanakaksha turned to Sumitra, beaming with misplaced pride. "There! You see my men are very obedient."

The Swan King, unaware of the superstition surrounding owls, was genuinely impressed. "Since he is my friend, I will humour him," he thought. "Yes! Yes!" he nodded politely. 

The next morning, the army commander blew his conch shell, signaling the troops to march again. Hearing the commotion, Kanakaksha confidently hooted again. "HOO HOO!"

"Drat that owl," the commander groaned. "We have to postpone our march again! I will pull up my general for indiscipline."

Sumitra, growing slightly uncomfortable with his friend's display of power, thought, "He is going too far."

But Kanakaksha was drunk on his imaginary authority. On the third day, as the army once again prepared to leave, the owl hooted loudly a third time to prevent their departure. 

This time, the commander's patience completely snapped. "Blast that owl! Will someone take care of this ill-omen maker?" he roared furiously. 

An expert archer immediately stepped forward, drew his bow, and aimed carefully into the high branches. "Ah, there he is," the archer muttered, spotting the golden-eyed owl. 

*THWACK!* 

An arrow shot through the leaves and struck Kanakaksha squarely in the chest. With a sharp cry of pain, the pretentious owl tumbled from the branch and fell dead to the forest floor. 

Sumitra, the King of Swans, looked down at his fallen friend in shock and deep sorrow. "Oh! My stupidity has cost my friend his life," he mourned, realizing too late that his polite indulgence of Kanakaksha's dangerous pretense had ultimately led to his tragic end.
