# Tiruppan

The dusty road to Srirangam, the holy city of Lord Ranganatha, was crowded with pilgrims making their way to attend the grand festival held there every year. 

By the side of the busy road, a tiny infant had been abandoned. Its piteous cries made a few pilgrims pause for a while. 

"Mother, he is beautiful! Can we take him home?" a young girl asked. 

"Hush. Don't go near him!" her mother scolded. "For all we know he may be an untouchable."

"Don't touch him!" a man agreed, and the pilgrims went on with their journey, ignoring the weeping child. 

At last, a humble sweeper from the lowest caste came by. He saw the baby and his heart melted. He picked up the child tenderly. "Poor little thing! You must be hungry. Don't cry! I will take you home."

The sweeper took the child to his home on the outskirts of the city, where people of his community lived in small huts. 

"You have been praying for a son," he smiled, placing the baby in his wife's arms. "God has sent you one. Here, take him."

The child grew up happily under the loving care of his foster-parents. They named him Tiruppan. 

"Look, Tiruppan wants to help you," his mother laughed as the little boy picked up a broom to sweep the floor. 

After a hard day's work keeping the city clean, Tiruppan's father and his relatives used to go home late in the evening. After eating a frugal meal, they would assemble together around a warm fire and sing devotional songs, while Tiruppan and the other children would dance. 

As he grew older, Tiruppan learnt to play on the *yal*, a kind of beautiful stringed instrument. Tiruppan's music was so enchanting that when he went out playing on the yal, even the high-caste Brahman boys would forget themselves and follow him around to listen. 

Tiruppan used to accompany his father, whose job it was to keep the surroundings of the grand Srirangam temple clean. 

"Father, to whom does that big beautiful house belong?" young Tiruppan asked, looking up at the towering temple *gopuram*. 

"It belongs to Lord Ranganatha," his father replied with deep reverence. 

Later, Tiruppan asked his mother, "Mother, who is Ranganatha? He has such a big house. Is he a very rich man?"

"The whole world belongs to him," she smiled gently. "He is the father of all."

*I want to see Lord Ranganatha,* Tiruppan thought to himself. *I wonder what he looks like.*

Early one morning, before anyone was awake, Tiruppan went to the temple. But just as he approached the steps, a stern priest spotted him. 

"Hey, stop! How dare you come this far!" the priest shouted, raising his stick. "Untouchable, don't you know your place? Never come here again!"

Humiliated and heartbroken, Tiruppan ran all the way back to his mother. "Mother, why can't I see the Lord? Is he not the father of all?" he wept. 

"He is," his mother sighed, wiping his tears. "But the priests won't let us see him."

Soon afterwards, tragedy struck. Tiruppan lost both his loving foster-parents to illness. But the kind people of his community in Srirangam looked after him, giving him food and shelter. 

As the years passed, Tiruppan, singing with a yal in hand and surrounded by a mesmerized crowd, became a common sight in Srirangam. 

"This young boy is mad!" some scoffed. 

"But he's harmless," others defended him. "And his songs are so moving!"

His favourite haunt was a quiet, sandy corner on the bank of the river Kaveri, from where he could clearly see Lord Ranganatha's towering temple across the water. 

"Ranganatha, although I am not allowed to see you, I can at least see your house from here," he would whisper, plucking the strings of his yal. 

Tiruppan's thoughts were constantly fixed on the Lord. *I wish to see Lord Ranganatha. Perhaps the Lord himself is longing to see me,* he mused. *These priests have made my Lord a prisoner. He can't see those whom he wants to see.*

One night, the pain of separation became too much for Tiruppan to bear. He sat on the stone steps leading down to the river, gazing at the temple silhouette in the moonlight. "I wish I were dead, Lord," he wept softly. "With death, the questions of birth and impurity cease to exist. No one can separate us then."

Exhausted by his sorrow, a blissful, deep sleep overcame him right there on the steps. 

Early the next morning, Lokasaranga Muni, a senior priest, came down to the river to fetch holy water for the temple puja. He stopped in his tracks. 

"What's this? The untouchable Tiruppan blocking the way! Get up!" he yelled. 

Tiruppan was fast asleep, and the angry words of the priest failed to wake him. 

"You insolent untouchable!" Lokasaranga screamed. In a fit of rage, the priest hit Tiruppan hard on the head with his heavy walking stick. 

Tiruppan woke up with a sharp cry of pain, blood trickling down his forehead. But the priest wasn't finished. Still smouldering with anger, he picked up a handful of stones and showered a volley of them on poor Tiruppan. 

"Ranganatha!" Tiruppan cried out, scrambling to his feet and fleeing for his life. 

Having chased away the "impure" boy and collected the water, the priest walked back towards the temple. "Ranganatha, on my account the puja will be delayed today. Pardon me, Lord," he muttered self-righteously. 

But when he reached the inner sanctum, he found the massive wooden door firmly closed. 

"Who has locked the door? Perhaps it was the chief priest," Lokasaranga thought. He banged on the heavy wood. "Open up, sir. This is Lokasaranga. I have brought water for the puja."

From the other side, the chief priest called out, "Whoever you are, open up. I am the chief priest asking you to open the door."

"Sir, I thought you were inside the sanctum!" Lokasaranga gasped. "Someone has locked the door from within."

All the priests joined together and pushed against the heavy door in an attempt to force it open. 

"Why doesn't it open? It just can't be locked from inside," one panted. 

"Maybe one of us has incurred the displeasure of the Lord and the Lord himself has closed the door," another suggested fearfully. 

The priest was right, for just then a booming, divine voice was heard from inside the locked sanctum. 

"Lokasaranga has hurt me by hurting my devotee, Tiruppan. Unless Lokasaranga carries Tiruppan on his shoulders and goes round the temple, the door will not open."

Lokasaranga fell to his knees in horror and shame. "What a sinner I am! Hurting the devotee of the Lord! Pardon me, Lord."

Without wasting a moment, Lokasaranga ran back to the riverbank and went to the bleeding Tiruppan. He fell at the young man's feet. "Tiruppan, please pardon me. Come to the temple. Lord Ranganatha is calling you."

"Has my Lord really sent for me? I am so glad," Tiruppan wept with joy. 

"Come on, Tiruppan. I will carry you there on my shoulders," the priest insisted. 

"You, a high priest carrying me, a sinner? No, you can't do this," Tiruppan protested in shock. 

"You can't say 'No' to the Lord. It is His will," Lokasaranga replied firmly, lifting the untouchable youth onto his own shoulders. 

When Lokasaranga approached the sanctum with Tiruppan riding high on his shoulders, a miracle occurred. The heavy wooden doors, which the priests could not force open, swung wide open of their own accord. 

At last, Tiruppan was face to face with his Lord. He slid down from the priest's shoulders, utterly overwhelmed by the glorious sight of the reclining deity. 

"Ranganatha!" Tiruppan cried out, his hands folded in prayer. "Having seen my transcendent God, I don't wish for anything else. I am coming to you, Lord."

"Tiruppan!" a soft voice seemed to echo in the sanctum. 

Suddenly, a brilliant flash of light left the physical body of Tiruppan as he collapsed to the floor, lifeless. The light floated gracefully and entered directly into the heart of Lord Ranganatha, merging with the divine forever. 

A magnificent temple dedicated to Tiruppan was constructed right next to the grand temple of Sri Ranganatha. To this day, Tiruppan, the humble sweeper's son, is worshipped as one of the greatest saints of devotion.


# Kanakadasa

Biregowda, a humble shepherd, rose to the respected position of headman of the village Bada near Dharwar in Karnataka by dint of his sheer hard work and honesty. 

Accompanied by his devoted wife, Bachamma, he went on a long pilgrimage to Tirupati, and finally reached the magnificent temple of Lord Venkateshwara. 

Standing before the towering idol, Bachamma prayed with all her heart. "Lord Timmappa! Bless me with a child. I crave for nothing else."

In due course, the Lord answered her prayers, and Bachamma gave birth to a beautiful boy. 

"Such a lovely child! What will you name him?" her relatives asked. 

"He was born by the grace of Lord Timmappa. We will call him Timma," Bachamma smiled happily. 

Little Timma grew up to be a strong, intelligent boy, and was always helpful to his father. He used to water the young seedlings in their fields every day. 

"Without water these seedlings can't survive, can they, Father?" Timma asked one sunny morning. 

"You are right, Timma, they can't," Biregowda nodded. 

"Then they live because of us, Father."

"There you are wrong, son. I will tell you why." 

Biregowda took his young son to the top of a nearby hill overlooking a vast, lush forest. "Father, I have never seen so many trees before. Hundreds of beautiful trees!" Timma gasped in wonder. 

"Yes, Timma. Now tell me who waters these trees? You? I? Who takes care of the trees in the forest?"

Timma fell silent, thinking hard. 

Timma's father drove home the profound truth. "Yet these trees won't perish because the kind and all-powerful God is the protector of all."

Those words took deep root in Timma's heart. Later, when Timma was just a young teenager, both his parents passed away. 

"Now I have no father, no mother. To whom shall I turn? Who will care for me?" the grieving boy wept. As Timma walked into his big and empty house, panic set in. "No one at home! I can't live alone. What will happen to me? I am so afraid..."

Then the gentle words of his father came back to him in a rush. *Who waters these trees? You? I? Who takes care of the trees in the forests? Yet these trees won't perish because the kind and all-powerful God is the protector of all.*

Timma wiped his tears and stood tall. "No, I am no longer afraid. God Keshava will protect me."

As he grew up, Timma exhibited remarkable qualities of leadership inherited from his father. And his implicit, unshakeable faith in God helped him to grow up completely fearless. 

One day, some panicked men from a neighbouring village arrived in Bada. "Timma, these people are from the next village," an elder explained. 

"What can I do for you?" Timma asked. 

"We are being menaced by a man-eating tiger. Three lives have been lost so far," they pleaded. 

"Don't worry. I will go with you at once," Timma declared. 

In the dense forest, Timma fearlessly tracked the beast. Hiding in the high branches of a tree, he waited patiently. When the massive striped beast appeared, Timma shot the tiger with a single, perfectly aimed arrow, ridding the village of the terrible menace. 

One day, he was digging in the backyard of his house when his spade struck something hard. "What's that? It looks like an iron box!"

He carefully unearthed the heavy chest. When he pried it open, his eyes went wide. "Gold!"

The neighbours came rushing to see the massive treasure. "Since you found the Kanaka (gold), we will call you Kanaka," the village elders decided happily. 

"What will you do with all this gold?" they asked him. 

"I will use it to build a temple, a house for God. It will benefit many people," Kanaka replied without hesitation. 

He immediately started the construction of a beautiful temple for Lord Keshava in the nearby village of Kaginele. 

However, hardly had the temple been completed when he was called upon to take part in a fierce battle. The headman of an envious village nearby was preparing to attack Bada. 

"Kanaka, we must get ready for the enemy," a messenger warned him. 

"This time we will give him such a beating that he won't dare to attack us again," Kanaka promised, drawing his sword. 

Led by Kanaka, the brave young men of the village rode into battle against the invader. They fought fiercely all day long. But towards the end of the bloody battle, disaster struck. 

"Ah!" Kanaka cried out in agony as a stray arrow pierced his chest. He tumbled off his white horse and hit the ground. 

By dusk, the battlefield was deserted except for the dead and the wounded. Kanaka lay bleeding heavily, his life slipping away. 

"I am... dying! This... pain is... unbearable," he gasped. "O Keshava... won't you come to my help?"

Lord Keshava responded to his desperate cry for help. A brilliant light enveloped the dying warrior, and the majestic form of Lord Keshava appeared before him. 

"Keshava, you are so kind," Kanaka wept, feeling a miraculous warmth spread through his body. "By your very presence my wounds have healed. I experience new strength, my Lord."

When he rose to his feet, perfectly healed, Kanaka made a decision that changed the course of his entire life. *God brought me back to life. Hereafter I will devote my life solely to His service.*

He approached Saint Tatacharya, a revered holy man. "Sir, I wish to dedicate my life to God. Please guide me."

"Become a dasa, a servant of God, O Kanaka. God will be pleased," the saint advised. 

Kanakadasa, as he was now called, abandoned his worldly life. He went from one village to another playing a simple stringed instrument, singing beautiful, self-composed praises of the Lord. 

One day, while walking by a river, he saw a Brahman sitting perfectly still on a rock, deep in meditation. "How serene this Brahman looks! I wish I too could meditate," Kanakadasa thought. 

When the Brahman finally opened his eyes, Kanakadasa approached him humbly. "Sir, accept me as your disciple and teach me a mantra to meditate upon."

"Brother, a mantra should be taught by an accomplished saint. Go to Saint Vyasateertha and see if he will help you," the Brahman advised kindly. 

Saint Vyasateertha was at the time supervising the construction of a massive stone water tank in his village. But the work had ground to a halt. 

"Sir, we can't remove this rock. It's so big," the desperate workers complained, pointing to a massive, stubborn boulder blocking the excavation. 

"The work has been held up," the supervisor sighed. 

"Don't worry. God will show us a way," the saint replied calmly. 

When the saint was thus preoccupied, Kanakadasa approached him with folded hands. "Master, I am Kanakadasa, a humble shepherd. Teach me a mantra upon which I can meditate."

On hearing Kanakadasa's innocent plea, Saint Vyasateertha was secretly amused. *What could a simple shepherd meditate upon—save, perhaps, such a mundane mantra as "Buffalo"?* he thought. 

"Meditate on 'Buffalo'," the saint said jokingly. 

"Thank you, sir," Kanakadasa bowed deeply, taking the instruction completely seriously. 

Sitting right there beside the massive rock, Kanakadasa closed his eyes and began to meditate with absolute, unwavering focus. "Buffalo! Buffalo!"

A crowd collected around Kanakadasa. "He is meditating on 'Buffalo'!" they laughed. "Ha, ha! Fool!"

But suddenly, the ground began to shake. A massive, terrifying black buffalo appeared out of thin air, snorting wildly! 

"It is Yama's buffalo! The steed of the God of Death!" the crowd screamed in sheer terror. "Run!"

"Here is the buffalo invoked by the mantra," Kanakadasa said calmly to the astounded saint. "What would you like him to do?"

"Get your buffalo to remove the rock which is hindering our work," Saint Vyasateertha stammered, realizing the immense power of the shepherd's pure faith. 

"O vehicle of Yama, please fulfil the wish of the master," Kanakadasa requested. 

The giant beast lowered its massive horned head and charged at the immovable boulder. *CRASH!* With a single, mighty heave, the buffalo pushed the massive rock out of the way as if it were a pebble, and then vanished. 

Some days later, the beautiful water tank was completed. "Thanks to Kanaka, the tank has been completed," the workers rejoiced. 

"Faith can move even mountains! Blessed is Kanaka," Saint Vyasateertha declared. 

One day, when Kanaka was cooking his simple meal in his hut, a stray street dog sneaked in and snatched a freshly baked *roti* (unleavened bread) from his plate. 

When Kanaka turned suddenly, the frightened dog dropped the roti in shock and ran out the door. Kanaka immediately grabbed the roti and ran out after the dog, while the people on the street watched the strange sight in bewilderment. 

"Come back and take this!" Kanaka called out to the fleeing animal. 

"Why did you run after the dog, Kanaka?" the villagers asked, bursting into laughter. "Ha, ha! Kanaka calls a dog, God. He is mad."

"God came to my house in the form of that hungry creature, and went away without accepting the roti," Kanaka explained sincerely. 

But Saint Vyasateertha understood perfectly. *Kanaka sees God everywhere and in everything. He is a true worshipper,* the saint marvelled. 

Kanaka soon became the absolute favourite disciple of Vyasateertha. But the other disciples, who were proud of their high-caste birth, soon became bitterly jealous of him. 

"He doesn't even know the scriptures," one grumbled. 

"He is of low birth—a mere shepherd!" another scoffed. 

"Yet he is our master's favourite," a third complained bitterly. 

Vyasateertha was well aware of how his proud disciples felt. He called them all together and gave them each a single, ripe banana. 

"I will show the difference between Kanaka and the others," the master thought. Aloud, he commanded, "Go to some secluded spot and eat this sacred banana, unobserved by anyone."

His disciples ran in different directions to find the most secret hiding places. One climbed high into the leafy branches of a mango tree. "I am sure, nobody can see me eating here," he thought happily, peeling his banana. 

Another burrowed deep into a massive haystack. "Ah, a good place! I will hide myself in the hay and eat!"

After eating the bananas in secret, each one came back proudly to the master. "We have obeyed your order, Master."

"Good. But where is Kanaka?" the saint asked. "Come forward, Kanaka."

Kanakadasa stepped forward, his banana still unpeeled in his hand. 

"Why haven't you eaten the banana yet?" the disciples laughed mockingly. 

"How can I eat the banana unobserved by God?" Kanaka asked softly. "He is everywhere."

The mocking laughter instantly died away. 

"You see!" Saint Vyasateertha turned to his ashamed disciples. "For Kanaka, God is not just a name to be invoked in empty prayers. He is real and is always present."

Soon after this incident, Kanaka left his Guru with his blessings and started roaming from village to village, singing about the glory of God. One day, he met a group of pilgrims who were on their way to Udupi. 

"Why just sing of Krishna! Wouldn't you like to visit his famous temple at Udupi as well?" they suggested. 

"I would like to do that," Kanaka agreed eagerly. 

But when he reached the grand temple at Udupi, his heart broke. 

"Go away," a stern priest blocked the entrance. "The sacred temple is not open to people of low caste."

"Sir, God doesn't give thought to the origin of His devotees," Kanaka pleaded reasonably. "Is not the beautiful lotus which is rooted in mire dear to Him?"

"How dare you argue with me? I will have you beaten!" the priest shouted, calling the guards who ruthlessly pushed Kanaka out into the street. 

Weeping, Kanaka went around to the back of the temple wall, where no one could see him. He sank to his knees in the dirt. "Open the door to me, Krishna! Can't you hear my pleas?" he sang, tears streaming down his face. 

Inside the temple, a strange and miraculous thing happened in the sanctum where Shri Vadiraja Teertha, the head Acharya, was worshipping the Lord. 

"What's happening?" the Acharya gasped in shock. "The Lord's image has turned around!"

The massive idol of Lord Krishna, which had faced the main doors for centuries, magically turned completely around to face the solid back wall! 

The Acharya hurried to the back of the temple. Suddenly, with a loud crack, an opening appeared in the solid stone wall, right where the idol was now facing! Through the miraculous crack, the weeping Kanaka was finally able to have a clear, breathtaking glimpse of his beloved deity. 

"Krishna, you are kind and merciful," Kanaka sobbed, folding his hands. 

"Blessed are the pure in heart!" the Acharya whispered in awe, bowing to the humble shepherd. 

Later, a beautiful, carved window was officially built exactly where the miraculous opening had appeared in the wall. To this day, thousands of devotees visiting Udupi first have a glimpse of the Lord through this special window, which has become famous throughout the world as "Kanaka's Window". 

His devotional songs, filled with pure love and wisdom, continue to be sung all over Karnataka, even though four hundred years have passed since he left this world.
