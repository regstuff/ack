# The Silent Teacher

King Brahmadatta of Varanasi had always been a loving husband, but he felt deeply concerned about his queen's happiness. For years, they had longed for a child, and at last, their prayers were to be answered. 

"They say that only a happy mother will bear a healthy baby," the King said tenderly. "Ours shall be the healthiest one ever born. I have never felt happier or healthier. But..." the Queen hesitated.

"But?" the King asked.

"I have just one longing not fulfilled. I long to get away for a while to the peace and quiet of the forest," she replied.

"What! What are you waiting for? Let's go to the forest!" the King immediately agreed. He knew she wished to have a son who would rule the world, but he understood her need for peace. 

They spent their time in the tranquil woods, absorbing the serenity of nature. The Queen felt a deep sense of calm, especially when looking at a still, beautiful lake. Soon, they returned to the city. As he was born near the tranquil waters of the lake, they named their son Abja.

Abja did everything a child was expected to do, but as he grew, he made no attempt to stand up. The nurse tried propping him against a wall, but he would simply slide down. The King called in a physician, but the prince did not respond to his treatment.

A few weeks later, the King was in for another shock. "Children of his age speak fluently, and the prince has not uttered even one word," he lamented to the Queen. 

"Aren't you disappointed that our only son is a cripple?" he asked her.

"No, I love him as he is," she smiled gently.

"I'm glad. Because I have decided that he shall succeed me... even though he is handicapped."

But the commoners and ministers had their doubts. "A crippled king, people may accept. But will they accept a dumb one?" they whispered. "Some mute people say more by their silence than we who have the power of speech," an old man noted. "But we do not understand them. That too is a handicap!"

Despite his condition, the King and Queen gave him a good education. The Queen engaged scholars to read out books of wisdom to the mute prince.

When the prince was sixteen, a celebrated physician from a far-off land came to Varanasi. The King requested him to examine their son. The physician asked the Queen, "What can one who cannot walk and cannot talk do?"

"He can talk. He speaks to me... with his eyes... and what they say fills me with peace... like the silent forest... the still lake..." the Queen replied softly.

The learned physician approached the prince. "Breathe in... hold your breath... breathe out..." he instructed, feeling his pulse. "Now answer me. What is your name? Who are you? Can't you speak?"

The prince only looked at him in serene silence. 

"Well. That's all," the physician concluded.

Later, he went to the King. "He is neither lame... nor dumb."

"Are you sure?" the King asked, astounded.

"Yes. He can hear all right... and there is nothing wrong with his organs of speech... or his spine... or his legs! The prince is only pretending to be lame and dumb."

"Then why..."

"I'll tell you why. I have seen quite a few cases of this sort. If you want to see him walk and talk... you must do as I tell you. Listen carefully..."

Following the physician's secret advice, the King hardened his heart. "That's what the physician recommended. You must not stop me. I'll be doing it for his own good," he told the Queen.

"Why are you quiet? Why don't you say something?" the King implored her.

"Because I know the peace of the mute. You wouldn't. If you understood, you would not try so hard to make our son talk," she replied wisely.

"What do you mean? I don't understand," the King said.

"To his silence. Please! Must you always talk in riddles?" he pleaded, trying to listen, but failing to grasp her profound meaning.

The following day, in the royal court, the King summoned Prince Abja. "Prince Abja, the man who sits on this throne after me has to rule a vast kingdom. Since you can neither walk nor speak, you cannot rule either." The King paused, his voice turning stern. "So I have ordered the executioners to bury you alive."

A gasp went through the court, but not a voice spoke out in protest. The dumb prince remained silent.

The executioners lifted the prince, put him on a cart, and drove away towards the forest.

On the way, a voice suddenly rang out from the cart: "Is this city of Varanasi deserted or inhabited?"

The executioners jumped in shock. "Eh?! Did you... Did you speak? It's the prince! The prince spoke!"

Overjoyed, they turned the cart around and sped back to the palace. "Maharaj, the prince spoke..." they reported, and repeated what the prince had uttered.

"My son!" the King cried, rushing to him. "I am so glad! You will speak from today, won't you?"

But when the prince made no answer, the King's brief joy turned to anger. "Take him away," he ordered. 

The reluctant executioners took the prince back towards the forest. On the way, they passed a funeral procession.

The prince spoke again! "Is this corpse that of a dead man or a living man?"

The executioners were baffled. "The prince spoke again!" But they had their orders. When they reached the forest, they got down and began to dig a pit. 

"We hate doing it. But we have to obey the king's orders," one of them sighed.

"Why don't you walk and speak and save us from committing this crime?" the other pleaded with the prince. 

For a moment, there was silence. Then, the prince replied, "All right. I'll walk back to the palace and speak to the king... provided he agrees to grant me a wish."

"He'll give you anything. You have only to ask," the executioners promised. They rushed back to the King and told him the condition.

The King readily agreed. "Why one, I'll grant him a thousand wishes!"

"You have agreed to grant him his wish, without knowing what it is," the Queen warned gently. 

"It will be the throne... kingship... power... money! What else could a young prince want?" the King laughed.

The city was decorated, and the citizens stood on either side of the road to see the lame prince walk home. 

"Here he comes!" the crowds cheered as the prince walked through the palace gates. 

"My son! Come! Come into the palace," the King welcomed him with open arms. "What do you want? Let me hear you speak."

"I seek your permission... to become an ascetic," Prince Abja said calmly.

"No!" the King cried in anguish. "Have I longed to hear you speak only for this? No! No! No!"

"You have given your word..." the Queen reminded him.

"Heartless woman. How can you part so easily with a son you claim to love?" the King snapped.

"Love is freedom, my lord. Let him go," she replied softly.

The King turned to his son. "But why didn't you speak all these years?"

"He did speak!" the Queen interjected. "Only the language he used was silence. Silence sometimes is more eloquent than all the loud speeches and declarations of orators. Especially if the message one wants to spread is one of peace and love."

"Then why should he choose to go away from us?" the King asked sadly.

"To free us from the fetters that bind us to him," she answered.

The King looked at the prince. "The first time you spoke you asked whether Varanasi was deserted or inhabited. What did you mean by that question?"

"You ordered my execution in an open assembly. No one in that assembly protested," the prince explained. "When a city has no one to question injustice, it is as good as deserted."

"I understand," the King nodded slowly. "And you wanted to know whether the corpse you saw was that of a living man or a dead man."

"A man who does good deeds lives in the memory of men even though his body is dead. So his corpse is the corpse of a living man. Whereas a man who harms others is as good as dead even while he lives. So his corpse is the corpse of a dead man."

"My son, you are wise beyond your years. I grant you your wish," the King conceded, bowing his head.

"Salutations to you, O teacher," the people cheered.

The prince discarded his princely robes and fell at the feet of his parents. Then he rose, turned his back on the royal life, and walked away into the horizon, leaving behind a profound legacy of wisdom and silence.
