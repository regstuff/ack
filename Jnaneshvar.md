# Jnaneshwar: Founder of the Bhakti Movement in Maharashtra

Towards the end of the thirteenth century, an incredible story of faith and devotion began to unfold in Maharashtra. One morning, Siddhopant Kulkarni, a noble officer of Alandi, found a serene young boy meditating deeply on the banks of the river Indrayani. 
"Whose son are you? Where have you come from?" Siddhopant asked gently.
"I am Vitthal, from Apegaon," the boy replied. "I left home on a pilgrimage seven years ago."

Impressed by the boy’s lofty ideals, Siddhopant took him home. That very night, Lord Panduranga appeared in Siddhopant's dream and instructed him, "Give your daughter Rukmini to Vitthal. Their children will be great spiritual teachers." 

Siddhopant arranged their marriage, and for a few years, Vitthal and Rukmini lived happily. However, when Vitthal's parents died in quick succession, the young man was overwhelmed with grief. Seeking a change of scene, Siddhopant brought the couple to Alandi. 
But the grief had ignited a deep desire for renunciation in Vitthal’s heart. "I've made up my mind to become an ascetic," he told his wife, pleading for her permission. When she refused, Vitthal used a simple excuse to leave the house and never returned. 

Vitthal traveled to Kashi, where he met the great Swami Ramananda. Desperate for spiritual initiation, Vitthal lied, claiming he was utterly alone in the world with no wife or parents. Believing him, Swami Ramananda initiated him into *sanyasa* (renunciation), naming him Chaitanya Swami.

Months later, Swami Ramananda happened to visit Alandi. When the sorrowful Rukmini bowed to him, he blessed her, saying, "May you be blessed with many children." 
Hearing this, Rukmini burst into tears, explaining that her husband had deserted her to become an ascetic. Realizing the truth, Ramananda immediately returned to Kashi and confronted Vitthal.
"You lied to free yourself!" the Swami scolded. "I command you to go back to Alandi and lead the life of a householder."

Vitthal obeyed his guru and returned to Rukmini. But the orthodox Brahmans of Alandi were furious. "Once having renounced the world, a man cannot return to his family," they decreed. "Vitthal and his wife must be excommunicated."

Despite being outcastes, the couple lived peacefully in a small hut near the river. They were blessed with four extraordinary children: three sons named Nivritti, Jnanadev, and Sopan, and a daughter named Mukta. 

When the boys came of age, Vitthal begged the Brahmans to take his family back into the fold so his sons could receive the sacred thread and study the scriptures. The Brahmans refused, stating that only the learned scholars of Paithan could grant such sanction. 

The family journeyed to Tryambak near Nasik to perform penance. One day, while walking in the hills, a fierce storm broke out, and a tiger suddenly attacked them! In the chaos, the eldest son, Nivritti, was separated from the family. 
Wandering blindly, Nivritti stumbled into a hidden cave where he met a profound mystic named Gahininath. The sage initiated the boy into the spiritual secrets of the Natha cult. When the storm subsided, Nivritti reunited with his family and excitedly passed on his spiritual initiation to his younger brother, Jnanadev.

The family then traveled to Paithan to appeal to the Brahman scholars. The orthodox priests mocked them mercilessly. 
"What is the difference between your Jnanadev and that buffalo over there?" one priest jeered, pointing to a beast of burden carrying water.
"There is no difference at all," Jnanadev replied softly. "All creatures are created by God. All are equal."

To mock him, the water-man suddenly brought his heavy whip down hard on the buffalo’s back. To the absolute shock of the assembly, bright red lash marks instantly appeared on Jnanadev's bare back, and he cried out in pain!

The Brahmans were stunned into silence, but one skeptic stepped forward. "Wait! If there is no difference between you and that buffalo... can that buffalo recite the Vedas?" 

Without a word, Jnanadev walked over to the beast and gently placed his hand on its head. Instantly, the buffalo opened its mouth and began reciting the sacred verses of the Vedas in a deep, flawlessly resonant voice!

Overwhelmed with awe, the Brahmans fell at the boy's feet. "You are a great soul! We will happily perform your thread ceremony," they declared, giving the brothers a letter of high commendation.

The family traveled on, stopping at Nevase. One morning, they encountered a tragic funeral procession. The grieving widow, preparing to die on her husband's funeral pyre, fell at Jnanadev's feet. 
"May you enjoy a long life with your husband and children," Jnanadev blessed her unconsciously.
The crowd laughed bitterly. "Impossible! Her husband is dead!" 
Jnanadev calmly asked the pallbearers to place the body on the ground. With a single touch, he brought the dead man back to life, explaining to the awestruck crowd that the soul is immortal. 

Recognizing that the profound wisdom of the scriptures was locked away in Sanskrit, accessible only to priests, Jnanadev made a revolutionary decision. At Nevase, he began to translate and explain the *Bhagavad Gita* into chaste, simple Marathi so that the common people could understand it. This magnificent work, known as the *Jnaneshwari*, drew massive crowds and became the foundational text of the Bhakti movement in Maharashtra.

Even back in Alandi, Jnanadev continued to perform astonishing miracles. When a cruel Brahman named Visoba smashed the earthenware pan his sister Mukta was using to cook pancakes, Jnanadev simply smiled. "Don't cry, Mukta," he said. He bent down, entering a deep yogic state. His bare back became glowing red hot, and Mukta happily cooked her pancakes directly on her brother's back! Visoba, witnessing this, fell at Jnanadev's feet in tears, begging for forgiveness.

Another time, a proud yogi named Changdev, who rode a live tiger and used a venomous snake as a whip, came to challenge Jnanadev. Jnanadev and his siblings were sitting on a crumbling brick wall at the time. Smiling, Jnanadev patted the wall and commanded, "Move forward to meet the sage!" 
To Changdev's sheer disbelief, the lifeless brick wall lifted off the ground and flew forward to greet him. Completely humbled, the great yogi jumped off his tiger and became Jnanadev’s devoted disciple.

Driven by a desire to spread the message of universal love and brotherhood, Jnanadev and his siblings traveled across the country, visiting great centers of learning like Kashi. Everywhere they went, they broke down the rigid barriers of caste and orthodoxy, preaching that God could be reached through pure, simple devotion (*Bhakti*) rather than complex rituals.

Having completed his divine mission and established the Bhakti movement, Jnanadev returned to Alandi. There, at the extraordinarily tender age of twenty-one, the great saint entered a state of blissful consciousness and willingly withdrew from the physical world, taking *Sanjeevan Samadhi*—a conscious, eternal meditative state—leaving behind a spiritual legacy that would guide millions for centuries to come.
