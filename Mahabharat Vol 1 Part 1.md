# Veda Vyasa

Rishi Krishna-Dwaipayana was profoundly revered as 'Veda Vyasa' because he had painstakingly analyzed, divided, and interpreted the ancient Vedas. A timeless seer of immense wisdom, Vyasa spent three long years deep in meditation, contemplating the lives, philosophies, and destinies of his ancestors and descendants. 

He dwelt extensively on the circumstances that would inevitably lead to a great, cataclysmic war amongst his grandsons—the Kauravas and the Pandavas. In his infinite wisdom, he saw their actions not as stark black or white, but in shades of grey, recognizing a profound universality in the human condition. 

As Vyasa pondered how he could possibly record this massive, complex history for the benefit of all mankind, Lord Brahma, the Creator, appeared before him. "O divine Brahma," Vyasa bowed, "I have composed a great poem in my mind, but I cannot think of any scribe on earth capable of taking it down."
"You call your work a poem, O Muni," Brahma replied warmly, "so it shall be a poem. There shall be no poet whose work will surpass this one. Therefore, let Lord Ganesha be your scribe."

Following Brahma's advice, Vyasa invoked Lord Ganesha, the remover of obstacles. Ganesha agreed to the monumental task but laid down a strict condition: "I will be the scribe of your work only if you can dictate it to me without a single pause."
Vyasa, matching wits with the deity, replied, "And you will not inscribe anything if the meaning is not perfectly clear to you." Ganesha smiled and nodded his assent. 

With the invocation of Narayana and Saraswati, Vyasa began dictating the epic tale of *Jaya* (Victory), which came to be known as the *Mahabharata*. Whenever Vyasa needed a moment to compose the next sequence, he would deliberately recite complex, profoundly intricate verses, forcing even the omniscient Ganesha to pause and grasp their deep meaning. 

When the epic was finally complete, Vyasa taught it to his disciples—Sumanta, Jaimini, Paila, Vaishampayana—and his own son, Shuka. However, it was only after the main characters of his work had departed from the earth that Vyasa permitted the story to be recited in public. 

The opportunity arose during the *Sarpa Satra* (a massive, twelve-year-long snake sacrifice) conducted by King Janamejaya, the great-grandson of Arjuna. Seeing Vyasa and his disciples at the pavilion, King Janamejaya bowed respectfully. "O Sage, you have been a witness to the terrible feud between the Kauravas and the Pandavas," the king requested. "I wish to hear from you the true history of those events."

Vyasa instructed his disciple, Vaishampayana, to narrate the epic to the king. Vaishampayana began by tracing the illustrious history of the Bharata race, starting from the ancient times when kings ruled with pure virtue.

He told the story of King Dushyanta, who while hunting in the forest, discovered a beautiful maiden named Shakuntala in the ashrama of Sage Kanwa. Shakuntala was the daughter of the celestial apsara Menaka and the sage Vishwamitra, but was raised by Sage Kanwa. Captivated by her grace, King Dushyanta married her in a secret *Gandharva* wedding. Before returning to his capital, he promised to send for her. 

In due time, Shakuntala gave birth to a son of extraordinary strength, who fearlessly played with wild lions and tigers. Recognizing the boy's destiny, Sage Kanwa sent Shakuntala and her son to Dushyanta's court. However, fearing public scandal because the marriage was secret, Dushyanta publicly denied knowing her. As Shakuntala turned to leave in righteous anger, a celestial voice thundered from the heavens, commanding the king to accept his lawful wife and son. Dushyanta happily complied, revealing he had only feigned ignorance to clear her name before his subjects. The boy, Bharata, grew up to be a legendary emperor whose name defined the entire race. 

Vaishampayana then traced the lineage down to King Uparichara (Vasu), whose seed was miraculously swallowed by an apsara cursed to live as a fish in the Yamuna river. When caught by fishermen, the fish was cut open, revealing two human infants. The boy became King Matsya, but the girl, who carried a strong odor of fish, was raised by the fisherman chief and named Satyavati. She grew up ferrying passengers across the river.

One misty morning, the great Sage Parashara boarded her ferry. Struck by her beauty, the sage created a dense, miraculous fog to hide them and asked for her hand. Touched by his divine grace, Satyavati consented, but asked that her fishy odor be replaced with a sweet fragrance. Parashara granted the boon, transforming her into *Gandhavati* (the sweet-scented one). 

On an island in the river, Satyavati gave birth to a son of immense spiritual energy. Because he was dark-skinned and born on an island (*dweep*), he was named Krishna Dwaipayana. Leaving his mother with a promise to appear whenever she needed him, the boy departed to live a life of asceticism. 

This boy grew up to become the legendary sage who arranged the Vedas and composed the Mahabharata—the great Veda Vyasa himself.


# Bheeshma's Vow

Vaishampayana continued his narration to King Janamejaya. Decades after the events surrounding Vyasa's birth, King Shantanu ruled Hastinapura. Though a great monarch, his heart was heavy with sorrow after losing his beloved wife, the river goddess Ganga. His only solace was their heroic son, Devavrata, whom he proudly installed as the heir-apparent to the Kuru throne.

Four years later, while strolling along the banks of the Yamuna, Shantanu was captivated by a divine, sweet fragrance. Tracing the scent, he discovered a breathtakingly beautiful woman ferrying passengers across the river. It was Satyavati (Gandhavati), the fisherman chief's daughter. Deeply smitten, Shantanu approached her father and asked for her hand in marriage.

"O King, I would gladly bestow my daughter on you," the fisherman chief replied respectfully but firmly. "But I ask for one promise: the son born to Satyavati must be your sole successor to the throne."
Unable to disinherit his noble and deserving son, Devavrata, Shantanu returned to Hastinapura in silent, brooding agony. 

Seeing his father wither away in despair, Devavrata quietly investigated and learned the truth from the king's old ministers. Without hesitation, the dutiful prince rode to the fisherman chief’s village, accompanied by Kuru elders.
"I relinquish my right to the throne," Devavrata announced boldly. "The son born of this maiden and my father shall be our king."

The fisherman hesitated. "O Prince, your word is absolute. But what if your future children contest the claim of my grandsons?"
To completely secure his father's happiness, Devavrata made a terrifying sacrifice. Raising his hand toward the heavens, he roared, "From this day, I adopt the vow of strict *Brahmacharya* (lifelong celibacy)! I shall never marry, nor father children."

The heavens trembled at his staggering sacrifice. Gods and sages showered flowers upon him from the skies, crying out, "Bheeshma! Bheeshma!" (The Terrible/The One of the Terrible Vow). From that moment on, Devavrata was known as Bheeshma. 
Overwhelmed by his son's devotion, King Shantanu granted Bheeshma a divine boon: *Ichcha Mrityu*—the power to choose the exact moment of his own death.

Shantanu and Satyavati were happily married and had two sons, Chitrangada and Vichitraveerya. After Shantanu passed away, Bheeshma crowned Chitrangada as king. However, the arrogant young king was soon killed in a fierce, three-year-long celestial duel with a Gandharva of the same name. Bheeshma then placed the minor Vichitraveerya on the throne, acting as his faithful regent.

When Vichitraveerya came of age, Bheeshma heard that the King of Kashi was hosting a grand *Swayamvara* for his three beautiful daughters: Amba, Ambika, and Ambalika. Determined to find suitable brides for his half-brother, Bheeshma crashed the ceremony. Thundering into the arena, he boldly abducted all three princesses onto his chariot, challenging the assembled kings to stop him if they dared. A massive battle ensued, but Bheeshma effortlessly defeated the entire coalition of kings, including the valiant King Shalva.

Upon returning to Hastinapura, the eldest princess, Amba, confessed that she had already given her heart to King Shalva. Respecting her wishes, Bheeshma honorably sent her to Shalva with an escort. However, King Shalva, humiliated by his defeat, cruelly rejected her. 

Meanwhile, Ambika and Ambalika were happily married to Vichitraveerya. But tragedy struck the Kuru line once more. Just seven years later, Vichitraveerya died of consumption, leaving no heir. 

Desperate to save the dynasty from extinction, Queen Satyavati turned to Bheeshma. "You are the only one who can carry forward the line," she pleaded. "Rule the kingdom and beget children with your brother's widows."
"Breach of truth is never approved by our sacred texts," Bheeshma refused gently but firmly. "I will never break my vow."

Left with no other choice, Satyavati summoned her firstborn son, the great ascetic Veda Vyasa, using the mental bond they shared. Following the ancient, sanctioned custom of *Niyoga*, she asked Vyasa to bless the widows with offspring. Vyasa agreed but warned that the queens must accept him in his unkempt, ascetic appearance. 

When Vyasa approached Princess Ambika, she was so terrified by his matted hair and rugged look that she squeezed her eyes tightly shut. Vyasa told Satyavati, "Because she closed her eyes, her son will be born blind." This son was Dhritarashtra.

Next, Vyasa visited Princess Ambalika. Terrified by the sage's intense aura, she turned completely pale. "Her son will be pale," Vyasa predicted. This son was Pandu.

Desperate for a perfectly healthy heir, Satyavati ordered Ambika to visit the sage again. But the frightened princess secretly sent her devoted maid in her place. The maid served Vyasa with deep reverence and humility. Pleased by her devotion, Vyasa blessed her, and she gave birth to Vidura—an incarnation of Dharma (Justice) himself, born to be supremely intelligent and wise.

Bheeshma raised the three boys with utmost love and care, training them in all princely arts. When they came of age, the blind Dhritarashtra was deemed unfit to rule, so the pale Pandu was crowned King of Hastinapura. 

Bheeshma then arranged their marriages to secure the kingdom's alliances. He matched Dhritarashtra with Princess Gandhari of Gandhara. Upon learning her husband was blind, the fiercely devoted Gandhari permanently blindfolded herself, refusing to enjoy the light her husband could not see. 

For King Pandu, Bheeshma secured Princess Kunti, the adopted daughter of King Kuntibhoja, and Princess Madri, the sister of King Shalya of Madra, ensuring the Kuru dynasty was fortified for the future.


# The Advent of the Kuru Princes

Following his marriage, King Pandu embarked on a massive military campaign, brilliantly expanding the Kuru empire. He conquered the Dasharnas, defeated the arrogant King of Magadha, vanquished the Videhas, and subdued the kingdoms of Kashi, Suhma, and Pundra. Returning to Hastinapura with immense wealth and glory, Pandu established himself as the undisputed sovereign of the land. 

Having secured the empire, Pandu handed over the administration to his blind brother Dhritarashtra and the wise Bheeshma. Seeking rest and recreation, Pandu retreated to the serene slopes of the southern Himalayas for an extended hunting expedition, accompanied by his two wives, Kunti and Madri. 

One spring day, while hunting in the dense forest, Pandu spotted a deer engaged in the act of mating and shot it down with a well-aimed arrow. But as the creature fell, it revealed itself to be a powerful sage in disguise. 
With its dying breath, the deer-sage placed a terrible curse upon the king: "As you have killed me at such a time, the same fate shall befall you. You shall die the moment you touch your wife in desire!"

Shocked and stricken with grief, Pandu renounced his royal life immediately. He ordered his attendants back to Hastinapura and informed his wives that he would now live as a wandering, celibate ascetic. Devoted to their husband, Kunti and Madri refused to leave him, vowing to share his austere life in the forest. 

When the royal attendants returned to Hastinapura with the tragic news, Dhritarashtra was deeply disturbed by his brother's fate. Meanwhile, his own wife, Queen Gandhari, had been pregnant for two long years following a boon from Sage Vyasa promising her a hundred sons. Desperate and impatient, she struck her womb in frustration and delivered a hard, lifeless lump of flesh. 

Sage Vyasa immediately appeared and intervened. He sprinkled holy water on the mass, divided it into a hundred and one parts, and placed them in sealed pots of ghee. In due course, from the first pot emerged Duryodhana, braying loudly like a donkey as terrifying omens—howling beasts and raging fires—swept the land. 
Vidura, sensing the evil portents, advised Dhritarashtra to abandon the child to save the dynasty. "This child shall be the ruin of your race," Vidura warned. But Dhritarashtra's blind love for his firstborn son deafened him to the wise counsel. Soon, the remaining ninety-nine sons and one daughter, Duhshala, were born.

Meanwhile, high in the Shatashringa mountains, Pandu lamented his inability to father heirs. He urged Kunti to use the ancient practice of *Niyoga* to bear children through holy sages. 
Kunti hesitated, then revealed a profound secret. "In my maidenhood, I served the irritable Sage Durvasa so well that he granted me a powerful mantra," she explained. "With it, I can summon any deity to bless me with a child."

Overjoyed, Pandu urged her to use the mantra immediately. At his command, Kunti invoked Dharma, the god of righteousness. By his grace, Yudhishthira was born—a prince destined to be just, honest, and the future king. 
Next, Kunti invoked Vayu, the mighty wind god, and gave birth to Bheema, a child of matchless, earth-shattering strength. 
Finally, Pandu asked her to invoke Indra, the king of gods. Kunti chanted the mantra, and Arjuna was born—a prince who would become invincible in battle and the greatest archer of his age. 

(Kunti secretly remembered that years ago, before her marriage, she had carelessly tested the mantra by invoking Surya, the sun god. She had given birth to a radiant son clad in divine armor, but out of fear of social stigma, she had tearfully abandoned the infant in a river. That boy, found and raised by a charioteer, grew up to be the mighty warrior Karna). 

Seeing Kunti blessed with three glorious sons, Pandu asked her to share the mantra with his second wife, Madri. Madri eagerly invoked the Ashwini twins, the celestial physicians, and gave birth to the exceptionally handsome and wise twins, Nakula and Sahadeva. 

The five Pandava brothers grew up in the forest under the loving care of the sages, demonstrating their extraordinary strength and divine lineage from a very young age. 

But tragedy lurked in the tranquil woods. One intoxicating spring day, years later, Pandu found himself alone with the beautiful Madri. Overcome by the romantic beauty of the blooming forest and the sheer force of desire, he momentarily forgot the deer-sage's deadly curse and embraced her. 

Instantly, the curse took effect, and King Pandu fell dead. 

Madri’s agonizing screams echoed through the forest. When Kunti and the children rushed to the scene, they found Madri weeping inconsolably over Pandu's lifeless body. Blaming her own beauty for his death, Madri firmly resolved to join him on the funeral pyre. Despite Kunti's desperate pleas, Madri committed *Sati*, entrusting her twin sons to Kunti's care. 

With their father dead, the forest sages realized it was time for the young princes to return to their rightful home. They gently led the grieving Kunti, the five Pandava boys, and the ashes of Pandu and Madri out of the mountains. Thus began the fateful journey of the Pandavas to Hastinapura, setting the stage for the greatest epic in history.
