# Arjuna Fulfils His Vow

"When Arjuna took a vow to kill Jayadratha and twanged his bow, and Krishna blew lustily on his conch Panchajanya, the very earth trembled," Sanjaya narrated to King Dhritarashtra. 

In the Kaurava camp, Jayadratha was terrified. "Arjuna has sworn to kill me. I think I'll run away to my kingdom," he told Duryodhana. 

"Ah! But who can change Arjuna's oath?" Duryodhana wavered, before regaining his composure. "Fear not. Who can dare to kill you while you are amidst brave Kshatriyas? I will guard you. Besides, there are heroes like Karna, Chitrasena, Duhshasana, and several others who, along with their armies, will protect you."

Seeking ultimate assurance, they went to Drona. "I have taught you both equally well," Drona told Duryodhana. "But the possession of celestial weapons, constant practice, and his endurance gives Arjuna an edge over you. Yet there is no cause for you to fear Arjuna while I protect you. Such a formidable Vyuha (formation) shall I build that even Arjuna will not be able to break through."

That night, an anxious Arjuna drifted into a fitful sleep. In a dream, Lord Krishna appeared. "Meditate on Lord Shiva," Krishna advised. "If he grants you that weapon, you will surely overpower Jayadratha." Arjuna dreamt he and Krishna journeyed to the Himalayas and received the *Pashupata* weapon from Shiva himself. 

The dawn of the fourteenth day found the Pandavas in a determined mood. The Kauravas had arrayed their forces in a complex triple formation. Drona guarded the Lotus formation, and Durmarshana stationed in front of the Needle formation (Shakatavyuha). Deep inside, heavily guarded, stood Jayadratha. 

Arjuna, driven by his fierce vow, launched his chariot like a thunderbolt. "Lead me, O Krishna, to Durmarshana," he ordered. Like a strong gale scattering clouds, Arjuna's arrows destroyed Durmarshana's forces. He then bypassed Drona with Krishna's deft maneuvering and plunged deep into the Kaurava army. 

Furious battles raged across the field. Drona attacked Dhrishtadyumna. Bheema, Satyaki, Nakula, and Sahadeva fought desperately to support Arjuna's advance. 

As the sun began its descent, Yudhishthira grew deeply anxious, unable to hear Arjuna's conch or see his banner. He sent Satyaki, and then Bheema, to find Arjuna and assist him. Bheema smashed his way through the Kaurava ranks, even destroying Drona's chariot with a single throw of his mace, before finally reuniting with his brother. 

The sun was rapidly sinking towards the horizon. Jayadratha was still heavily protected by Karna, Ashwatthama, Kripa, Shalya, and Duryodhana. Arjuna fought fiercely, but the sheer numbers of the Kaurava heroes slowed him down. 

"I must see my brother at once. Then I will return and kill Karna," Arjuna told Krishna, feeling the pressure of time. 

Krishna realized the danger. "I will do as you bid to help Arjuna succeed," Krishna muttered. Using his immense divine powers, Lord Krishna created an illusion of darkness, making the sun disappear completely from the sky. 

Believing the sun had set and Arjuna had failed his vow, the Kaurava army rejoiced. The tension evaporated, and the warriors relaxed their guard. Even Jayadratha, curious and relieved, stretched his neck out to look at the sky. 

"Look Arjuna! How the relieved Jayadratha raises his head towards the sun!" Krishna cried out. With a wave of his hand, Krishna withdrew the illusion. The sun reappeared, shining brightly above the horizon. 

"The sun is yet in his southern voyage. I do not wish to die at this inauspicious hour," Bheeshma had said earlier, and now Jayadratha faced his doom. 

Krishna quickly cautioned Arjuna. "Hear me well, O Arjuna! Jayadratha's father has blessed his son thus: 'In battle, whoever causes the head of my only son to fall on earth, his head will immediately shatter into hundreds of fragments.' Therefore, you must shoot in such a way that his head falls onto his father's lap."

Without a moment's hesitation, Arjuna drew the Gandeeva and released a specialized, hawk-like arrow. The arrow struck Jayadratha, severing his head cleanly, and carried it skyward. Following Krishna's instructions, Arjuna shot a continuous stream of arrows, juggling the severed head through the air over a great distance. 

Far away from the battlefield, Jayadratha's father was sitting in deep meditation. Unaware of the gruesome delivery, the severed head of his son suddenly fell onto his lap. Startled, the old king stood up, and the head rolled off his lap onto the ground. Instantly, the father's own head split into a hundred pieces, fulfilling his own curse. 

As the sun finally set, Krishna blew his conch, signaling the fulfillment of the terrible vow. The Pandavas rejoiced, while the Kauravas stared in horrified silence at the sheer power and strategy of Krishna and Arjuna.


# The Battle at Midnight

Infuriated by the killing of Jayadratha and the deceitful illusion of the sunset, Drona, Kripacharya, and Ashwatthama launched a savage attack on the Pandavas. Discarding the ancient rules of warfare that dictated fighting must stop at sunset, Drona ordered the battle to continue into the night. 

Torches were lit, and the battlefield became a surreal, terrifying scene of carnage. The darkness favored the Rakshasas, who drew immense power from the night. 

Ghatotkacha, Bheema's mighty Rakshasa son, unleashed his full fury upon the Kauravas. He created terrifying illusions, conjuring fierce beasts, fire, and darkness, slaughtering thousands. Duryodhana sent Alambusha, the Kaurava's own Rakshasa, to counter him, but Ghatotkacha engaged him in a brutal, magical duel and eventually severed Alambusha's head, throwing it at Duryodhana's feet. 

As Ghatotkacha's rampage threatened to annihilate the entire Kaurava army, a panicked Duryodhana pleaded with Karna. "O Karna, you must save us! Kill this Rakshasa, or we will all perish tonight."

Karna confronted Ghatotkacha. The battle between the supreme archer and the master of illusion was apocalyptic. When conventional weapons failed to stop the Rakshasa, Karna found himself in a desperate dilemma. 

He possessed the divine *Shakti* weapon, a gift from Lord Indra. It was an infallible, one-time-use missile that guaranteed the death of any opponent. Karna had been saving it for his ultimate showdown with Arjuna. But seeing the Kaurava army facing imminent destruction, he had no choice. 

Reluctantly, Karna took up the *Shakti* and hurled it at Ghatotkacha. Like a shaft of lightning, it struck the Rakshasa. Knowing his end was near, Ghatotkacha deliberately expanded his body to a gargantuan size before falling, crushing a vast number of Kaurava soldiers under his immense weight. 

The Pandavas were devastated by Ghatotkacha's death. Yudhishthira wept openly. But to everyone's surprise, Lord Krishna was smiling. 

"Do not grieve, O Yudhishthira," Krishna consoled him. "Karna has used his Shakti once and it has returned to Indra. He had worried about his using it against you. Now bereft of the Shakti, Karna is like a fangless serpent. Arjuna is safe."

The battle raged on into the next day. Drona, fighting as if possessed by a demon, was unstoppable. He mercilessly slaughtered King Virata and King Drupada. 

"This is not like the Acharya at all," Yudhishthira observed aghast. "He does not look human."

Krishna realized that Drona could not be defeated by force of arms. "If Drona keeps fighting thus, you will have no army left. We will have to make him lay down his weapons. He will stop fighting only if he hears that his son Ashwatthama is no more."

Arjuna absolutely refused to participate in such deceit. But Yudhishthira, seeing the annihilation of his forces, finally agreed to the grim necessity. 

Bheema rushed forward, found a large war-elephant named Ashwatthama, and killed it with a single blow of his mace. He then roared at the top of his voice, "Ashwatthama is dead!"

Drona trembled with shock, but knowing Bheema's penchant for exaggeration, he turned to the one man he knew would never lie: Yudhishthira. "Is it true that my son is dead?" Drona asked. 

Yudhishthira, his heart heavy, spoke the words that would haunt him forever. "Ashwatthama is dead," he shouted clearly, but then added in an almost inaudible whisper, "Ashwatthama the elephant." 

Just as the lie was uttered, Yudhishthira's chariot, which had always floated four fingers above the ground due to his pure righteousness, suddenly touched the earth. 

Hearing only the first part of the sentence from the truthful Yudhishthira, Drona's spirit broke. He dropped his weapons, sat down in his chariot, and entered a deep meditative trance, separating his soul from his body. 

Dhrishtadyumna, who had sworn to avenge his father Drupada's death, saw his opportunity. Rushing forward with a drawn sword, he leapt onto Drona's chariot and swiftly decapitated the venerable teacher, even as Arjuna screamed for him to stop. 

The fall of Drona sent shockwaves of despair through the Kaurava army. Ashwatthama, upon learning of his father's deceitful death, was consumed by an apocalyptic rage. He invoked the *Narayana Astra*, a supreme weapon of mass destruction. 

As the fiery Astra descended upon the Pandavas, Krishna shouted a desperate command. "Brave warriors, alight from your vehicles and throw your weapons down! The only way to escape the fury of this celestial weapon is to stand unarmed on the ground. Do not even think of fighting back!"

The entire army complied, dropping their weapons. Only Bheema, stubbornly defiant, remained in his chariot, attacking the Astra. The weapon immediately focused its immense power on him. Krishna and Arjuna physically dragged Bheema from his chariot and forced him to the ground, stripping him of his weapons. 

With no armed combatants to target, the Narayana Astra passed harmlessly over their heads, its terrifying energy dissipated. Defeated and demoralized, Ashwatthama withdrew, and the bloody day finally came to an end.


# Karna in Command

With the fall of Drona, the Kaurava army was plunged into panic and despair. To them, Drona's death seemed as remote a possibility as the oceans running dry. Even great warriors like Karna and Kripacharya initially fled the battlefield in shock. 

Duryodhana, recovering his wits, formally installed Karna as the third Commander-in-Chief of the Kaurava forces. Karna, eager to fulfill his lifelong ambition, set to work reorganizing the army. 

"I need a charioteer who is equal to Krishna," Karna told Duryodhana. "King Shalya is the only one who can match him. Request him to be my charioteer."

Duryodhana approached King Shalya, who was initially insulted by the request to serve a charioteer's son. But Duryodhana flattered him, comparing his skills to Krishna's, and Shalya agreed—on one condition. "I will drive his chariot, but I must be free to speak my mind to him, however harshly," Shalya demanded. Duryodhana, desperate, agreed. 

The sixteenth day of the war saw Karna leading the forces in a Makara (crocodile) formation. A fierce battle raged. Bheema and Duhshasana finally met in a horrifying duel. Remembering Draupadi's humiliation, Bheema fought like a demon. He defeated Duhshasana, threw him to the ground, and fulfilling his terrible vow made years ago, he ripped open Duhshasana's chest and drank his blood. The sight was so gruesome that even seasoned warriors trembled in fear. 

On the seventeenth day, Karna arrayed his army in a half-moon formation. The final, decisive confrontation between Karna and Arjuna was at hand. 

The two greatest archers of their time clashed with earth-shattering intensity. They released celestial weapons, countering each other's attacks with matching brilliance. Karna unleashed the Agneya Astra, which Arjuna countered with the Varunastra. 

During the fierce exchange, the wheel of Karna's chariot suddenly sank deep into the soft earth. This was the result of a curse from a Brahmana, whose cow Karna had accidentally killed years ago. 

"Arjuna, please stop fighting while I raise this wheel," Karna pleaded, jumping down from his chariot. "It is not fair to fight me now when I am on the ground. You know the rules of righteous combat."

Upon hearing Karna invoke the rules of fairness, Lord Krishna's fury erupted. 

"So you want us to be fair!" Krishna roared from the driver's seat. "Did you think of being fair at the game of dice? Were you righteous when you surrounded and killed Abhimanyu? Where was your Dharma when Draupadi was dragged into the assembly and disgraced? You, who stood by and watched every deceitful act of Duryodhana, dare to speak of Dharma now?"

Karna hung his head in shame, unable to answer Krishna's searing accusations. He tried desperately to lift the wheel, but it wouldn't budge. He then tried to invoke the Brahmastra, but found that he had forgotten the mantras—the result of another curse by his guru, Parashurama, for lying about his caste. 

"Arjuna!" Krishna commanded sharply. "Hurry! Kill him before he gets up on his chariot."

Arjuna, his heart hardened by Krishna's words and the memory of Abhimanyu's murder, invoked the *Anjalika*, a divine, crescent-shaped weapon. With deadly precision, he aimed at Karna, who was still struggling helplessly with the chariot wheel. 

The brilliant shaft sped from the Gandeeva and cleanly decapitated Karna. Like the sun descending on the earth, the head of the great warrior fell to the ground. 

A shadow of doom spread over the Kaurava army. The soldiers, having lost their greatest hope, scattered in terror. Duryodhana was inconsolable, his dreams of victory crushed along with his dearest friend. 

The fall of Karna marked the beginning of the end for the Kauravas. The great war, which had claimed the lives of millions, was rapidly approaching its bloody conclusion.
