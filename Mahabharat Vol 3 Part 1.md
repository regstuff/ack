# Krishna's Peace Mission

Following the wedding celebrations of Abhimanyu and Uttaraa, the illustrious descendants of the Kuru race and their allies assembled at the court of King Virata. The assembly included the five Pandavas, Virata, Drupada, Lord Krishna, Balarama, and Satyaki. 

Krishna addressed the gathering, stating the facts plainly. "You are all aware of how Shakuni deceitfully defeated Yudhishthira in the game of dice, and how the Pandavas have now completed their thirteen years in exile. What is rightfully theirs should be restored. I suggest we send a wise and virtuous man as our ambassador to persuade Duryodhana to give half his kingdom to Yudhishthira."

Balarama agreed, advocating for peace. However, Satyaki and Drupada strongly believed that Duryodhana would never yield without a fight and urged the Pandavas to prepare for war. Drupada ultimately sent his own learned priest as an emissary to Hastinapura to test the waters. 

In Hastinapura, the priest delivered the message: the Pandavas demanded their rightful share but wished to avoid war. Bheeshma acknowledged the justice of their claim, but the arrogant Karna insulted the priest, claiming the Pandavas had not fulfilled the terms of their exile. Dhritarashtra, avoiding a direct answer, sent his trusted charioteer, Sanjaya, to the Pandava camp with a hollow message of peace, asking the Pandavas not to fight but offering nothing in return. 

Yudhishthira saw through the empty words, and Krishna firmly rebuked Sanjaya. "Tell Dhritarashtra that theft is despicable, whether committed openly or otherwise. The Pandavas and their allies are prepared for war if that is what Duryodhana wants. But that will be the end of the Kauravas."

Sanjaya returned to Hastinapura and severely criticized Dhritarashtra for his blind love for his son, warning him of the impending destruction. Dhritarashtra, deeply anxious, sought the counsel of Vidura, who advised him to restore the kingdom to the Pandavas. 

In the open court, Sanjaya delivered the Pandavas' final terms: give them at least five villages—Kushasthala, Vrikasthala, Makandi, Varanavata, and one other—or face war. 

Bheeshma, Drona, and Vidura pleaded with Duryodhana to accept the generous offer. But Duryodhana remained obstinate. "I will not surrender to the Pandavas even so much land as covered by the sharp point of a needle!" he declared, stalking out of the court. 

Back in Upaplavya, Draupadi, weeping bitterly over the memory of her public humiliation, demanded revenge. Krishna comforted her. "O Draupadi, soon you will see the Kaurava women crying as you do now. I will make a final effort for peace, but if war comes, not a single Kaurava prince will be left alive."

The next morning, Krishna set out for Hastinapura on a historic peace mission. Despite strange omens like thunder from a clear sky and rivers flowing upstream, Krishna arrived safely. Dhritarashtra had ordered lavish preparations to welcome him, hoping to bribe him with gifts, but Vidura and Bheeshma saw through the ruse. 

Krishna was received with great honor. He first visited Kunti, his aunt, who wept at the plight of her sons and daughter-in-law. Krishna consoled her before returning to the court. He refused Duryodhana's invitation to a royal feast, choosing instead to eat at Vidura's humble home, stating he would only accept hospitality after his mission was successful. 

The following day, Krishna addressed the Kaurava court. He made an impassioned plea to Dhritarashtra. "O King, I come here to prevent bloodshed and establish lasting peace. You keep your sons under check, and I will restrain the Pandavas."

Bheeshma, Drona, and Vidura urged Duryodhana to listen to Krishna's wise counsel. But Duryodhana was furious and unyielding. When reprimanded, he stormed out of the assembly with his brothers. 

In a desperate, foolish move, Duryodhana, Karna, Shakuni, and Duhshasana conspired to arrest Krishna, hoping to break the spirit of the Pandavas. Satyaki learned of the plot and informed Krishna. 

Krishna laughed scornfully at their ignorance. As the conspirators approached, Krishna revealed his terrifying, divine universal form (*Vishwaroopa*). The court was blinded by his radiance, and the earth trembled. The kings closed their eyes in fear and awe. 

Resuming his human form, Krishna told Dhritarashtra, "You have seen the power I wield. Your sons' efforts to bring about peace are a sham." He declared his peace mission a failure and left the court. 

Before departing Hastinapura, Krishna visited Kunti one last time. She gave him a message for her sons: "Tell Yudhishthira that the kingdom that rightfully belongs to him lies in the hands of the enemy. He must try every means to recover it—if not by conciliation, then by war. Tell him that he must fight."

Krishna then met with Karna privately. He revealed the shocking truth of Karna's birth—that he was the eldest son of Kunti and Surya. Krishna offered Karna the kingdom and the position of emperor if he joined his brothers. 

Karna, though deeply moved, refused. "I cannot abandon Duryodhana, who has given me wealth, status, and friendship. My foster parents raised me with love. I know that the Kauravas are doomed to lose this war against you and Arjuna, but I must fight for my friend. Please keep my identity a secret, for if Yudhishthira knows I am his elder brother, he will never accept the kingdom."

With a heavy heart, Krishna accepted Karna's tragic choice. "If we come out of this great battle alive, then we shall meet again here on earth," Karna said. "Otherwise, certainly, we shall meet in heaven."

Krishna embraced Karna fondly and then drove his chariot back to Upaplavya, bringing the inevitable news that war could no longer be avoided.


# The War Begins

Upon Krishna's return to Upaplavya, he reported the failure of his peace mission to the Pandavas. Recognizing that Duryodhana was bent on war, Yudhishthira began his final military preparations. 

The Pandavas assembled an army of seven Akshauhinis. Yudhishthira asked his brothers whom they should appoint as the Commander-in-Chief. After considering Drupada, Virata, and Shikhandi, they unanimously chose Dhrishtadyumna, the valiant prince of Panchala. Arjuna selected Lord Krishna not as a warrior, but as his guide and charioteer. 

The massive Pandava army marched towards the sacred plains of Kurukshetra and pitched their tents. 

In Hastinapura, the Kauravas amassed a colossal force of eleven Akshauhinis. Duryodhana appointed the great patriarch, Bheeshma, as the Commander-in-Chief. Bheeshma accepted, but with strict conditions: "I will fight for you, but I will not slay the Pandavas. I will, however, kill ten thousand of their warriors every day. Also, there is another condition—I will not fight Shikhandi."

This decision angered Karna, who vowed not to fight as long as Bheeshma was the commander. 

Before the battle commenced, both sides established strict rules of warfare. Combat would take place only between equals—charioteer against charioteer, infantryman against infantryman. Fighting would cease at sunset, and the warriors would behave as friends in the evening. Those who surrendered, non-combatants, and those without weapons were not to be attacked. 

The great sage Veda Vyasa visited King Dhritarashtra and offered to grant him divine vision so he could witness the epic battle. Dhritarashtra, heartbroken at the thought of seeing his family slaughtered, declined. Instead, Vyasa bestowed the divine vision upon Sanjaya, the king's trusted advisor, so he could narrate the events of the war to the blind monarch. 

As the two massive armies arrayed themselves on the battlefield of Kurukshetra, a sudden gloom descended upon Arjuna. Seeing Bheeshma, his beloved grandsire, Drona, his revered teacher, and countless cousins and friends on the opposing side, his resolve shattered. 

"O Krishna," Arjuna said, his voice trembling. "The Gandeeva is about to fall from my hands. I can barely stand on my feet. I cannot bear to see the destruction of all the Kauravas. I shall not fight." He dropped his bow and sank into his chariot, overwhelmed by sorrow. 

It was then, on the battlefield, that Lord Krishna delivered the profound spiritual discourse that would become known as the Bhagavad Gita. He spoke to Arjuna of duty, righteousness, and the immortal nature of the soul. 

"The soul is indestructible," Krishna taught. "No weapon can pierce it, no fire burn it. One who is born is certain to die, and one who dies is certain to be reborn. There is nothing to grieve over. Rise up! Without making any distinction between victory and defeat, pain and pleasure, gain and loss, resolve to fight. Perform your duty with an evenness of mind, O Arjuna!"

Enlightened and freed from his delusions, Arjuna picked up the Gandeeva, his mind clear and ready to do Krishna's bidding. 

Just before the conches sounded, Yudhishthira did something entirely unexpected. Removing his armor and weapons, he walked barefoot across the battlefield towards the Kaurava army. He approached Bheeshma, Drona, Kripa, and Shalya, bowing respectfully to each and seeking their blessings for the impending war. Touched by his nobility, the elders blessed him with victory, though they remained duty-bound to fight for Duryodhana. 

As Yudhishthira returned, he called out, "I make an appeal to all Kaurava warriors. If any of you wish to come over to my side, he is welcome."

Yuyutsu, a son of Dhritarashtra, stepped forward. "O King, if you will accept me, I will join you." Thus, Yuyutsu crossed over to the Pandava side. 

The war-drums beat, the conches blew, and the two armies hurled themselves at each other. The first day of the great Kurukshetra war began with intense, hand-to-hand combat. Chariots confronted chariots, elephants clashed with elephants. The battlefield turned into a scene of unimaginable slaughter. 

Bheeshma fought with matchless valor, devastating the Pandava ranks. Abhimanyu, the young and heroic son of Arjuna, fought brilliantly against the Kaurava veterans, including Bheeshma himself, drawing praise from all. 

As the sun set on the first day, the weary soldiers returned to their camps. The Pandavas were deeply troubled by the heavy losses they had suffered at the hands of the invincible Bheeshma. The great war had truly begun, and its bloody toll was already evident.


# Bheeshma in Command

The Kurukshetra war raged on. As the days progressed, the immense power of the Kaurava Commander-in-Chief, Bheeshma, became terrifyingly apparent to the Pandavas.

On the second day, Arjuna and Bheeshma engaged in a spectacular duel. Bheeshma's arrows enveloped Arjuna, and Arjuna retaliated with equal ferocity. Drona engaged Dhrishtadyumna, while Bheema fought a desperate battle against the Kalinga forces and their war elephants. When Bheeshma attacked Bheema, Satyaki and the Pandava forces rushed to his aid, pushing back the Kauravas. 

By the third day, the Kaurava army was retreating in fear from the sheer fury of the Pandava assault. Duryodhana, watching his army scatter, rushed to Bheeshma and angrily accused him of holding back. "O Grandsire! You are deliberately not using up all your strength. Why did you not tell me before the hostilities commenced that you would not fight the Pandavas?"

Stung by Duryodhana's harsh words, Bheeshma promised to unleash his full wrath. Like a roaring fire, he attacked the Pandava army, slaughtering thousands. Seeing the immense destruction, Krishna turned to Arjuna. "O Arjuna, the time has come to vanquish your enemies! Strike at Bheeshma."

But Arjuna, still fighting out of a sense of duty rather than a desire to kill his beloved grandsire, fought mildly, parrying Bheeshma's arrows but not pressing the attack. Bheeshma's arrows continued to decimate the Pandava ranks and even struck Krishna. 

Unable to watch the slaughter any longer, Krishna's patience broke. He threw the reins of the horses, jumped down from the chariot, and rushed towards Bheeshma, his Sudarshana Chakra blazing in his hand, ready to strike down the patriarch himself. 

Bheeshma calmly dropped his bow and welcomed the attack. "O Krishna! I know not of a higher honour than that of dying at your noble hands. I welcome your attack, O Lord."

Arjuna, horrified that Krishna was about to break his vow of not fighting, chased after him. He caught Krishna by the waist, but Krishna dragged him along for several steps before Arjuna finally stopped him. "O Krishna, do not break your promise! I will not take sides in this battle anymore... I mean, I will do as you bid. I will destroy the Kauravas."

Pacified, Krishna returned to the chariot. Aroused to true fury, Arjuna unleashed the celestial Mahendra weapon. The devastating shower of arrows caused massive casualties among the Kauravas, forcing them to flee in terror as the sun set on the third day. 

Over the next few days, the battles grew increasingly fierce and bloody. Bheema engaged in brutal combat with Duryodhana and his brothers, killing many of Dhritarashtra's sons. In one encounter, Bheema struck Duryodhana with an arrow that made him faint in his chariot, forcing his charioteer to carry him off the battlefield. 

Ghatotkacha, Bheema's Rakshasa son, also fought with terrifying power, using illusions and immense strength to crush the Kaurava forces until Bheeshma intervened to stop him.

On the eighth day, tragedy struck the Pandava camp. Arjuna's brave son, Iravan, fought a magnificent battle against the forces of Shakuni. Duryodhana dispatched the Rakshasa Alambusha to kill him. After a fierce, magical fight where both warriors changed forms, Alambusha managed to behead Iravan. Arjuna was heartbroken. "O Krishna," he wept, "what use are wealth and power gained through large-scale destruction?"

The fighting continued to escalate. On the ninth day, Bheeshma fought like a man possessed. He caused complete panic in the Pandava army, routing their forces and seemingly fulfilling his vow to slaughter ten thousand warriors a day. No one, not even Arjuna, could stand against him.

That night, a deeply depressed Yudhishthira consulted Krishna. "O Krishna, Bheeshma is destroying our army like a raging fire. It is better to retire to the forest than to lead these brave men to their deaths. I see no way to defeat him."

Krishna offered to take up arms again. "I will slay Bheeshma myself, O Yudhishthira, if Arjuna hesitates."

But Yudhishthira refused to let Krishna break his vow. Instead, they decided on a drastic course of action. Yudhishthira, Krishna, and the Pandava brothers went unarmed to Bheeshma's tent in the dead of night. 

They bowed respectfully to the Grandsire. "O Grandsire," Yudhishthira pleaded, "how can we win this war when you are fighting against us so fiercely? Tell us, O wise one, how your own death can be brought about."

Bheeshma, knowing his duty but also desiring the ultimate victory of Dharma, revealed his weakness. "As long as I hold a weapon, no one can defeat me. But I have taken a vow never to strike a woman, or anyone who was previously a woman. Your commander, Shikhandi, was born a female. If you place Shikhandi in front of Arjuna, I will lay down my arms. Then, Arjuna can strike me down."

The Pandavas returned to their camp with a heavy heart, knowing what they had to do. 

On the morning of the tenth day, as the two armies arrayed for battle, Arjuna placed Shikhandi at the vanguard of his forces, directly facing Bheeshma. The stage was set for the fall of the greatest warrior of the Kuru race, as the war entered its most pivotal phase.
