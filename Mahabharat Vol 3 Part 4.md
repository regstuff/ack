# The Kurus Routed

By the afternoon of the seventeenth day of the great war, Karna lay dead on the battlefield. His fall broke the spirit of the Kauravas. King Shalya, weeping, drove Karna's empty chariot back to the camp, his ears filled with the triumphant cries of the Pandava forces. 

Dhritarashtra, utterly devastated by Sanjaya's report of the battle, sank into despair. But Duryodhana refused to yield. "Take my chariot ahead," he commanded his forces. "I will kill Arjuna, Krishna, and that conceited Bheema, and avenge the death of Karna."

However, his brave words could not stop the panicked retreat of his soldiers. Disgusted by their cowardice, Duryodhana begged Shalya to take command. "This is a testing time for us. Please take command and lead us to victory," Duryodhana pleaded. Shalya accepted the formidable task. 

With Shalya as the new Commander-in-Chief, the Kaurava army rallied for the eighteenth day of the war. They marched out in a complex wedge formation. Yudhishthira, recognizing the threat, divided the Pandava forces into three divisions, led by Dhrishtadyumna, Shikhandi, and Satyaki. 

The battle was ferocious. Yudhishthira sought out Shalya, eager to end the war. "The mild and calm Yudhishthira appeared to be transformed into a determined and formidable foe," Sanjaya recounted. "Like a strong gust of wind dispelling clouds, Yudhishthira played havoc with the Kaurava army."

Shalya and Yudhishthira engaged in a brutal duel. They shattered each other's bows, killed each other's horses, and finally resorted to a terrifying fight with maces. Both warriors struck each other simultaneously and collapsed, but were quickly rescued by their allies. 

Upon recovering, Yudhishthira knew he had to finish the task. He picked up a fierce, golden javelin, studded with gems, and hurled it at Shalya with all his might. The javelin struck true, piercing Shalya's broad chest. With his arms spread out, the great King Shalya fell to the earth, dead. 

The fall of their final commander sent the Kauravas into absolute panic. The Pandava army, led by Bheema, Nakula, and Sahadeva, relentlessly pursued the fleeing soldiers. Bheema swung his mighty mace, crushing chariots, horses, and elephants. Sahadeva sought out the wily Shakuni, destroying his chariot and forcing him to fight on foot. With a vicious, golden-feathered spear, Sahadeva finally beheaded the man who had caused the Pandavas so much misery. 

By noon, the immense Kaurava army, which once boasted eleven Akshauhinis, was completely annihilated. Not a single soldier remained standing. Duryodhana, the mighty king who had started the war, stood utterly alone on the battlefield, surrounded by millions of corpses. 

Petrified with fear and bleeding from his wounds, Duryodhana fled on foot. He reached Dwaita Lake and, using a magical yogic power, submerged himself in the water, remaining hidden beneath the surface. 

Meanwhile, Yudhishthira realized Duryodhana was missing and sent spies to find him. Some hunters, who had overheard Duryodhana conversing with Ashwatthama, Kripa, and Kritavarma near the lake, reported his location to the Pandavas. 

The Pandavas, accompanied by Krishna, rushed to the lake. Yudhishthira stood at the bank and taunted his cowardly enemy. "Come out and fight! Conquer us and rule, or be killed like a warrior."

Stung by the insults, Duryodhana rose from the water, his armor glistening. Yudhishthira made a magnanimous offer: "Choose any one of us to fight with you. And if you kill one of us in the fight, we will anoint you King."

Duryodhana chose to fight Bheema with a mace. The two colossal warriors clashed, their maces creating sparks that lit up the dusk. Balarama, who had arrived from his pilgrimage just in time, watched the duel. 

The fight was brutal and prolonged. Bheema was tiring. Krishna pointed out that Duryodhana had spent the last thirteen years practicing with the mace on an iron statue of Bheema, while Bheema lacked such practice. "He cannot beat Duryodhana in a fair mace fight," Krishna warned Arjuna. "Remember Bheema's vow to break Duryodhana's thighs?"

Catching Krishna's hint, Arjuna caught Bheema's eye and slapped his own thigh. Bheema understood instantly. When Duryodhana leaped into the air to dodge a blow, Bheema swung his mace forcefully, smashing Duryodhana's thighs. 

Duryodhana collapsed, his thighs broken. Bheema, his anger boiling over, kicked the fallen king's head. Yudhishthira quickly intervened, reprimanding Bheema for disrespecting a dying king. 

Balarama, infuriated by the foul blow, raised his plough weapon to kill Bheema, but Krishna restrained him, reminding him of Bheema's vow and the Kauravas' past sins. Disgusted, Balarama left for Dwaraka. 

As Duryodhana lay dying, he bitterly accused Krishna of orchestrating the war through deceit. Krishna silenced him. "You are suffering now as a result of your own misdeeds," Krishna retorted. "It was you who caused the death of all your dear ones, and now you have been made to suffer by the Kauravas."

The gods showered flowers upon the Pandavas, confirming their righteous victory. The great Kurukshetra war had finally ended, but the cost was unimaginable.


# After the War

Though the great Kurukshetra war was won, the bloodshed was not yet over. Duryodhana lay dying on the battlefield, his thighs broken by Bheema's mace. As night fell, three surviving Kaurava warriors—Ashwatthama, Kripacharya, and Kritavarma—visited their fallen king. 

Seeing Duryodhana's pathetic state, Ashwatthama was consumed by rage. "I swear to you by everything sacred that I will send all the Panchalas and the Pandavas to death tonight!" he vowed, clenching his fists. Touched by his loyalty, Duryodhana formally installed Ashwatthama as the final Commander of the Kaurava army. 

The three warriors retreated to a nearby forest. As Kripa and Kritavarma slept, Ashwatthama sat fuming under a large banyan tree. Suddenly, he noticed a terrible owl swoop down and mercilessly slaughter hundreds of sleeping crows in the branches above. 

"This bird has shown me the way," Ashwatthama thought. "I know what I must do now. It is wiser to use a foolproof though foul method than take a foolhardy chance to defeat them in a fair fight. I will attack the unsuspecting Pandavas while they are asleep."

He woke Kripa and Kritavarma and declared his plan. Kripacharya, horrified, tried to dissuade him, arguing that killing sleeping men was a sin and completely against Kshatriya Dharma. But Ashwatthama was adamant. "My own mind now refuses to function," he declared. "I will not surrender to the Pandavas. I have avenged the death of my father."

The three arrived at the Pandava camp at midnight. Ashwatthama stationed Kripa and Kritavarma at the gates with orders to kill anyone who tried to escape. He then stealthily entered the camp alone. 

His first target was Dhrishtadyumna, the man who had beheaded his father, Drona. Ashwatthama found him sleeping in his tent, woke him with a brutal kick, and pinned him to the floor. Ignoring Dhrishtadyumna's pleas for a warrior's death by a weapon, Ashwatthama kicked and strangled him to death like an animal. 

The commotion awoke the camp. The women screamed, and guards rushed forward, but Ashwatthama, possessed by an unholy fury, slaughtered them all. He sought out Shikhandi and killed him. Then, he found the tents of the Upapandavas—the five young sons of Draupadi—and ruthlessly murdered them in their sleep. 

Kripa and Kritavarma set fire to the camp from three sides. The flames spread quickly, creating absolute panic. Soldiers and animals ran amok, crushing each other in the darkness. Anyone who managed to escape the flames was mercilessly cut down by Kripa and Kritavarma at the gates. 

By daybreak, the entire Pandava camp, including the mighty Panchala army, was completely annihilated. Ashwatthama returned to the dying Duryodhana and delivered the gruesome news. "Only the five Pandavas, Krishna, and Satyaki remain," he reported. "All the Panchalas and all the sons of Draupadi are dead." 

Hearing this, Duryodhana smiled for the last time and breathed his last, content that his enemies had suffered as much as he had. 

When Yudhishthira learned of the midnight massacre, he swooned in shock. Draupadi arrived, her heart shattered by the loss of all her five sons and her brother. "I will kill the wretched Ashwatthama and all his friends!" she vowed. 

Bheema, driven by Draupadi's grief, mounted his chariot and chased after Ashwatthama. Yudhishthira, Arjuna, and Krishna quickly followed, knowing that Ashwatthama possessed a weapon that could destroy the world. 

They found Ashwatthama on the banks of the Ganga, hiding among sages. Seeing the Pandavas approach, a terrified Ashwatthama picked up a blade of grass, chanted a deadly mantra, and invoked the ultimate weapon: the *Brahmashira*. "May this weapon destroy all the Pandavas," he cursed, releasing the devastating energy. 

Krishna instantly commanded Arjuna to release his own *Brahmashira* to counter it. "Only a Brahmashira can quell a Brahmashira," Krishna shouted. Arjuna released his weapon, praying for the safety of the world. 

The two supreme weapons clashed in the sky, creating a terrifying ball of fire that threatened to burn the entire universe to ashes. The oceans boiled, and the earth trembled. 

Suddenly, the great sages Vyasa and Narada appeared between the two weapons, suspending their destructive power. "Recall your weapons immediately!" Vyasa commanded. "Such weapons must never be used against human beings. They will destroy the world!"

Arjuna, pure of heart, immediately withdrew his weapon. But Ashwatthama, his mind clouded with hate, admitted he could not retract the *Brahmashira*. "I cannot recall it," he confessed. "Let its effect be directed to the future progeny of the Pandavas if not to them." He directed the weapon's lethal energy toward the wombs of the Pandava women, specifically targeting Princess Uttaraa, who was pregnant with Abhimanyu's child. 

Krishna was furious. "You wicked Ashwatthama!" he cursed. "Despite your curse, the son of Uttaraa will be revived at birth. He will grow up to be a mighty king and continue the Pandava dynasty. But as for you, you shall wander the earth for three thousand years, alone, stinking of blood and pus, suffering from incurable diseases, despised by all!"

Ashwatthama surrendered the precious, magical jewel he wore on his head and fled into the jungle, destined to live out his eternal curse. Bheema brought the jewel back to Draupadi, who, having avenged her sons, offered it to Yudhishthira to wear on his crown. 

The war was over. The enemy was destroyed, but the cost was an empty kingdom, a slaughtered family, and a grief that would never truly heal.


# Yudhishthira's Coronation

Following the devastating Kurukshetra war and the tragic midnight massacre, the Pandavas set out for Hastinapura to meet the grieving King Dhritarashtra and Queen Gandhari. 

The blind king, overwhelmed by the death of his hundred sons, wept inconsolably. When the Pandavas arrived, he embraced Yudhishthira and the others, but his heart harbored a deep, murderous hatred for Bheema, who had killed Duryodhana and Duhshasana. 

"Where is Bheema?" Dhritarashtra asked, holding out his arms. 

Krishna, divining the king's dark intention, quickly pushed Bheema aside and replaced him with a life-sized solid iron statue of Bheema. Dhritarashtra enveloped the statue in a crushing embrace. With such immense force did the grieving father squeeze the iron figure that it shattered into pieces, and Dhritarashtra himself collapsed, vomiting blood. 

His anger spent, remorse washed over him. "Oh Bheema! What have I done?" he wailed. 

Krishna gently consoled him. "Grieve not, O King. It was not the real Bheema you crushed, but merely an iron image. I knew of your anger and arranged for the statue. Your sons died because of their own misdeeds." Dhritarashtra, realizing his folly, then warmly embraced the real Bheema and the rest of the brothers. 

Next, the Pandavas faced Queen Gandhari, who was burning with sorrow. As Yudhishthira bowed before her, Gandhari's intense, grief-stricken gaze pierced through a gap in her blindfold and fell upon Yudhishthira's toes. Instantly, his beautiful toenails were charred black by the sheer power of her sorrow and anger. 

Arjuna, terrified, hid behind Krishna. But Gandhari, a woman of great virtue, quickly composed herself and blessed the Pandavas, treating them as her own sons. 

Together with the grieving women of the royal household, the Pandavas went to the banks of the Ganga to perform the final funeral rites for the millions of fallen warriors. It was here that Kunti, unable to bear the secret any longer, made a shocking revelation. 

"Offer oblations to Karna," she wept, her voice trembling. "He was my firstborn son, blessed to me by the Sun God, Surya. He was your eldest brother."

The Pandavas were astounded and horrified. Yudhishthira recoiled in shock. "What have we done?" he cried. "We have killed our own brother!" He cursed all women, decreeing that from that day forth, no woman would ever be able to keep a secret. 

Overwhelmed by guilt and the staggering loss of life, Yudhishthira lost all desire for the throne. "I have no desire to rule over this kingdom built on the death of so many dear ones," he declared, wanting to renounce the world and become a forest ascetic. 

His brothers, Draupadi, and Sage Vyasa tried in vain to change his mind. Finally, Krishna told him, "Go to Grandsire Bheeshma. He lies on a bed of arrows, waiting for the auspicious time to leave his body. He alone can clear your doubts."

Yudhishthira, accompanied by Krishna and his brothers, went to the battlefield where Bheeshma lay surrounded by sages. Bheeshma, pleased to see them, imparted a vast treasury of knowledge. For fifty-eight days, he discoursed extensively on the duties of a king (*Rajadharma*), the subtleties of righteousness, and the path to salvation. 

"A king should duly honour the Devas and the Brahmanas," Bheeshma instructed. "He should be a man of action and always strive for truth. He should be just, humble, straightforward, and righteous." 

Bheeshma's profound wisdom finally lifted the darkness from Yudhishthira's mind, reconciling him to his duty as the sovereign. 

When the sun finally began its northward journey (*Uttarayan*), the auspicious time arrived. Bheeshma gave his final blessings to the Pandavas and Dhritarashtra. "I grant you your wish, you have the power to summon death at will," Krishna told him. 

With a serene smile, Bheeshma used his yogic powers to draw his life force upwards. The onlookers witnessed a miraculous sight as his soul finally escaped through his skull, ascending to the heavens amidst a shower of celestial flowers. The Pandavas performed his last rites with great honor on the banks of the Ganga. 

Free from doubt and sorrow, Yudhishthira returned to Hastinapura. He was formally crowned Emperor in a magnificent coronation ceremony. To ensure a just and prosperous reign, he immediately allocated responsibilities. He appointed Bheema as the *Yuvaraja* (Crown Prince), Vidura as the Chief Minister, Sanjaya as the Minister of Finance, and Arjuna as the Commander-in-Chief. Nakula and Sahadeva were appointed as his personal protectors. 

The celebrations culminated when Princess Uttaraa gave birth to Abhimanyu's son. Though the child was born lifeless due to Ashwatthama's curse, Lord Krishna fulfilled his promise. Using his divine power, Krishna touched the infant and brought him back to life. 

"I bless the boy who will be called Pareekshit," Krishna announced, as the child opened his eyes. 

With the Kuru lineage secured and the righteous King Yudhishthira firmly on the throne, peace and prosperity finally returned to Hastinapura, marking the end of the great epic of the Mahabharata.
