    Shalivahana

    The story begins in a small village, soon after the death of a man whose widow, Gautami, and young son, Shatakarni, were heartlessly turned out of their home by her own brothers. "Mother, why did they throw out our belongings? Why are they locking us out?" young Shatakarni asked, tears in his eyes. "Because they don't want us here, my son," Gautami replied bitterly. "That's not fair. It's our home! Let's stop them, mother!" "How can we? We are helpless without your father," she sighed, picking up their meagre belongings. "Where will we go now?" "Wherever fate takes us, my son."

    They walked for many miles until they reached a small village on the outskirts of Pratishthana (modern Paithan). "Look, mother! What is he doing? What is that?" Shatakarni asked, pointing to an old man working. "He's a potter. That's the wheel he uses to make pots," Gautami explained.

    Shatakarni ran up to the old potter. "Will you teach me how to do that, grandfather?" The old potter immediately took to the endearing child. "Certainly I will! My fingers are not what they used to be, though." "Shatakarni! Don't disturb him at his work. Come away," Gautami scolded gently. "Let him be. He is a delightful child," the potter smiled warmly. "My dear, you look ill and tired! Why don't you come inside and rest for a while?"

    His kindness and genuine concern were too much for Gautami after the harshness of her own kin. She broke down in tears. "Now, now. Don't weep. Come in and tell me what the matter is. Perhaps I can help you," the old man comforted her. Later, when Gautami told him her tragic tale, he offered, "Why don't you stay here with me? I am old and lonely. The little one could help me and learn a trade." "Mother, let us stay here. Please," Shatakarni begged. Gautami agreed. "I am grateful to you."

    Little Shatakarni spent most of his time with the potter. "Grandfather, when will you teach me to work at the wheel?" he would ask eagerly. "If you want to become a potter, you must first learn how to knead clay and fire pots in the furnace."

    One hot day, a wandering sadhu stopped at the potter's hut. "Son, may I have some water to drink?" "In a moment, holy one!" Shatakarni ran into the hut. While he was gone, the sadhu sighed. "But for the dacoits, Pratishthana would be one of the best cities that I have passed through." "I know. It's a shame," the potter agreed. "I've seen many seasons; but the city has not yet seen a soul valiant enough to fight and vanquish the dacoits."

    Just then, Shatakarni came out with water. "Quench your thirst, holy one!" The sadhu drank, then looked at the boy intently. "Why do you look at me like that, holy one?" Shatakarni asked. "My son, one day you will become king and that day will be an auspicious one for Pratishthana!" the sadhu blessed him.

    The potter smiled sceptically. "The apprentice of a poor old potter, the son of a destitute widow to become king! Impossible!" "Be it a prophecy or a blessing, it makes me happy," the sadhu smiled.

    But little Shatakarni took the sadhu's words very seriously. If I am to be king, I must have an army of soldiers, chariots, horses and elephants! he thought. He began to shape his own army out of clay figures. Ah! These are ready. While they dry in the sun, I'll collect bits of dry wood and twigs. After drying them in the sun, he baked them in the furnace. The potter watched him proudly. "How perfect the images are! The boy certainly has deft fingers!"

    Shatakarni then took the baked terra-cotta images and, to the potter's complete bafflement, threw them into a well in the backyard. He did this several times over the years. One day, the potter asked, "Son, why do you throw those lovely toys into the well? Why don't you play with them?" "They are not toys, grandfather. They are the soldiers, chariots and animals that I'm going to need when I become king. I'm preserving them here till then."

    The child has taken the sadhu's words seriously! the potter realized. But seeing the earnestness and simple faith of the child, he swallowed the practical words that almost tumbled out. Instead he said, "True, my son. You are going to need them. And what better place to preserve them in, than this well!"

    The years went by. Shatakarni, now a strong young lad, was a great help to the aging potter. He sat at the wheel from dawn till dusk. One day, the potter said, "That's enough for today, son. You may go and play now."

    Shatakarni ran off to join the boisterous boys of the neighbourhood. They were playing near a sturdy Shali tree. Shatakarni climbed up and sat astride his favourite branch. "I am a king and this is my vahana (vehicle)!" he declared playfully. "Born to vanquish the dacoits of Pratishthana!" "And I will, too," another boy shouted. "Just wait till I'm older and stronger." "Then we will be your aides!" the boys cheered. "A Shali branch for a vahana! The great Shalivahana!" "Hail King Shalivahana!"

    The boys paraded through the village. "King Shalivahana is out to see that all is well in his city." "Ha! Ha! Shalivahana! That's a fine name," the potter chuckled. And the name stuck.

    As Shalivahana grew older, he spent his leisure hours with his companions in the busy streets of Pratishthana. One evening, he noticed a group of rough-looking men. "Sh! Wait! Those men look suspicious. Let's go closer and listen to what they're saying," Shalivahana whispered to his friends. "The merchant Suvarna's caravan leaves tonight! And loaded too!" one of the men muttered. "Send word to the others to get ready for the loot." "They're dacoits!" Shalivahana realized.

    That night, as the dacoits brutally attacked the merchant's caravan, Shalivahana and his friends ambushed them. "Leave him alone!" they shouted, beating the dacoits back with heavy sticks. The miscreants took to their heels. "God bless you," the merchant thanked them profusely.

    Later, at the dacoits' hideout, their leader seethed. "We must teach that busybody, Shalivahana, a lesson. He is the leader. We must get rid of him. It won't be difficult."

    A few evenings later, as Shalivahana and his friends were returning home, the dacoits ambushed them. "It's those dacoits! They have daggers and knives!" But Shalivahana and his friends did not flinch. They fought back so fiercely that the dacoits soon panicked. "We underestimated them! Run for your lives!"

    After this incident, Shalivahana actively armed his friends with swords, spears, and daggers. He organised a regular patrol to keep Pratishthana safe and peaceful. The people of Pratishthana encouraged and helped them. "Shalivahana, you and your friends have brought prosperity to our city. Is there anything we can do for you?" the town elders asked. "All we need is your support to get rid of those dacoits," Shalivahana replied.

    The prosperous traders eagerly showered gifts on Shalivahana and his men. "Please accept this horse," a merchant offered. "These swords are for you—from the blacksmiths," another said.

    The dacoits were alarmed by these developments. "Now Shalivahana has arms and horses as well! He will put us out of business. He must be finished." "But how? He is stronger than we are." "Not cleverer, though!" their leader sneered.

    The dacoits disguised themselves as merchants and traveled to the court of King Nahapana, the cruel suzerain of Pratishthana. "Sir, a potter called Shalivahana persecutes innocent citizens and..." they lied. "And what? Speak up, man!" Nahapana barked. "Pardon us, sir. He says, he will soon dethrone you." "He will, will he? The impudent potter!"

    The very next day, King Nahapana marched towards Pratishthana with a massive army. "Have you heard?" the panicked citizens cried. "King Nahapana is marching towards Pratishthana with a huge army!" "I suggest we seek the help of King Vikramaditya of Ujjayini," one elder suggested. "If we do, he might annexe Pratishthana," Shalivahana argued calmly. "No, we shall not seek outside help." "Your decision is wise. What if we are few in number? The people are with us. With their support, we'll repulse the attack."

    Most of the men of Pratishthana rallied around Shalivahana. They lay waiting behind trees and rocks along the narrow route to the city. As soon as the approaching army was sighted, Shalivahana yelled, "Charge!"

    Nahapana and his men were taken completely unawares in the ambush. In the bloody battle that ensued, Nahapana was killed. "Victory to Shalivahana!" the people cheered.

    When Shalivahana returned to Pratishthana, the entire city celebrated. "Long live King Shalivahana!" Gautami wept tears of joy. "My son! My noble son!" When Shalivahana officially ascended the throne of Pratishthana, two great scholars, Sarva Varma and Gunadhya, became his most trusted ministers.

    Once, while Shalivahana was out on a hunt with Gunadhya, they came across a sage outside his hermitage. "May your reign be a long and peaceful one, Shalivahana," the sage blessed him, inviting them into his hut.

    Inside, a beautiful young woman served them food. What a radiant face she has! Her eyes shine with intelligence! Shalivahana thought, instantly captivated. "Who is she, revered one?" he asked. "She is my disciple. Her name is Naganika. She is the daughter of a Naga chief. An exceptionally clever girl!"

    As Shalivahana rode back to the palace, he confessed to his minister, "I can't get that girl out of my mind." "I'll speak to her father," Gunadhya smiled. "The kingdom needs a queen."

    Naganika's father readily agreed to the prestigious proposal, and Shalivahana and Naganika were married with great celebration.

    A few days later, the young couple were playfully bathing in the palace pool. Shalivahana enthusiastically splashed water on his new bride. "Modaka...!" Naganika cried out, sputtering. Thinking she wanted a sweetmeat, Shalivahana immediately ordered his attendants, "The queen wishes to eat modaka. Have some brought here."

    Naganika burst out laughing. "Ha! Ha! Ha! I didn't ask for modaka! I made a pun on the Sanskrit words Ma and Udaka—meaning 'No water, hence don't splash water'."

    Shalivahana flushed deeply. "My ignorance shames me, my queen. You deserved a scholar for a husband, not a fool like me!" "Never say that again, my lord," Naganika said gently. "I am proud to be your wife and I love you with all my heart."

    Her loving words, however, did not soothe his wounded pride. If I had learnt Sanskrit, I would not have made a fool of myself today, he resolved. I will learn the language.

    He summoned his learned ministers. "How long does it take to learn Sanskrit, Gunadhya?" "It usually takes twelve years, my lord. But you could do it in six." "And what do you have to say, Sarva Varma?" the king asked the other minister. "I can teach it to you in six months, my lord." Gunadhya scoffed. "It's a vain boast, my lord! If I am proved wrong, I shall forever give up speaking Sanskrit as well as Prakrit, the spoken language of our people."

    Sarva Varma and his royal pupil worked tirelessly, day and night. Six months later, the king appeared before the scholars of the court and addressed them in flawless, eloquent Sanskrit. "I can't believe it!" the court marvelled.

    At the end of the session, Gunadhya quietly went up to the king, bowed low, and turned to walk away forever. "Gunadhya! Where are you going?" Shalivahana asked. "How can I reply?" Gunadhya smiled sadly. "I have vowed not to speak in the only languages I know! And Paishachi, the crude language of the tribes, which the king knows, I don't."

    A few months later, two wild-looking Paishachas called on Shalivahana. "Your majesty, we bring you this great Paishachi classic, the Brihatkatha, written by our guru." "A classic in that crude, unrefined language?" Shalivahana dismissed them. "You may take it back. I don't have the time for it."

    Soon after, Shalivahana fell ill. The royal physician, suspecting his diet, sent for the cook. "The king has been eating inferior meat," the physician accused. "We cook what the hunters supply, sir," the cook defended himself. When the hunters were summoned, they explained, "It's that Brahman in the Vindhya forest! He reads out his verses, and all the animals stop to listen, completely oblivious of hunger and thirst. They stand there spellbound. That's what has made them lean and unhealthy!"

    "He must be a great sage! I must visit him," Shalivahana decided.

    He rode deep into the forest. As he approached a clearing, he heard the hermit reciting his compositions to a mesmerised audience of deer and rabbits. Why, I never dreamt that Paishachi could sound so musical! Shalivahana thought in awe.

    Suddenly, he saw the hermit take a palm leaf manuscript and throw it into a fire! "Stop!" Shalivahana yelled, rushing forward to wrest the manuscript from the flames. He looked closely at the wild, unkempt hermit. "Gunadhya! You?"

    Gunadhya smiled sadly and spoke to Shalivahana in Paishachi. "Yes, I learnt Paishachi here in the forest and composed the Brihatkatha for you. Saddened by your rejection, I have been destroying my life's work leaf by leaf." "What a conceited fool I have been!" Shalivahana cried. "Pardon me, Gunadhya. Please let me have what is left of your great work." "Take it, my king. It is for you." "Why don't you return to the palace with me, Gunadhya?" "No, my king. I have fulfilled my destiny. I would now like to depart from this earth." Heartbroken, the king had to return to the capital alone, carrying the precious remains of the Brihatkatha.

    A few days later, far away at the court of the legendary King Vikramaditya of Ujjayini, an astrologer astounded everyone with a dark prediction. "Sir, you will be defeated and killed by a young king, a potter by upbringing, who now lives in the south."

    Vikramaditya immediately sent for his powerful demon-slave, a Vetala. "Track that man down and kill him." "It shall be done, my lord."

    The Vetala flew south and reached Pratishthana, where he heard tales of the king who was once a potter. People here extol this king even as we do Vikramaditya, the demon thought. If the tales of his glory and greatness are true, he is without doubt a threat to my king.

    The Vetala boldly entered the court of Shalivahana. "I come from Vikramaditya's court. You were not born of a royal family. But if you are a true king, I challenge you to a wrestling bout." "I accept your challenge," Shalivahana replied calmly, stepping down from his throne.

    The Vetala sprang forward, grappling with superhuman strength, and threw the king to the ground. Then, lifting Shalivahana high into the air, he hurled him away. But Shalivahana flipped gracefully, fell on his feet, and quickly rose up to face the Vetala once again. With a mighty surge of strength, Shalivahana overpowered the demon, pinning him to the floor.

    "Mercy, O king! You have won," the Vetala gasped. "And in a fair bout. Leave my kingdom and don't dare come here again!" Shalivahana commanded. This is the very man, the potter, who is destined to defeat King Vikramaditya, the Vetala realized in awe as he fled.

    The Vetala returned to Vikramaditya's court. "I have found the potter. He is none other than King Shalivahana. But I could not kill him. He defeated me in a fair fight." "Defeated you?" Vikramaditya roared. "Then I must vanquish him myself!"

    When news of Vikramaditya's massive impending attack reached Pratishthana, the citizens grew anxious. "Prepare to give battle. He is a mighty foe," Shalivahana ordered. "Not too mighty for us, your majesty!" his loyal men shouted.

    Before leaving for battle, Shalivahana went to seek his mother's blessings. "Victory shall be yours," Gautami smiled. At the head of his comparatively small army, Shalivahana marched forth to face the mighty Vikramaditya and his vast armies.

    Soon, the two armies stood face to face on a vast plain. Vikramaditya took one look at the small forces of his enemy and laughed loudly. "So that's the king and that his army! Ha! Ha! They do frighten me! Shalivahana has invited his doom. Attack!"

    The two armies met, and a bloody battle ensued. But the sheer numbers of Ujjayini's forces began to overwhelm the defenders of Pratishthana. "My men are falling one by one," Shalivahana realized grimly. "What shall we do?"

    Meanwhile, news of the desperate battle reached the several villages and towns nearby. "Our king needs us. Rise to the occasion, every man among you!" the village leaders shouted. Men marched to the battlefield from every direction, armed with whatever they could find. "Glory to King Shalivahana! Victory shall be ours!"

    Vikramaditya's commanders pointed nervously. "Look! My lord! More of them! We have under-estimated his strength!" While Shalivahana was pleasantly puzzled by the sudden reinforcements, Vikramaditya was dismayed. "Shalivahana is a sly one! Where have they come from?"

    As the tide of battle turned, Vikramaditya's forces started retreating in panic. "Where did our king Shalivahana post all these forces? And those war animals?" a Pratishthana soldier wondered aloud. "I cannot but believe that the clay images he made as a child have magically come to life to aid him," an old villager whispered in awe.

    At last, the legendary Vikramaditya fell in battle. "Long live Shalivahana! Long live the King of Kings!" the victorious army chanted.

    At Ujjayini, as Shalivahana ascended the throne of Vikramaditya, he made a grand proclamation. "To commemorate our great victory, this day shall mark the commencement of a new era—the Shalivahana Shaka."

    (The day Shalivahana, the son of a destitute widow, became the emperor of Ujjayini, is observed as New Year's Day, to this day in Maharashtra, Karnataka and other parts of India.)
