# True Friends: Jataka Tales of Good Conduct

## True Friends (Nigrodha Jataka)
Long, long ago, during the reign of King Magadha, a grand wedding was celebrated in Rajagriha. The bride was beautiful, and her father had given generous gifts. However, after a few months, her mother-in-law began treating her with harshness because she hadn't borne a child. 
"I will not get any respect in this house unless I am with child," the worried daughter-in-law confided in her maid. The maid advised her to pretend to be expectant and return to her parents' house to avoid the constant nagging. 

She set out on her journey with a caravan. They camped near a grove of trees for the night. Next morning, she awoke to find the caravan had moved on without her. Right there, under a Nigrodha (Banyan) tree, she gave birth to a baby boy. Her attendant soon found her and they returned to Rajagriha amidst a joyous welcome. The boy was named Nigrodha Kumar. 

On the same day, two more boys were born in the city: Pottik, in a weaver's house, and Shakha Kumar, in a merchant's house. 
The three boys grew up playing together. When they were about eleven years old, they went to Takshashila for studies. Pottik, being poor, worked for Nigrodha to pay for his fees. 

While returning from Takshashila, they reached the outskirts of Varanasi and slept under a tree. Early at dawn, Pottik woke up hearing the cackle of a hen. 
"Why are you cackling so loudly over an egg?" a rooster asked. 
"Because this one is not an ordinary egg," the hen replied. "The one who eats the yolk of it is destined to be a king. The one who eats the white of it will become commander of the army. And the one who eats the shell will become a treasurer."

Pottik quietly picked up the egg. He cooked it, separated the parts, and served them: the yolk to Nigrodha, the white to Shakha, and he ate the shell himself. He then told them what the hen had said. Shakha was unhappy he didn't get the yolk but kept silent. 

They walked into Varanasi. The city was in mourning because its childless king had died seven days ago. The tradition was to send out a flower-decorated, riderless chariot; the one in front of whom the chariot stopped would be the new king. 
The three friends were resting in a park when the chariot arrived and stopped right in front of the sleeping Nigrodha. The royal priest examined his feet and saw the signs of an emperor. Nigrodha was promptly installed as the King of Varanasi. 

True to his friends, King Nigrodha named Shakha as his commander-in-chief and asked Pottik to stay with him in the palace. One day, Shakha asked Pottik to fetch his parents from Rajagriha. Pottik travelled there, but Shakha's parents refused to leave their business. Pottik's own parents also refused to move. 

Tired and out of money, Pottik returned to Varanasi and went to Shakha's grand house to rest. "Tell your master that his friend Pottik is here," he told the guard. 
But Shakha, arrogant with his new power, ordered his guards to beat Pottik and throw him out. 

Bruised and heartbroken, Pottik reached the king's palace late in the evening. Nigrodha welcomed him warmly. When Pottik recounted what Shakha had done, Nigrodha was furious. He summoned Shakha and ordered his execution for his ingratitude. 
But Pottik intervened. "No! Please don't, O King! It is not easy to bring anyone back to life... please forgive him."
Moved by Pottik's compassion, the king spared Shakha's life but banished him from the kingdom. He then offered Pottik the post of commander-in-chief, but Pottik refused, knowing his limitations. Instead, he accepted the role of Bhandagarik (treasurer), exactly as the magical egg had foretold, and the two true friends ruled wisely for many years.

## Kanni, The Unlucky (Kalakanni Jataka)
During the reign of Brahmadatta in Varanasi, there lived a prosperous merchant named Anathapindika. One day, a man named Kalakanni (meaning 'Unlucky') came to see him. They were childhood friends who had studied together at the Gurukula, but Kalakanni had fallen on hard times. 

Anathapindika warmly welcomed his friend. "You will stay with me and share my life," he insisted. Kalakanni happily agreed and helped Anathapindika in his business. 
However, Anathapindika's associates were perplexed. "How can you trust a stranger so much? And what an inauspicious name—Kalakanni! Even uttering such a name repeatedly might bring you bad luck."
"Bah! I don't believe in such things," Anathapindika dismissed their superstitions. 

After a few months, Anathapindika had to travel for a wedding. "I'll be away for a few days. You will look after my house and belongings," he told Kalakanni. 
Some thieves overheard this. "The rich Seth is leaving town. We'll burgle his house tonight," they plotted, thinking Kalakanni would be easy to overpower. 

But Kalakanni was clever. At dusk, he gathered all the servants. "Here! You blow loudly on the conch shell. You play the drum. You sing. The rest of us will clap. Make as much noise as you can!"
When the dacoits arrived at night, they heard the loud celebrations. "It sounds like a grand party. The Seth must have returned with a large crowd," they concluded, and fled in fear, leaving their weapons behind. 

The next morning, the servants found the weapons and footprints. When Anathapindika returned, he was told how Kalakanni's quick thinking had saved his wealth. 
"Of course, he is clever!" Anathapindika smiled at his associates. "Now do you realise why I value him so much? A man's worth is proven by his actions, not his name."

## A Monk in Need is a Friend Indeed (Tiritavachchha Jataka)
One day, King Brahmadatta of Varanasi and his son were discussing matters of state. Suddenly, a monk named Tiritavachchha arrived. The king immediately rushed out, welcomed him warmly, served him a royal feast in his private chambers, and ordered the Chief Minister to build a spacious dwelling for the monk in the palace compound. 

The young prince was puzzled. "Father, why do you give this man so much importance? He is not an expert in any art. He is neither your relative nor your friend. Our king treats him as if he were a brave warrior who has made a great conquest."

The king smiled. "Do you remember, son, two months ago, I went with the army to quell a rebellion? While leading our soldiers, my elephant ran in a frenzy and went deep into the forest. I was wounded and tired. Luckily, we reached an ashrama with a well. I tied a rope around the elephant's leg, climbed down the well, and drank my fill of cool water."

"But then," the king continued, "I found the walls of the well were too slippery. I couldn't climb back up. I was trapped."
"In the evening, the monk Tiritavachchha returned to his ashrama. Seeing the elephant, he looked into the well and rescued me. He then took me to his hut and cared for me for two days, treating my wounds with medicinal herbs and feeding me. Had he not rescued me and looked after me, I would not have survived."

The king looked at his son. "He saved my life, and I invited him to visit me in Varanasi. Don't you think he deserves to be my friend?"
"Yes, father," the prince realised his mistake. "Now I understand that you owe your life to him. Indeed, we must do all we can for him."
