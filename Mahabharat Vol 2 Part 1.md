# A Hall for Yudhishthira

After the Pandavas were recalled to Hastinapura and established their capital at Indraprastha, they expanded their power and vanquished many rival kings. The new city prospered, and the brothers lived in joyous harmony.

One warm day, Arjuna suggested to Krishna, "The days are warm, let us go to the Yamuna with our friends and return in the evening."

"That would please me too," Krishna smiled. 

With Yudhishthira's permission, Arjuna and Krishna set out with their friends to a verdant spot near the river. While the others regaled and amused themselves, the two heroes sat quietly. Suddenly, a Brahmana, whose visage was resplendent like burnished gold, approached them. Arjuna and Krishna rose hastily from their seats, awaiting his commands.

"I am a Brahmana whose hunger is not easily satiated," the radiant man said. "I beg of you to give me my fill of food this once."

"Tell us what food will give you contentment, and we shall strive to procure it," Arjuna replied.

The Brahmana's form shifted, blazing with intense light. "I do not partake of ordinary food. I am Agni, the god of fire! I need food that befits me. I wish to consume the Khandava forest, but it is always guarded by Indra because his friend Takshaka, the Naga, lives there. Whenever I try to burn it, Indra wields his thunderbolt and pours torrents of rain to thwart me." Agni looked at them expectantly. "With your help, I shall consume the forest. This is the meal I wish to have from you."

Arjuna considered the request. "I have with me many marvellous weapons to fight any number of wielders of the thunderbolt," he told Agni. "But I have no bow to match my strength and dexterity. Besides, I need an inexhaustible supply of arrows for the speed with which I shoot. My chariot cannot bear the weight of the missiles I wish to store in it. I need a chariot potent like the sun, drawn by storm-swift steeds. Furthermore, there is no weapon with Krishna which matches his immense prowess."

Agni smiled and closed his eyes, invoking Varuna, the presiding deity of water and a guardian of the universe. Varuna appeared instantly. 

"Hasten and give me the bow, the ever-full quiver, and the chariot marked by its banner carrying the emblem of the ape," Agni requested. "Also, bring the discus for Krishna."

Varuna presented Arjuna with the legendary Gandeeva bow, an unassailable weapon revered by celestials. He also gave him an inexhaustible quiver and a magnificent chariot yoked to white steeds, bearing the emblem of Hanuman on its banner. Arjuna joyfully strung the Gandeeva; the terrifying rumble of the bow sent a shiver of fear among all who heard it. 

To Krishna, Agni gave the Sudarshana Chakra, a divine, fiery discus whose center was hard as agate. Varuna then presented Krishna with the mighty mace Kaumodaki. 

Fully armed, Arjuna and Krishna rode their chariots to either side of the forest. "O Lord, devour all sides of this forest as you deem fit," they announced. "We are at hand to assist you."

Agni blazed forth in magnificent flames, surrounding the Khandava forest and consuming it with a deafening roar. The water-spots began to boil, and leaping flames rose to the sky, striking fear into the hearts of the celestials. 

When Indra beheld the massive clouds of smoke, he covered the skies with multitudinous rain clouds and began raining down showers as thick as chariot axles. But the scorching heat of Agni's fire dried the rain in mid-air. An enraged Indra gathered even more massive clouds for a heavier downpour, filling the forest with blinding lightning and thunder.

Arjuna quickly released countless arrows, setting up a perfect blockade that closed off the skies, preventing a single drop of rain from putting out the fire or allowing any creature to escape. 

While the forest burned, Takshaka the Naga was absent, but his mighty son Ashwasena was trapped inside. Indra, keen on saving his friend's son, raised a terrible storm that struck Arjuna unconscious for a brief moment, allowing Ashwasena to escape. 

Piqued by the trickery, Arjuna covered the skies with his arrows and carried the fight straight to Indra. A fierce battle erupted in the firmament, stirring up howling gales and raging oceans. The celestials were thwarted time and again until they retreated in fear. In desperation, Indra uprooted a massive peak of the Mandara mountain and hurled it at Arjuna, but Arjuna's fire-spitting arrows smashed it to smithereens. 

Then, a deep, grave voice from the heavens addressed Indra. "Your friend Takshaka is not in there. He is fated to survive. Know Krishna and Arjuna to be invincible in battle. Therefore, it is proper that you turn back with your retinue." Beholding Indra's retreat, the celestials fled. 

Agni consumed the forest for a whole fortnight. As the fire raged, Krishna and Arjuna noticed a terrified figure running from Takshaka's burning abode. Agni pursued him closely. 

"Arjuna! Come quick and save me!" the fleeing figure cried out. 

"Fear not!" Arjuna yelled back, granting him asylum. Because of Arjuna's promise, neither Krishna's discus nor Agni's flames harmed him. 

The survivor fell to his knees in profound gratitude. "You saved me from the anger of Krishna and the flames of Agni," he gasped. "Is there anything I can do for you? I am Maya, Vishwakarma of the Asuras, the master architect. I wish to be of some service."

"You have our goodwill," Arjuna smiled. "We need nothing more."

But Maya insisted. Urged by Maya, Krishna thought for a moment and said, "Build a hall for Yudhishthira. Make it a mansion men will marvel at. Make it unique."

Maya was thrilled with the assignment. He journeyed to the Kailasa mountain, where he had stored many rare and precious materials. From a hidden lake, he retrieved a fierce mace encrusted with gold knobs for Bheema, and the massive Devadatta conch-shell for Arjuna. He also gathered flawless crystal and dark, huge gems. 

Returning to Indraprastha, Maya began constructing a magnificent, inimitable palace. He designed walls encrusted with precious stones and columns of solid gold. Within the palace grounds, he designed a breathtaking pond; the banks were set in marble slabs decorated with pearls, with a flight of crystal steps leading into the water. He placed lifelike lotuses made of gems, and artificial fish and tortoises that looked incredibly real. The water itself was so transparently clear that it was indistinguishable from the crystal floors. 

It took fourteen months to complete the wondrous mansion. Yudhishthira and his brothers made a ceremonial entry, astounded by the heavenly abode. The palace was guarded by eight thousand Rakshasas known as Kinkaras, placed there by Maya. 

One day, while the Pandavas were assembled in their new palace, the great Sage Narada arrived. Yudhishthira welcomed him with all honors. After admiring the hall, Narada spoke to Yudhishthira. 

"A king who conscientiously preserves the order of hierarchy and discharges his duties will lead a happy life and enter the domain of Indra," Narada advised. "But I must tell you that he, with the help of his brothers, can conquer the world and embark upon the grand Rajasooya Yajna."

Narada's words planted the seed of a grand ambition in Yudhishthira's mind: to become the Emperor of the world.


# The Pandavas Conquer the World

After Sage Narada's visit, Yudhishthira became careworn, his thoughts consumed by the monumental undertaking of the Rajasooya Yajna. He summoned his counsellors and brothers, seeking their views. 

"You are eminently qualified to perform the Yajna," his counsellors agreed. "And we are here at your command. Let your plans be made forthwith."

However, Yudhishthira remained cautious. "As one who treads the path of righteousness, you, O King, are qualified," he reasoned to himself, "but it is an Emperor who is worthy of this Yajna. I must seek Krishna's guidance." 

He sent a fast chariot to bring Krishna from Dwaraka. When Krishna arrived, Yudhishthira explained his ambitions. "All my friends say that I should perform the Rajasooya. But, O Krishna, some through their very affection fail to see my shortcomings. Others give encouragement for selfish reasons. You are beyond such motives. Tell me what I should do."

Krishna smiled warmly. "O great King, you have all the qualities of an Emperor. But I perceive that you will not accomplish the Rajasooya as long as the mighty Jarasandha of Magadha is alive. By the grace of Shiva, he has acquired immense strength and has vanquished and imprisoned many kings to fulfill his vow of slaying them in sacrifice. He has captured eighty-six kings and needs fourteen more to start his cruel ritual."

"How can I send you, O Krishna, to Jarasandha merely because I covet the status of an Emperor?" Yudhishthira hesitated, deeply concerned for Krishna's safety. "Life without you three—Krishna, Arjuna, and Bheema—is meaningless for me. The Rajasooya Yajna appears ill-omened; it oppresses my mind."

"If we are slain in battle, we shall ascend to heaven as our goal is the protection of kings," Bheema declared fearlessly. "I have the strength, and Arjuna the will to win. If we three approach him in private, he will certainly agree to a duel with us."

"Jarasandha cannot be overcome in battle, even by the Devas and Asuras," Krishna agreed. "But I see his destruction as imminent. I think he should be vanquished in single combat with bare hands. Bheema who can send him to his death."

Seeing the brightened faces of Bheema and Arjuna, Yudhishthira finally relented. "O Krishna, pray do not speak thus. You are our support. If you know my mind, then entrust them to me. Now that I have your guidance, I can foresee the death of Jarasandha."

Disguising themselves as Snataka (learned) Brahmanas, Krishna, Bheema, and Arjuna set out for Magadha. They crossed rivers and mountains until they reached the hills of Goratha, overlooking Girivraja, the capital of Magadha. 

"Behold, Arjuna!" Krishna pointed. "Girivraja, guarded by that chain of five hills."

Instead of entering through the regular city gates, the three heroes boldly climbed the massive and venerable mountain peak called Chaityaka and razed it to the ground. Then, entering the city, they forcibly took flower garlands from a vendor and marched directly to Jarasandha's palace. 

Jarasandha noticed the ill omens of the destroyed peak and began sacrificial rituals to ward off evil. When the three "Brahmanas," covered in sandal paste and bright garlands, approached him, he stood up to receive them with honor. 

"I welcome you," Jarasandha said, though his eyes narrowed in suspicion. "But your attire of garlands and sandal-paste is not usual for Snataka Brahmanas. You bear the marks of the bow-string on your arms. Who are you? Why did you enter through an irregular gate, and why do you refuse my worship?"

"Brave men enter the abode of an enemy through an irregular gate," Krishna replied in a grave voice. "We have entered the abode of our enemy with a purpose. Therefore we do not accept the worship. You have imprisoned many Kshatriyas, having committed such atrocities, how do you call yourself innocent? We are here to subdue you."

Jarasandha laughed scornfully. "I have imprisoned none who has not been vanquished by me in battle. Is there a king here not defeated by me? To subdue the enemy by valour is moral for a Kshatriya. I shall fight! Is it to be single combat with each one of you, or a battle between our armies?"

"O King, whom will you choose for single combat from among us?" Krishna asked. 

"I will fight with you," Jarasandha pointed at Bheema. "It is better to go down fighting a full man."

Jarasandha removed his crown and stood ready. He rushed at Bheema, and the two mighty heroes, discarding weapons, engaged in a terrifying wrestling match. They fought with bare hands, locking and dragging each other, their blows sounding like thunderclaps. Sparks flew from their foreheads as they clashed. 

The fierce combat raged without a break for fourteen days. The citizens of Magadha thronged to witness the spectacle, watching the two colossuses trying to crush each other's skulls and hit each other's chests. 

On the fourteenth day, Krishna noticed Jarasandha falling behind in fatigue. "An exhausted foe is not to be pressed hard," Krishna reminded Bheema loudly. "He might even lose his life. Therefore do not be hard on him. Fight him if you will, but matching his reduced might."

Bheema perfectly understood Krishna's hidden meaning: the time had come for Jarasandha's death. 

"This wretch who dared me is not to be spared," Bheema roared. He lifted Jarasandha high into the air, whirled him around a hundred times, and dashed him forcefully to the ground. Then, pressing his knees against the fallen foe, Bheema ruthlessly broke his back and tore his body in two. The triumphant roar of Bheema mingled with the dying shriek of Jarasandha, striking terror in the hearts of the citizens.

The heroes immediately rushed to the dungeons and set the imprisoned kings free. The liberated kings, overjoyed, bowed down to Krishna and the Pandavas, pledging their eternal support. Krishna installed Jarasandha's large-hearted son, Sahadeva, as the new King of Magadha, assuring him of safe conduct and protection. 

Triumphant, Krishna, Bheema, and Arjuna returned to Indraprastha in Jarasandha's own magnificent chariot. Yudhishthira was overjoyed to see them unharmed and worshipped Krishna for his guidance. 

With Jarasandha gone, the path to the Rajasooya Yajna was clear. However, Yudhishthira's treasury needed to be augmented to host such a grand event. He commanded his brothers to go forth and extract tributes from the surrounding kingdoms. 

Arjuna, with his Miraculous chariot and Gandeeva bow, subdued the North. Bheema conquered the East, Sahadeva conquered the South, and Nakula subjugated the West. Under Yudhishthira's just rule, even cheats and thieves spoke no lies, and the kingdom's political stability and wealth prospered immensely. 

The brothers returned with immense bounties. Yudhishthira's treasury was filled with such wealth that it was enough to last hundreds of years. The stage was finally set for the grandest undertaking of their lives: the Rajasooya Yajna.


# Yudhishthira's Rajasooya Yajna

Because of the protection provided by the just ruler Yudhishthira and the subjugation of their enemies, the people of Indraprastha could go about their work freely. The virtuous rule rained abundantly in his land, and it became remarkably prosperous. Having in mind the vastness of his treasures, which seemed inexhaustible, Yudhishthira said to himself, "I must perform the Rajasooya Yajna."

His well-wishers and the revered Sage Vyasa agreed. "O Lord! The appropriate time has arrived for you to perform the Rajasooya Yajna," Vyasa told him. 

Yudhishthira respectfully welcomed Krishna, who also gave his full support. "O tiger among kings, you deserve to be Emperor," Krishna praised him. "It is proper that you perform the Yajna. Tell me what I must do, and I shall do your bidding."

Having received Krishna's consent, Sahadeva, along with his brothers, set about collecting the necessary materials. "O Sahadeva, procure everything that Sage Dhaumya and the Brahmanas require," Yudhishthira ordered. Arjuna's charioteers—Indrasena, Vishok, and Pooru—were dispatched to collect immense quantities of food grains. 

Messengers were sent with speedy invitations to all honorable Brahmanas, Kings, Vaishyas, and Shoodras of the land. Rulers from different kingdoms arrived, including King Drupada and the valiant King Shishupala of Chedi, along with his son. 

Yudhishthira assigned specific duties to ensure the grand event's success. Duhshasana was tasked with feeding the vast crowds of invitees. Sanjaya was appointed to oversee protocol. The safe-keeping of diamonds, gold, gems, and the distribution of gifts were entrusted to Kripa. The great and wise Bheeshma and Drona were asked to ensure that all assigned duties were carried out properly. Sage Vyasa himself assumed the role of the chief priest of the Yajna.

On the day of the ceremonial bath, the Brahmanas and kings entered the inner quarters of the sacrificial mansion. Graced by the presence of Sage Narada and saintly kings, the assembly was as glorious as the assembly of Brahma. 

Bheeshma addressed Yudhishthira regarding the crucial protocol of honoring the guests. "O Bharata, offer suitable worship to the kings who deserve your respect. The preceptor, the priest, the kin, those who have studied the Vedas, friends, and kings—these six men are said to deserve worship. Offer libations to each of them, beginning with the one who is foremost among them."

"O Grandsire," Yudhishthira asked respectfully, "who do you deem fit to receive the first libation?"

Bheeshma did not hesitate. "As the sun is the most brilliant amongst all luminous objects, so is Krishna amongst us all. Therefore, let the first libation be offered to Krishna."

Having obtained Bheeshma's consent, Sahadeva offered the first libation to Krishna in accordance with the Shastras. Krishna accepted the honor gracefully. 

But King Shishupala of Chedi could not bear to witness this. He stood up, furious. "O Pandava, Krishna does not deserve to receive this singular honour when so many great lords of the earth are present here!" he shouted. "You are mere children, so you do not know the subtleties of Dharma. How does this Yadava deserve precedence over the kings, when he is not even a king?"

Shishupala gestured dismissively at Krishna. "If you revere Krishna considering him to be an elder, what of his father Vasudeva? If you regard him as a preceptor, how does he deserve worship in the presence of Drona? If you believe him to be a priest, how does he deserve worship in the presence of Sage Vyasa? O Bharata, if your intention was to worship Krishna, then why were these monarchs invited here to be insulted thus?"

Shishupala turned his ire towards Krishna directly. "How could you accept it when you are not worthy of it? Like an undeserving dog lazing away in a corner lapping up the leavings of sacrificial offerings, you probably believe you have gained superiority!"

Yudhishthira rushed after Shishupala to pacify him. "O Lord of men, your utterances are not worthy of you. They are improper and harsh. It is not conceivable that Bheeshma does not know what is proper."

Bheeshma remained calm. "It is futile to plead with one who does not appreciate the honour bestowed upon Krishna," he told Yudhishthira. "He who is the patriarch of all the worlds... I see no king who has not been subdued by the glory of Krishna. Among the Brahmanas those mature in wisdom are honoured, but among Kshatriyas those superior in might are worshipped. Krishna is supreme in both wisdom and might." Bheeshma praised Krishna as the origin and the final dissolution of the universe. 

"If Shishupala believes that this worship has been improper," Bheeshma concluded, "then he may do what he deems fit."

Sahadeva stepped forward, his eyes blazing. "I will trample upon the heads of those who cannot tolerate my worship of Krishna!" he declared, raising his foot. The celestials hailed his action and showered flowers upon him. "Well done! Well done!"

Many kings' faces darkened with anger, their eyes bloodshot with rage. "O kings, I am standing here to lead you," Shishupala incited them. "Let us fight with Krishna and the Pandavas!"

The Chedi king urged the angry monarchs to defile the Yajna. Beholding the restless ocean of kings, Yudhishthira asked Bheeshma for guidance. 

"Fear not, O tiger of the Kurus," Bheeshma reassured him. "Can a dog slay a lion? Indeed, the minds of those whom Krishna desires to destroy become unsettled like that of the Chedi king. The feeble-minded Shishupala wants to carry all the kings with him to the abode of Yama, the God of Death."

Listening to these words, Shishupala addressed Bheeshma with profound disrespect. "O Bheeshma, why do you tarnish the name of your race? It is not proper that you frighten the kings thus... O degenerate Bheeshma, perhaps you have not heard these words of the learned: The valiant never strike a woman, a cow, a Brahmana, and those whose food they have eaten."

Shishupala sneered, mocking Krishna's legendary feats. "What is so astonishing about Krishna who slew a horse or a bull? These animals did not possess the skill of combat! He killed the mighty Kamsa whose bread he broke. So how does one who has killed a woman, and a cow, become worthy of worship?"

Hearing these rude words, the valorous Bheema was seized with uncontrollable anger. Blood descended into his eyes as he rushed at Shishupala like Death incarnate. Bheeshma held the raging Bheema in check. 

"Release him, O Bheeshma!" Shishupala laughed confidently. "Let the kings witness him perish like a moth in the fire of my glory."

Bheeshma, retaining his matchless wisdom, turned to the assembly. "The moment Shishupala was born, he brayed like a donkey. He was born with three eyes and four arms. The terrified parents decided to abandon the child. Then an incorporeal voice told them: 'Your son will acquire glory and great strength. Have no fear. He will not die of your efforts... but his destroyer has also been born.' The voice further prophesied that he would be slain by the man on whose lap the child's extra limbs fell off and his third eye vanished."

Bheeshma pointed at Krishna. "When Krishna, along with Balarama, came to visit Shishupala's mother—their father's sister—the queen placed her son on Krishna's lap. The very next moment, the child's extra limbs fell off and his third eye vanished. The distressed queen pleaded for a boon: 'O mighty-armed Krishna, grant me a boon. Always pardon Shishupala's offenses.' Krishna replied: 'Even when your son deserves to be slain, I shall forgive a hundred of his misdeeds.'"

Bheeshma concluded, "Otherwise, who on earth would dare abuse me? Who other than this king is a disgrace to his race? Undoubtedly, the Chedi king does not act of his own accord. He is impelled by Krishna, the Lord of the world. Shishupala is but a part of the living force of Hari who desires to deprive him of it."

Incensed by Bheeshma's words, the reckless Chedi king challenged Krishna directly. "I challenge you, O Krishna! Fight me so that I slay you and the Pandavas!"

Krishna finally addressed the Pandavas and all the kings. "Shishupala is our greatest enemy. Once, when we had gone to Pragjyotishapura, this brute, despite being my father's nephew, set fire to Dwaraka. This dissolute stole the sacrificial horse of my father... I quietly bore these insults out of concern for my father's sister. He has done me harm indirectly too... driven by the wish to die, wanted to possess Rukmini."

The kings condemned Shishupala's misdeeds, but Shishupala only laughed. "O Krishna, do you not feel ashamed of speaking thus about Rukmini whose hand had been promised to me?"

"I have forgiven him a hundred misdeeds, according to my promise to his mother," Krishna announced, his voice carrying undeniable finality. "Now I shall slay him."

While Shishupala was uttering further insults, Lord Krishna calmly raised his hand. He summoned his divine weapon, the Sudarshana Chakra. With blinding speed, the blazing discus shot across the hall and beheaded Shishupala where he stood. 

Shishupala fell to the ground like a mountain uprooted by Indra's thunderbolt. A brilliant light emerged from his fallen body, merged with Krishna, and vanished. The moment Krishna slew the Chedi king, rain inexplicably poured from a cloudless sky, meteors fell, and the earth trembled. 

In due time, Yudhishthira completed the great Rajasooya Yajna without further interruption. Protected by the mighty-armed Krishna, the Yajna was a magnificent success. When the rituals were completed, all the kings departed for their kingdoms, taking their leave of the newly crowned Emperor. 

Only Duryodhana and his scheming uncle Shakuni remained, choosing to stay at Indraprastha for some more time, their hearts already consumed by the dark seeds of envy.
