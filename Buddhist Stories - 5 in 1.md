# Buddha

In the Himalayan foothills, Kapilavastu was a small but prosperous kingdom. The Sakyas ruled over it, and Suddhodana was their king. 

One day, his queen, Mayadevi, dreamt that a white elephant with six tusks pierced her womb. Ten months later, the queen was on her way to her father's house. As she was passing through a beautiful grove on the way to Lumbini, she commanded, "Stop! I would like to spend some time here."
The queen got down and started walking towards a majestic *sala* tree in the middle of the garden. Suddenly, she stopped. "I am in great pain. I must rest."
There, a baby was born to her. It was the full moon night of Vaisakha. There was profound silence all around. 

On hearing the joyous news, the king rushed to Lumbini and brought the mother and the child to the palace. The revered sage Asita came to the palace to see the baby. 
As the sage gazed upon the infant, tears filled his eyes. 
"I see tears in your eyes, sage Asita. Why are you unhappy?" King Suddhodana asked anxiously. 
"This boy will be a king of kings... or a great saint," Asita prophesied. "I am crying because I will not live to hear his great words."

Both the king and the queen were happy. On the fifth day, the king said, "Let us name him Siddhartha."
"And we will see that he becomes a king of kings and not a saint," the queen added. 
Immediately afterwards, the queen became seriously ill. On the seventh day after the birth of Siddhartha, she called for her sister. "Sister Prajapati, I shall soon leave this world. When I am gone, please be a kind mother to Siddhartha. Promise me."
"I promise," Prajapati wept. 

After a year had passed, the king said, "You have been a good mother to him, Prajapati."
"I love him dearly. I am proud to be his mother."

As Siddhartha grew, the king became increasingly anxious about the prophecy. "He spends so much time alone, meditating under that jambu tree. I don't like that."
"He says his playmates play cruel games," Prajapati explained. 

One day, as Siddhartha was walking along in the garden, a wounded swan fell from the sky. "Poor bird! I wonder whose arrow has hurt him," Siddhartha cried softly. He gently removed the arrow and tended to the bird's wound. 
Some time later, Devadatta, Siddhartha's cousin, arrived with his bow. "This is my bird. I shot it! Give it to me."
"Devadatta! It is such a lovely bird. Why do you wish to harm it?" Siddhartha asked. 
"The bird belongs to me. Give it back."
"I won't do that."

The matter was taken to the court. "Prince! What right have you to keep the bird?" Devadatta demanded. 
"Sir," Siddhartha addressed the judges, "if I had not removed the arrow, the bird would have died. The bird owes its life to me. Should the bird belong to the one who took its life or to the one who gave it life?"
"What the boy says is right," the head judge proclaimed. "The one who saved the life of the bird has a greater right. Devadatta, the bird belongs to the prince."
"Thank you, noble judges," Siddhartha smiled. 

Years rolled by. Dandapani, a Sakyan noble, arranged the *swayamvara* of his daughter, Yashodhara. Princes from far and near came to the event. Princess Yashodhara chose Siddhartha as her husband and placed the garland around his neck. 

The assembled princes were deeply hurt by Yashodhara's choice. They approached Dandapani. "Sir, the princess should not be married to Siddhartha. He is not a good fighter," one complained. 
"Sir, you should hold a test in archery. The princess should be given in marriage to the winner."

Dandapani tried to persuade his daughter. "Yashodhara! Choose someone else as your husband."
"Father, I have made my choice. Please agree to it," she replied firmly. 

When the news reached King Suddhodana, he felt sad. 
"Father, why are you sad?" Siddhartha asked. 
"The people don't think you are a good warrior."
"Father, let Dandapani hold a test. I will take part in it."
"I am happy to hear that. Your ancestors were great warriors, Siddhartha," the king beamed. 

Many gathered to witness the test. To everyone's astonishment, Siddhartha excelled in everything. "It is Siddhartha who has won in horse-riding!" the crowds cheered. "No one could equal him in archery too!"
Siddhartha and Yashodhara were married with great pomp. The king tried his best to provide all the comforts of life to Siddhartha, isolating him in a luxurious palace. 

Soon, a son was born to them. King Suddhodana was immensely pleased when he heard the news. "Lord! It is a boy!" the messenger announced. 
"Good! Now Siddhartha is tied to worldly life and will never think of becoming a saint," the king thought. 

One day, Siddhartha approached his father. "Father, I would like to go out of the palace and see more of the world."
"I will order a chariot for you. After it is ready, you can go out in it," the king agreed cautiously. He ordered all sights of unhappiness to be cleared from the streets. 

A few days later, in a beautiful chariot with four horses driven by his trusted charioteer Channa, Siddhartha drove through the streets of the city. "How handsome the prince is," the people whispered. "I have heard he is brave and fearless."

But as he drove further, Siddhartha saw an unexpected sight. "Channa, who is this? His head is white. He seems very weak. His skin is wrinkled."
"He is an old man, master! He is bent with age."
"Does everyone get old, Channa?"
"Yes, my lord! Everyone has to grow old."
Siddhartha became deeply troubled. "Will my Yashodhara also grow old? Will my strength go away with years?"

On another day's ride, he saw a man wailing by the roadside. "What is the matter with this man?"
"He is ill, my lord! He is crying with pain," Channa replied. 
"Is disease peculiar to him?"
"No, my lord! Anyone may fall ill in his lifetime."
"Even I?"
"Yes, master, even you."

Still another day, he saw a procession carrying a body wrapped in white cloth. "Why are they carrying that man, Channa?"
"He is dead, my lord!"
"Is he the only dead man? Or do others also die?"
"Everyone who is born has to die some day."
"I feel sick, Channa," Siddhartha whispered. "Let us return to the palace."

On the way back, he saw a man sitting peacefully under a tree. "Stop, Channa! Who is that? He looks calm and different from all the other men I have seen."
"He is a saint. He has given up a life of pleasure and pain in search of truth."

At the palace, Yashodhara noticed his distress. "Siddhartha! Please tell me the cause of your unhappiness!"
"Mother," Siddhartha later told Prajapati, "I have learnt that all things alive and beautiful keep changing. Men grow old. Men fall ill and die. I feel unhappy when I think of these things."

In the middle of the night, he finally made his decision. "I must find a way to end sorrow. I will go in search of truth, like that saint."
He woke his charioteer. "Channa, get my horse ready! I wish to ride out."
"Yes, master!"

Before leaving, the prince returned to the bedroom of his wife. Yashodhara and their infant child, Rahula, were fast asleep. "I can't kiss Rahula goodbye; that will awaken Yashodhara. I must go."
Siddhartha mounted his horse and rode out into the night, accompanied only by Channa. 

Once they were safely outside the city, Siddhartha got down from the horse. "Channa, take all my jewels and return to Kapilavastu."
He then cut off his long royal hair and walked off alone. 

Later, he saw a beggar on the road. "Halt, my good man! Take my rich clothes and give me yours."
"With pleasure, master!" the beggar agreed happily. 

Soon he reached Rajagriha, the capital of Magadha. He went from house to house silently waiting till the people offered him food. King Bimbisara observed him from his palace. "Look at that saint. He looks so handsome. Find out who he is."

As soon as the messengers brought news about Siddhartha's true identity, King Bimbisara personally went to meet him. "You seem to belong to a noble family. Your hand should not hold a begging bowl, but the reins of an empire. I will give you a high position in my kingdom. Come."
"O king, you are kind," Siddhartha replied gently. "But I cannot accept your invitation. I feel that life is full of sorrow. I wish to find a way to end all sorrow."
"If that is your wish, I pray that you find it. Please come and teach me when you have found the solution," Bimbisara requested respectfully. 

From Rajagriha, Siddhartha went in search of the great sages of those days. Not satisfied with their teachings, he entered the thick jungles of Urubilva, near modern-day Gaya. 

There were five hermits meditating in the jungles. When they saw him, they thought, "This man is keen on his goal. He is sure to succeed. Let us wait on him."
Siddhartha practiced extreme asceticism. He drank only water and ate only fruits and herbs. He slept on the hard ground. After some time, he started eating only one hemp grain every day. This made him dangerously weak. One day, when he had gone to bathe in the river, he realized, "I feel weak. I don't have strength to get up." He caught hold of a low branch of a tree and barely raised himself with its support. But as he began to walk away from the bank, he felt faint and collapsed. Slowly, he managed to get up. 

"I have come to believe that starving does not help in reaching the truth," he realized. 
The next day, as he sat beneath a banyan tree, Sujata, the daughter of a herdsman, came to that spot. She respectfully offered a bowl of nourishing food to Siddhartha. "Thank you for feeding me," he said. 

Later, the hermits confronted him. "How is it that you have started eating food again?"
"Starving the body is not going to help," Siddhartha explained. 
The five ascetics were deeply disappointed. "He does not deserve our respect. Yes, he wants the pleasures of life," they muttered, and abandoned him. 

Siddhartha now lived a lonely life. "They have left me. Let them go. But starving the body is not going to help. I am now sure of that."
He moved towards a great Bodhi tree and sat beneath it. "Come what may, I shall not move from this seat till I find a way to end sorrow."
Heat and cold, hunger and thirst troubled him. Rain lashed on him. Visions of the life of pleasure he had abandoned floated before his eyes. But nothing could tempt him. 

And then, in deep meditation, he finally saw the light. 
"I know. I know the truth now. The way to end sorrow is found."
Siddhartha became the Buddha, the Enlightened One. 

He spent seven weeks under the tree enjoying his state of perfect happiness and enlightenment. Then he went out into the world to teach others. First, he went to Banaras, in search of the five ascetics who had abandoned him in Urubilva. He found them in Deer Park. 
"Here comes Siddhartha," the ascetics scoffed. "He returned to a life of ease. Let us not talk to him."
But as soon as he came near, his radiant presence overwhelmed them. They got up and received him with deep respect. 
"I have come to tell you what I have found. Listen!" the Buddha said. "There is great sorrow in this world. This sorrow is because of desire. If you can free yourself from desire, you will be free from sorrow. I will show you the way to remove sorrow from the mind."
When they heard the Buddha, they became his first disciples. The *Sangha* (community of monks) thus came into being. 

After that, he returned to Urubilva and went to the house of Kasshyapa, a great Brahman who was a worshipper of Agni, the God of Fire. 
"What do you want?" Kasshyapa asked. 
"I want to spend a night here," Buddha replied. 
"You are welcome. Please come in."
"May I stay in the room where you keep the sacred fire?"
"Haven't you heard that the sacred fire is guarded by a fierce serpent at night? The serpent will bite you if you go near the fire."
"I am not afraid. Please allow me to spend the night there."

At last, Kasshyapa agreed. Buddha sat silently meditating before the fire. Kasshyapa went to sleep outside. 
Early next morning, Kasshyapa thought, "Let me go and look for him. He must be dead. Poor man!"
But when he went inside the room, he saw Buddha sitting perfectly peacefully. The light from the fire shone beautifully on his serene face. 
Awed by his spiritual power, Kasshyapa bowed. "I accept you as my master. Teach me."
Buddha taught Kasshyapa and many others in Urubilva. 

One day, a disciple informed him, "Master, I have heard that King Bimbisara will be celebrating a great animal yagna."
"Let us go to Rajagriha," Buddha decided. 

On their way to Rajagriha, they saw a large herd of sheep being driven along. There was a lame lamb struggling to keep up. Buddha gently lifted it in his arms. "Poor thing, it must have been suffering a lot of pain. Good man, where are you going?" he asked the shepherd. 
"I am going to Rajagriha. These sheep belong to the king. They are going to be sacrificed in the yagna—in the sacred fire."

King Bimbisara heard that Buddha was on his way to Rajagriha. He went forward with his ministers to meet him. Seeing him with Kasshyapa, Bimbisara thought, "He is in the company of Urubilva Kasshyapa. Does that mean Buddha is his disciple?"

When they came near, Buddha gently placed the lamb down. "You had expressed the wish to see me after I had found the way. Here I am," Buddha said to the king. 
"Holy one! I am about to begin a yagna. Great Kasshyapa and you are also welcome to take part in it."
"O king, killing of innocent animals cannot be a good deed. The way to happiness does not lie in yagna," Buddha advised softly. 

The king turned to Kasshyapa. "What has the great Kasshyapa to say? You are known as a fierce worshipper of fire."
Kasshyapa stepped forward and bowed deeply to Buddha. "I am a disciple of Buddha."
Bimbisara was astounded. "I see Buddha is the teacher! Don't you also believe in yagna?"
"Buddha has shown me the right way. Neither the worship of fire nor the sacrifice of dumb animals can make us free from sorrow."

The king turned to Buddha respectfully. "Master, let us hear your words of wisdom."
And Buddha taught them the path of peace and compassion. 
"Lord, I have given up the idea of performing the yagna. I take refuge in Buddha," Bimbisara declared. 
The next day, the king invited Buddha and his disciples for a royal meal at his palace. "Lord, I give Venuvana, my garden, as a gift to the Sangha. Please accept it."

One day, a grief-stricken woman named Krisha Gotami brought her dead child before Buddha. "Master, please bring it back to life. It is my only child."
"Control your grief," Buddha said gently. "Do as I say. Get me a few mustard seeds from any house where no death has taken place. And I will bring back to life your child."
Full of hope, Krisha Gotami went from house to house. 
"I lost my husband last year!" one woman wept. 
"I lost my two children," another sighed. 
"My mother died yesterday."
Disappointed, Krisha Gotami came back to Buddha. "Lord! I could not find a single place where no death has occurred."
"My child, all that is born, must die one day. There is ultimately nothing but sorrow in life. It is freedom from desire that frees us from sorrow." Realizing the universal truth, she found peace. 

After a few years, at Suddhodana's court in Kapilavastu, a noble informed the aging king, "Lord! Our prince has become famous. Many have become his disciples. They are spreading his teachings everywhere."
"Go to Siddhartha and tell him that I am growing old and wish to see him before I die," Suddhodana commanded. 

When Buddha reached Kapilavastu, the king greeted him emotionally. "Welcome my son! I wish you would become king."
"I am sorry. I have chosen the path of peace."
The king arranged for Buddha's stay in a grove nearby. The next morning, the king was horrified to hear a report. "Lord, the prince is going from house to house to receive alms in the city!!"
Suddhodana rushed out to confront him. "My son, must you beg?"
"But it is our custom."
"What do you mean? You are descended from a proud line of kings!"
"O great king, you claim descent from kings. My descent is from the Buddhas of old. They always begged for their food and lived on alms."

Once they reached the palace, Buddha spoke softly to all those that had gathered. His words brought peace to their hearts. Buddha noticed that his wife, Yashodhara, was not present. 
"Where is Yashodhara?" he asked. 
"She has refused to come," Prajapati explained. "She has been very unhappy ever since you left. She has cut her hair, wears simple clothes, and eats sparingly just as you did."
Accompanied by two disciples, Buddha went to Yashodhara's chamber. "I must try to heal the sorrow in her heart."
When Yashodhara saw Buddha, she broke down, fell at his feet, and wept. Then, remembering that others were present, she got up and sat at a little distance. Buddha spoke gentle words of comfort, easing her long pain. 

A week later, Yashodhara pointed out a monk to her young son. "Rahula, do you see the man sitting there in the centre? He is your father. Go to him and ask for your share of his property."
Rahula walked up to Buddha. "Father! My mother sent me to ask you for my share of your property."
Buddha turned to Sariputra, his chief disciple. "My son asks for his inheritance. Well then, take him in the Sangha."

After Rahula joined the Sangha, many young men of the royal Sakyan family also joined. Amongst them was Buddha's jealous cousin, Devadatta. He also moved about with Buddha. One day, Devadatta ambitiously suggested, "Master, you should rest. I shall lead the Sangha."
"No, Devadatta. The Sangha still needs my guidance," Buddha refused. 

Devadatta felt incredibly jealous. He went to Rajagriha and met Prince Ajatasatru, son of King Bimbisara. "Prince Ajatasatru! How long can you wait to become a king? Put your father in prison and be a king yourself."
Ajatasatru agreed. After imprisoning his father, he said, "It is nice to be a king. Thank you, Devadatta! If you want my help in anything, ask for it."
"I need your help, King Ajatasatru. Help me kill Buddha. I hate him."

Devadatta tried many ways to assassinate the Buddha. Once, as Buddha was meditating, a disciple yelled, "Look out! A big stone is rolling down towards Buddha."
"Sit where you are. No harm will come to me," Buddha said calmly. The heavy rock magically split into two, and the pieces fell harmlessly on either side of Buddha. 
Many of Devadatta's own followers witnessed this miracle and came to join the Sangha of Buddha. "The rock did not kill him. He looks frail, but he is great. Let us go to him," they said. 

Ajatasatru was furious. "I hear many of my followers have joined his Sangha. Why not set an elephant on him?"
He ordered an elephant keeper, "Intoxicate the massive elephant Nalagiri, and then let him loose in the path of Buddha."
The drunken beast caused havoc. "Run, run, Nalagiri is free! He has ruined half the town already. Twenty men have been killed!"
As Nalagiri charged down the street where Buddha was walking, the disciples panicked. "Master, let us hide. A wild elephant is coming this way."
"No, he won't harm us," Buddha said firmly. 
Nalagiri came thumping along and rushed towards Buddha in a mad fury. Buddha simply smiled and raised his hand peacefully. The raging elephant instantly calmed down and knelt reverently at his feet. 

Years later, Ajatasatru became an unhappy, guilt-ridden man and fell ill often. He asked his wise physician, Jivaka, "Jivaka, why do I suffer?"
"Your ailment is not physical but spiritual. Only Buddha can help you."
"Let us go to him, then. Where will he be now?"
"In the Amravana, at Vaishali... which belongs to Amrapali, the courtesan. Amrapali has gifted it to Buddha."

When Ajatasatru reached Amravana in the dead of night, he grew paranoid. "I heard no sound. Where is Buddha?"
"He is here with over twelve hundred disciples," Jivaka assured him. 
Ajatasatru's mind troubled him deeply. "Twelve hundred people! Not a sound of human habitation here!! Have you brought me here to be killed? Is it a plot of yours?"
"My lord! Trust me. Let us go ahead."
Ajatasatru was spellbound when he finally saw the massive assembly of men meditating quietly around the great teacher. 
"All suffering and pain, fear and hatred, come from desire. The man who is free from all desire, need not worry. Whom has he to fear?" Buddha preached. 
"How soothing!" Ajatasatru thought, his paranoia melting away. He repented for his sins and became a disciple of Buddha. 

Not much later, the wicked Devadatta also repented after falling severely ill. He told his followers, "Children, take me to Buddha. He alone can bring peace to my troubled mind."
His men carried him on a litter, but stopped to rest just outside the monastery. "Where have my men gone? I am in a hurry to meet Buddha," Devadatta gasped, trying to walk himself. But before he could reach Buddha, he tottered and fell to the ground. 
"I am dying," Devadatta whispered. "But now I know that Buddha is indeed the enlightened one—the teacher of all. I take refuge in him."

Millions took refuge in him and in his teachings, chanting:
*Buddham Saranam Gacchami* (I take refuge in the Buddha)
*Sangham Saranam Gacchami* (I take refuge in the Sangha)
*Dhammam Saranam Gacchami* (I take refuge in the Dharma)

Buddha lived to a ripe old age, spreading peace. People who spoke different languages and belonged to distant lands became his devoted followers.


# King Kusha: A Jataka Story About Inner Beauty

Sheelavati, the chief queen of the heirless King Okkaka of Kushavati, was offered a boon by Indra, king of the gods. 
"You shall have two sons," Indra told her. "One wise but ugly, the other handsome but a fool. Which will you have first?"
"The wise one, my lord," Sheelavati chose without hesitation. 

In due course, Sheelavati gave birth to a son. 
"He shall be called Kusha," King Okkaka declared. 
Two years later, she gave birth to the second son, Jayampati. "What a beautiful baby!" the maids cooed over the younger prince. 

Even as a child, Kusha was deeply conscious of his unappealing appearance. "I must excel in all the arts to make up for my ugly looks," he resolved. In the years that followed, Kusha mastered the arts of music, painting, and sculpture, becoming a brilliant artisan and scholar. 

One day, the King told his wife, "Sheelavati, Kusha is almost sixteen years old. I would like to place him on the throne while I am yet alive. But before I do, I'd like to see him married."
"I too would love to have a daughter in the palace!" the Queen agreed. 

When Kusha learnt of his parents' wishes, he was sad. "Would any princess marry an ugly fellow like me?" he worried. "But I cannot tell my parents this. It would hurt them. I must find a way out."
After giving the matter a great deal of thought, he hit upon a plan. "I will create a beautiful image and ask for the impossible—someone exactly like it for a wife."

Kusha locked himself in his workshop. When the golden image was completed, Kusha could not help admiring his own work. "They will never be able to find a princess half as beautiful as you!" he thought confidently. 
He then brought his mother to see it. "If you can find me a princess as beautiful as this, I'll marry her."

Queen Sheelavati was astounded by the sculpture. She told the king, "Prince Kusha, the gift of Indra, is highly talented. He will have none but a princess worthy of him."
King Okkaka summoned his royal councillors. "Take this figure he has created and tour the country exhibiting it. When you come across its living likeness, present the image to her father and request him for her hand on behalf of your king."

The councillors travelled from kingdom to kingdom, exhibiting the golden image in town squares, till at last they arrived at Sagala, the capital of the kingdom of Madda. 
As usual, they placed the image at a conspicuous spot. "Now let us stand away from it and overhear the comments of those who pass by," a councillor whispered. 

After a while, a hunchbacked royal nurse followed by seven or eight maids came by. "As soon as we've bathed we will..." one maid began. 
Suddenly, the old nurse marched up to the golden statue and hit it angrily. "Prabhavati! What on earth are you doing alone here at this hour? Should your father hear of this it will be the end of us! ...Oo-oo-oo! My hand! It's a metal image!"

"Madam! What's the matter? Your hand! It's bruised and broken!" the maids cried. 
But the old hunchback soon recovered her composure and began laughing. "Ha! Ha! Ha! What a fool I've been. I mistook this cheap image to be the princess."
"Ha! Ha! How could you?"
"I have hurt my hands for insulting her. What is this worthless image compared to my darling Prabhavati!" the nurse scoffed. 

The hidden councillors were overjoyed. They stepped out. "What do you mean?"
"What I say," the nurse declared. "This image would appear ugly before her. And I am her nurse who says so."

The delighted councillors immediately sought an audience with Princess Prabhavati's father, the King of Madda. 
"Your majesty, our king, Okkaka of Kushavati, is anxious to place his son, the brave Prince Kusha, on the throne. We request you on his behalf for the hand of Princess Prabhavati."
"We gladly agree," the King of Madda smiled. "We would be honoured by the alliance."

The councillors rushed back to Kushavati. "Your majesty, we have found her—a princess more beautiful than the image!"
When King Okkaka and Queen Sheelavati heard the whole story, they were thrilled. "Let us set out for Sagala at once with a large retinue." "Yes! Let us not waste a moment."

When they reached Sagala, they were received graciously by the king, the queen, Princess Prabhavati and her seven younger sisters. Later, when Prabhavati came to pay her respects to Queen Sheelavati, the Queen was stunned by her absolute perfection. 
"Should this dazzling beauty see my son's face, she will run away from him that very moment," Sheelavati worried. "Yet I must not fail my son. Let me see what I can do."

When Prabhavati had left, Sheelavati spoke to the king of Madda. "Your daughter is worthy of my son. But we have an unusual tradition in our royal family. If she will agree to observe it, we will take her as our daughter-in-law."
"What is the tradition?"
"A wife is not permitted to see her husband's face in daylight till she has conceived. Will she agree?"

Prabhavati agreed to respect the royal tradition. After much giving and receiving of gifts, Okkaka and Sheelavati escorted their new daughter-in-law, along with her nurse and a vast retinue, back to Kushavati. 

Back at Kushavati, the King rejoiced. "Have the city decorated. Release all prisoners. The wedding and the coronation of Prince Kusha shall be celebrated today!"

Following the tradition, neither Kusha nor Prabhavati saw each other's face during the day. In the dark, Prabhavati would listen to him play music. "How well he plays the veena!" she thought lovingly. "He must have the sensitive, handsome face of a musician."
Meanwhile, Kusha pined for her. "Ah! I can only imagine how she looks by thinking of my golden image."

A few days after the wedding, King Kusha came to Sheelavati. "Mother, please permit me to look at my wife, but once."
"You will have to wait until she has conceived," his mother reminded him. 
"I cannot wait till then, mother."
Sheelavati relented. "All right. Go to the elephant stalls disguised as a keeper. I will bring Prabhavati there. But see that you do not make yourself known to her."

Sheelavati then went to Prabhavati. "You have not yet seen your lord's royal elephants. Come, I will show them to you."
When Kusha saw Prabhavati as she walked behind his mother, he was utterly enchanted. "Is she a princess or a goddess?" he marvelled. 
Overwhelmed by her beauty and determined to find out if she was real, he playfully picked up a handful of elephant dung and flung it at her dress. 

Prabhavati was enraged. "How dare you! You ugly fellow," she screamed at the disguised keeper. "I will get the king, my husband, to cut off your hands."
Kusha grinned to himself. "She is a true fierce princess!"
As Sheelavati pacified her and hurried her away, Prabhavati demanded, "Mother, I must see my husband's face—just once."
"No! Please don't ask for that. It would be inauspicious."

But Prabhavati went on pleading. At last, Sheelavati compromised. "Well, tomorrow my son will be riding through the city in the royal parade. You can open your window and see him."
Prabhavati thought happily, "I will finally see him."
Meanwhile, Sheelavati secretly arranged for Kusha's handsome younger brother to lead the parade. "I will tell Kusha to send Jayampati in a procession through the streets. He can wear Kusha's royal clothes and ride on his elephant."

Poor Kusha was not going to miss the chance of seeing his wife again. "Dressed as an elephant-keeper, I will ride behind Jayampati," he decided. 
The next day, when Prabhavati saw Jayampati on the royal elephant, she was thrilled. "How handsome my husband is! Indeed! I am proud of him."
Then, she noticed the keeper sitting behind him. "But what an impudent, ugly elephant-keeper he has! How dare he wave to me!"

When the procession had passed by, Prabhavati turned away from the window. "Did you see your lord?" Sheelavati asked. 
"I did, mother. But why do they let such an ugly, impudent man ride behind him?"
"Don't be misled by looks," Sheelavati said carefully. "That man is capable, and the king must have a highly capable man sitting behind him to guide the elephant."

But Prabhavati was not convinced. "No man would be allowed to behave like that, however excellent his services might be. Can it be that *he* is King Kusha and they do not let me see him because he is so ugly?"
She turned to her nurse. "Run, my dear. Find out who was the king—the man in front or the one behind."
"How am I to find out?"
"The king will be the first to alight from the elephant when it returns to the palace. Go. Don't waste any more time."

When the nurse hurried to the courtyard and saw the ugly 'keeper' alight first and be treated like royalty, she was shocked. "What! Is that hideous one King Kusha!"
Kusha spotted her. "Ah! Isn't that Prabhavati's nurse? She is here to spy on me."
He immediately sent for her. "I charge you on pain of death not to reveal my secret to Prabhavati," he commanded the nurse. 

The nurse went back to Prabhavati, who eagerly awaited her return. Fearing for her life, she lied, "The handsome one that sat in front was the first to alight."
"Thank God!" Prabhavati sighed in relief. 

The second sight of Prabhavati made Kusha want to see her yet again. He pleaded with his mother. "Well then, if you must, enter the lotus pool in the gardens, conceal yourself under the large lily pads, and wait," Sheelavati suggested. 
A little later, Sheelavati brought Prabhavati to the garden. "Oh! What a beautiful lotus pond. Mother, may I bathe in it?"
"Certainly. I'll go back to the palace, and send some maids with a change of clothes for you."

Prabhavati waded into the water. "Ah! That lotus is larger and more beautiful than the rest. I'll pluck it for myself."
As she stretched out her hand to pluck it, the lily pad suddenly rose. "Ee-ee-ah! A goblin has caught hold of me!" Prabhavati screamed. 
"I am King Kusha!" Kusha announced, grinning. 
Seeing his hideous face, Prabhavati swooned and collapsed in the water. 

When she regained consciousness, she found herself in her chamber surrounded by her nurse and her maids. 
"I was right. It was him all the time," she wept angrily. "Why should I live with such an ugly, hideous man, powerful king though he be."
She summoned the councillors who had arranged her marriage. "Have my chariot ready. I will return this very day to my parents."
"B-but... yes, your majesty," the terrified councillors stuttered. 

Not knowing what to do, they went and consulted King Kusha. Kusha sighed sadly. "Do not stop her. She would be unhappy here. I will go after her later and bring her back with her willing consent."

Though Kusha so kind-heartedly let Prabhavati go, he was desolate. Two days later, he went to his mother. "Mother, I will go and bring Prabhavati back, with her consent."
"Go, my son. But take care of yourself."
Equipping himself with all that he would need, Kusha set out for Sagala. "I will not rest till I win her love," he vowed. 

When he reached Sagala, he entered the palace and found his way to the elephant stalls. "Who are you? What do you want?" the keeper asked. 
"Let me stay here for the night and I will play the veena for you," Kusha offered. 
They agreed. Kusha tuned his veena and began to play a hauntingly beautiful melody. 

In the palace, Prabhavati suddenly woke up. "Those pure notes. They can only come from his veena," she realized, her heart aching. "How I loved to hear them till I saw his face. He has followed me here."

Early next morning, Kusha woke up. "I must now try to meet Prabhavati without letting anyone know who I am."
He went to the royal potter and became his apprentice. A few days later, the potter was amazed. "Why, my son, you are incredibly talented! These vases are beautiful! I shall take them to the palace right away."
"Master, do not forget. This one with these specific figures is for Princess Prabhavati. I made it specially for her," Kusha instructed. 

At the palace, the King of Madda examined the pottery. "Who has made these exquisite vases?"
"I have, your majesty," the potter lied nervously. 
"This is not your handiwork. Tell me the truth, who made them?"
"M-my apprentice, your majesty."
"Not your apprentice but your master," the King proclaimed. "Henceforth he shall make all the pottery for my daughters. You may take the vases to them. Ah! You may give him these gold coins."

When the princesses saw the vases, they were thrilled. "How delightful they are!" "Look at this one. I'll keep it." "Such exquisite workmanship!"
The potter came up to Prabhavati. "This he made specially for you, O Princess Prabhavati."
Prabhavati looked at the carvings. "Why, these figures...! They resemble me and my nurse perfectly... only *he* could have made these!"
She became terribly angry and flung the vase to the floor, smashing it. "I don't want it. It's hideous."
"Ha! Ha! Our sister behaves as if it were a gift from King Kusha himself," her sisters laughed. 

The potter returned and told Kusha all that had happened at the palace. "...But the king certainly liked your work. Here, these gold coins are a gift to you from him."
"You may keep the money," Kusha sighed. "It's no use my staying here. I'd better try from elsewhere."

Kusha left the potter and apprenticed himself to the royal wicker-worker. A few days later, the master worker watched him in awe. "Whoever he is, he is the best worker I've had so far. How deftly his fingers move. What are you making?"
"A palm leaf fan for the eldest princess. See, here she is, standing by this pillar," Kusha said, weaving a perfect portrait into the fan. 
"Remarkable workmanship! I shall take it to the king right away."

At the palace, the King was once again amazed. "Whose work is this? Not yours, I'm certain."
"It's the creation of my new apprentice, sir."
"Not your apprentice but your master. Give him these gold coins."

The moment Prabhavati saw the beautifully woven fan, however, she recognized the style. "This, too, is his handiwork. Why does he pursue me?"
She flung the fan on the floor. "Don't you dare show me such inferior work ever again."
"Ha! Ha! It was not King Kusha who made that fan. So why do you disown it, Prabhavati?" her sisters teased. 

The wicker-worker returned and told Kusha all that had happened at the palace. "...Women are strange. I don't know why she threw it on the floor in a rage."
Kusha knew. "I will have to try some other means to meet her."
He pondered for a while. "I know what. I shall seek service in the royal kitchen. Perhaps..."

Kusha was lucky. The royal cook had sacked one of his assistants that very day. When Kusha approached him, the cook agreed. "In fact, I need a help. But you will have to cook your own meal in the kitchen meant for menials."
"That is no problem, sir."

Kusha soon became an expert cook—better even than his master. One day, the cook gave him some meat. "Here, you may cook this chop for yourself."
An hour later, as the king was being served his royal feast, he caught a scent. "Mm-m-m! What a delicious aroma. Is that dish yet to come?"
"N-no, your majesty," the cook stuttered. "What you smell is the chop my new assistant is cooking for himself."
"Then let me taste it."
After the king had tasted it, he declared, "Henceforth your assistant shall cook for me and my daughters. Ah! And give him these gold coins."

Kusha was extremely happy when he heard about the king's order. "Please keep the coins for yourself, master. I have no use for them. At last I shall be able to see my wife."

The next day, as Kusha carried the food to the royal chambers, Prabhavati peeked from behind a curtain. "I feel sorry for him," she thought, seeing him doing menial work. "Yet I must not let him know that I do, or he will stay on, thinking I've yielded."
She stepped out. "Kusha, I can't bear the sight of your ugly face. Go back to Kushavati. It is not right for you to waste your time here."
"I am not going back without you," Kusha replied gently. "I want your love and not the throne. I would gladly give up crown and throne to live near you."

When Prabhavati heard this she was alarmed. "Suppose he declares 'I am King Kusha' right here and seizes my hand. No one will stop him. Besides, someone might overhear our talk." To discourage any further dialogue, she quickly closed her door and bolted it. 
Kusha gazed for a moment at the closed door, and then went down. "She is bound to relent. I will wait patiently."

But seven long months passed and Kusha toiled on in the hot kitchens. At last, his resolve broke. "In seven months, she has not so much as let me have a glimpse of her. Why do I pine for her? She is harsh and cruel. I will return to my kingdom and my parents."

At that moment, realizing how disappointed Kusha was, Indra, king of the gods, decided to intervene and help him. 
Using his divine powers, Indra magically sent identical messages to seven different powerful kings in the region. The messages read: *"Prabhavati has left King Kusha and returned home. I offer her hand to you."* He made sure the messengers claimed to come from the court of Madda. 

When the seven kings arrived at Sagala with their massive armies and discovered that they were all there for the exact same purpose, they were furious. 
"He has deliberately humiliated us!"
"He will have to keep his word to each one of us!"
They jointly sent an ultimatum to the King of Madda: *"Give Prabhavati to all seven of us or prepare for war."*

The King was utterly terrified and alarmed. "I will have to cut her up into seven pieces and send one to each monarch," he despaired. 
"How can you do such a cruel thing to your own daughter?" his queen cried. 
"If I send her to any one of them, it will be war with the other six. My kingdom will be destroyed!"

When Prabhavati heard of her father's desperate decision, she was terrified. 
"Why did she reject a husband as powerful as King Kusha?" her angry father yelled. "Let her suffer for it."
She ran to her mother's chamber. "Mother! O Mother, I am helpless. If only King Kusha were here, his armies could protect us."
"He *is* here, my child," the queen revealed. "Come, I'll take you to him."

Prabhavati led her mother to the kitchens. "Look! There he is—in the guise of a cook. He came here and suffered for my sake."
She ran forward and fell at Kusha's feet in tears. "My lord, forgive me. I have done you great wrong. I am to pay for it with my life. Yes. This beautiful body of mine, of which I was so vain, is to be cut into pieces, to be given to seven kings, to prevent a war."

Kusha dropped his ladles, his royal bearing returning instantly. "What! Will anyone dare touch my dear wife when I am yet alive!"
"B-but! Wh-what about the seven kings and their armies?" Prabhavati trembled. 
"Leave that to me," Kusha smiled confidently. "Go. Bathe and adorn yourself in your royal jewels, and wait for me in your palace."

King Kusha stepped out, revealed his true identity to the stunned court of Madda, and took command. Utilizing his immense wealth, power, and unmatched wisdom, he settled matters peacefully by arranging royal alliances, offering his seven beautiful sisters-in-law to the seven kings. 

With peace restored and Prabhavati's vanity completely humbled by his unwavering love and capability, he and Prabhavati returned to Kushavati in a grand procession, where they lived happily ever after.


# Angulimala: The Bandit Who Took Refuge in Buddha

Long, long ago, a king called Prasenajit ruled over Kosala from his capital, Shravasti. One night when he was fast asleep, his room was suddenly lit by incredible flashes of light. He woke up with a start. 
"Who - what's that? The weapons!" he gasped. "What's happening to them? Why do they glitter so?"

The next moment, however, all was dark again. "Was it a nightmare? Was my imagination playing tricks on me? Or was I really dazzled by the light from the weapons?" King Prasenajit wondered. 

The next morning, any doubts he might have had vanished. His generals reported, "At midnight, the weapons in the armoury and in every house of Kosala blazed, for a moment, with a brilliant light." 
"A strange thing happened last night, your majesty!"

At that exact moment in the city, a son was born in the house of the royal priest, Garga. 
"How handsome is our newborn son!" his mother smiled. 
"He is, no doubt," Garga frowned, pacing the room. "But I am worried. Immediately after his birth, strange lights issued forth from the weapons of the night-guards."

When the perturbed Garga consulted a learned astrologer, the old man shook his head grimly. "I regret to tell you that your son is going to be a robber—a menace to our people."
Garga went to the palace and told the king the story. 
"Ah! That explains why the weapons shone so brightly last night!" Prasenajit realized. 
"Your majesty, I cannot rear a son who is destined to be a menace to the kingdom," the royal priest pleaded. "The scriptures say that one life may be sacrificed to save a hundred. Therefore permit me to put him to death."

"No! I won't allow the killing of an innocent baby!" King Prasenajit firmly refused. "Lord Buddha has taught me to believe in the innate goodness of every living creature. Let your son have a sound education. Under your guidance, I have no doubt that he will grow up to be a good citizen."
"Thank you, your majesty. I will try my best to instil virtue in him."

The infant, who was named Ahimsaka, grew up to be an exceptionally intelligent and well-behaved boy. "One as dedicated as he is to the study of the scriptures can never become a robber," Garga thought proudly. 
When Ahimsaka was about fourteen years old, he requested, "Father, please permit me to go to Takshashila."
"You may go, my son. You have my blessings."

Ahimsaka left for the great university of Takshashila that very day. "All the way to Takshashila for the sake of learning!" he smiled to himself. "How can Ahimsaka ever become a robber? There must have been some mistake in the prophecy."

On reaching Takshashila, Ahimsaka went to a great scholar of those days. "I, Ahimsaka, son of Garga of Shravasti, salute you. Accept me as your disciple, master."
"You are welcome, my son. I will teach you."

Ahimsaka soon won the heart of the master. "Look at Ahimsaka," the master praised him in front of the others. "Within such a short time, he has mastered what you could not in the last three years. He has a great future before him."

Such comments aroused the deep jealousy of the other disciples. "Ahimsaka thinks too much of himself," they grumbled. "Our teacher's praise has gone to his head. We must do something about it."
They hatched a plot to estrange the master from his favourite disciple. 

One day, they saw the master approaching. "Quick! The master is approaching. Let us begin our little drama," the lead disciple whispered. 
"Ahimsaka is a great scholar," one said loudly. 
"That's why our master praises him so," another added. 
"Indeed, he is a greater scholar even than our master!" the first declared. 
"At least the wife of our revered master thinks so," the second mocked. 

The master overheard this and became deeply thoughtful. "Perhaps there is an element of truth in what the boys say. One day Ahimsaka may prove to be more learned than me. I must see that he is made an outcaste, shunned by all."
When he entered his house, he saw Ahimsaka chatting with his wife. "Does my wife really think he is a greater scholar than me? How attentively she listens to what he says! Does she have a greater regard for him than she has for me?" he wondered jealously. "Whatever the truth may be, Ahimsaka will have to go."

He entered the hall stealthily so that his wife and Ahimsaka, deeply engrossed in their discussions, did not notice his presence. 
"Ahimsaka! Get up!" the master suddenly barked. 
"Pardon me, master. I..." Ahimsaka stammered, jumping up. 
"I have been standing here all this while. Have you become so arrogant that you forget to acknowledge the presence of your guru?"
The master, however, didn't give him a chance to continue. "Humility should be the outcome of knowledge, not arrogance. You have no place here. You may go."

As a bemused, heart-broken Ahimsaka walked away from the master's house, the other disciples gloated over the success of their plot. "It worked!" 

When Ahimsaka returned to Shravasti, his father was furious. "Why are you back so soon? Have you finished your studies?"
"No, father. I was sent away. I incurred the displeasure of my master. But I..."
"What! Incurred the displeasure of your master!" Garga exploded. "Don't show me your accursed face! Go away from here!"

Ahimsaka was thrown out onto the streets. "What have I done to deserve such a fate? What shall I do? I will try to make a living with what little knowledge I have."
But Ahimsaka's efforts to find some work proved futile. "Employ a youth who has incurred the displeasure of his master? Never!" people scoffed. 
He was completely shunned by all. "Close the door! I don't want him to enter our house and pollute it," neighbors sneered when he walked by. 

"They slam their doors on my face," Ahimsaka wept. "Why my master sent me away, I still do not know. Father did not even want to hear my story."
Deep in thought, Ahimsaka left Kosala. "What was my fault? Why have I been treated so cruelly? They label me a sinner. But it is I who have been sinned against!"

Suddenly, a highway robber jumped out. "Surrender whatever you have if you value your life!"
Ahimsaka's grief turned into explosive rage. "No! No! No!" he screamed, brutally punching the robber to the ground. "Take this! And this! There! I am giving you all that I have!"
Disgusted, Ahimsaka let the bleeding man go. "He has shown me the way," Ahimsaka decided darkly. "I will be a highway robber. I'll be making a living while I take revenge on the society that rejected me."

Later, in the evening, a fleet of bullock carts carrying merchandise passed that way on their way to Kaushambi. 
"Halt!" Ahimsaka yelled, stepping onto the road with a sickle. 
"My god! A robber! He is one, we are many. Let us capture him!" the merchants shouted, drawing their swords. 
But the merchants were no match for Ahimsaka's furious strength. He slaughtered them all. 
"All dead! Good!" he panted, looking at the bodies. "I will keep their *angulis* (little fingers) and make a garland of them."

Soon, Ahimsaka's atrocious deeds became the talk of Kosala. 
"He is a monster," terrified citizens whispered. 
"No one knows who he is nor where he comes from."
"He wears a strange garland... so I've heard—an *Angulimala*! (A garland of little fingers)."
And Ahimsaka began to be known across the land as Angulimala. 

The traders who had to travel were the worst hit by Angulimala, as the shortest route to Kaushambi was through his haunted forest. 
"Ha, ha! There is no escape from me. Get down all of you," Angulimala would laugh as he stopped their carts. As soon as the merchants were massacred, he would feed their bodies to the vultures. "Come, my friends! This feast is for you."
"Spare my life. Have mercy on me," survivors would beg. 
"Mercy? What does that mean?" Angulimala would laugh maniacally, chopping off their little fingers. "I want to kill you for your little finger, ha! ha!"

At last the terror-stricken subjects turned to King Prasenajit. "Your majesty, deliver us from Angulimala."
"I will command my forest guards to capture the notorious murderer," the king promised. 
As a large contingent of heavily armed forest guards entered Angulimala's haunt, the bandit watched from a cliff. "A whole army of them! Good! That many more fingers for my garland!"
Lifting up huge boulders, Angulimala hurled them down at the guards, and then sprang upon them like a tiger. Only a few were able to escape with their lives. 

When they reported back to Prasenajit, the king was stunned. "Just one man, and you were helpless against him!"
That evening, the royal drummer went round the city. "Give ear! Give ear! People are advised to avoid Angulimala. Those going to Kaushambi should take the route via Magadha..."

As travelers entirely abandoned the forest route, Angulimala waited in vain for victims. "My garland has begun to wither," he realized. "I need new fingers! I wish someone would pass this way."
Suddenly he spotted a lone monk walking calmly by. "Halt!" Angulimala yelled, grabbing his sickle. "My prayer has been heard!"

Angulimala ran fiercely after the monk. To his astonishment, however, the monk, who seemed to be walking at a very leisurely pace, was always exactly a step ahead. No matter how fast Angulimala sprinted, he could not reach him. 
At last, exhausted and panting heavily from the chase, Angulimala paused. "O monk, stop! Stop moving!"
The Buddha turned to look at him. "I am not moving. I am at rest. It is you who are in perpetual motion."
"What do you mean?"
"My mind is at rest," the Buddha explained gently. "You are yet to find peace of mind, my child."

Angulimala was stunned. *"My child! He calls me his child! No! He cannot mean it,"* he thought. 
"Don't you know who I am?" Angulimala snarled, raising his bloody sickle. "I don't want your preaching. I want your little fingers."
"Take them, my son, if it makes you happy," Buddha replied peacefully. 
"Along with your fingers, I take your life too!"
"By all means do, my son, if it brings you peace of mind."

Overwhelmed by the Buddha's absolute fearlessness and unconditional love, Angulimala's rage shattered. He dropped his weapon and collapsed weeping at the feet of the monk. "Master!"
"Get up, my child."
"Master, henceforth I will never kill," Angulimala vowed. 

The monk brought him to the monastery on the outskirts of Shravasti. "Anathapindika, I have brought yet another brother—Angulimala," he introduced the fearsome bandit to the other monks. 
The loving monk was none other than Lord Buddha. 

The next morning, King Prasenajit visited the monastery. He was dressed in full battle armor. 
"It looks as though you have started on a military expedition," Buddha smiled. 
"Yes, master. I want to exterminate that monster—Angulimala. I have come for your blessings."
"Supposing Angulimala gives up the path of violence and begins to live the life of an ascetic, what will your reaction be?"
"I will salute him then, my lord," the King admitted. "But pardon me, I can't imagine Angulimala as an ascetic."
"But he has become one. There he is, watering the plants."
"What!" Prasenajit gasped. 

Angulimala had completely shaved his head and wore the yellow robes of a monk. He was devoted to Buddha and listened carefully to the master's words of wisdom. 
"I could not subdue Angulimala with all my strength, and you have won him without lifting your little finger," Prasenajit marvelled. "We are grateful to you, lord."

Angulimala lived peacefully in the monastery, nursing the sick and meditating. But his past haunted him. 
One day, when Angulimala went into town to beg for his food, a woman kindly offered him rice. "You are generous, my child. May you, your mother and father be blessed."
"I have no father. He is dead," the woman sighed. "He was killed by the wicked Angulimala."
Angulimala froze. "Oh!"

Sick at heart, Angulimala walked back to the monastery, where he spent many a sleepless night. The screams of his victims echoed in his mind. *"I am an orphan. You made me one! Murderer! You killed my father. Yes, you are a murderer!"*
"Oh! No, no!" Angulimala wept in the dark. 

That Angulimala had suddenly become despondent did not escape the notice of Buddha. "Why are you so sad, my son?"
"Master, these hands of mine are stained with blood. I am a sinner without a future, without hope!"
"Why child, repentance is the only fire that is capable of burning sins already committed. You are on the right path," Buddha assured him. 

But Angulimala continued to suffer from terrible guilt. "Father, how long must Angulimala suffer? Can't you ease him of his burden?" the other monks asked Buddha. 
"All in good time," Buddha replied. 

Then one day, Buddha took Angulimala with him on his rounds. As they proceeded, they heard a woman screaming in agony by the roadside. 
"Master, someone seems to be in pain. Let us hurry," Angulimala urged. 
They found an old woman sitting with her pregnant daughter. "What's the trouble, mother?" Buddha asked. 
"My daughter is about to deliver a baby, sir. We were on our way to town, but she is dying," the old woman wept. 
"Can't you alleviate her misery, master?" Angulimala begged. 
"My son, *you* bless the woman and say: 'When I killed people, I did so out of ignorance. If I speak the truth, let the woman get well.'"
"But master...!" Angulimala stepped back in horror. "How can I, a ruthless murderer, save her?"
"Oh, sir, don't say no! Please don't! Save her," the old woman pleaded. 

With much reluctance, Angulimala obeyed his master's orders. He placed his hand on the dying woman's head. *"I don't know if I had killed with full knowledge. If I am correct in saying so, let this woman get well,"* he prayed sincerely. 
Instantly, the woman's pain eased, and she delivered a healthy baby. The old woman brought the child to them. "Sir, you blessed my daughter and saved her life. Bless her little son, too."
"Mother, merciful Buddha will bless him," Angulimala smiled humbly. 
"I want both of you to bless the child."

When the old woman went away, Buddha turned to Angulimala. "Angulimala, at least now are you convinced that you have overcome your past deeds? You have given life."
"I am, master, thanks to you."
"Son, you no longer need me. You must walk alone in the world."
"If you insist, I will," Angulimala bowed deeply. "But it is you who have given me the strength to do so. *Buddham Saranam Gacchami.*"

After parting from Buddha, Angulimala decided to go back to Shravasti alone to beg for alms. As he entered the city, a man recognized him. "Look! Did you recognise him? He is none other than Angulimala in disguise!"
The news spread instantly, and people began to panic. "Angulimala is coming! Run for your lives!"
But some youngsters mustered up their courage. "No, we shall not run away. Come, we will kill him before he kills us all."

Arming themselves with swords, sticks, and stones, a furious mob approached Angulimala. 
"Look! A child has come out to offer rice to the monster in disguise," a man yelled, seeing a little boy approach the monk. 
"He is going to strangle that child!"
But Angulimala simply knelt and kissed the child's forehead gently. 

As soon as Angulimala stepped out of the house, a stone hit him hard on the forehead. It was followed by many more. Then the mob let loose its pent-up fury, beating him mercilessly with heavy sticks. 
"Hit him hard! Give me a chance. It's my turn now!"
"What does it feel like? Why don't you fight back?" they screamed at him. 

But the former fearsome bandit did not raise a single finger to protect himself. He accepted their wrath silently. 
Severely beaten, bloody, and dying, he started crawling towards the abode of his master. 
"May you die a dog's death," the mob spat as they left him. 

When he finally dragged his broken body back to the monastery, Buddha knelt beside him. "Angulimala, my child!"
"Buddham... Saranam... Gacchaa..mi," Angulimala whispered weakly. 
"Were you angry when they beat you up?" Buddha asked softly. 
"No, father. When I killed people, I did not know what I was doing. Similarly, they too did not know what they were doing."
"Do you feel better, my child?"
"Never before have I felt better than I do now, master."
"Are you at rest now?"
"Yes, father. I am at peace with the world," Angulimala smiled. 

And Angulimala breathed his last. 
Buddha addressed the weeping monks. "Angulimala was a great soul. He conquered vice, cruelty and anger. He has attained Nirvana."


# Amrapali: Chosen by Buddha

The Licchavi nobles, who ruled over ancient Vaishali, were great lovers of beauty and took a keen interest in the maintenance of their public gardens. The absolute best of gardeners were employed to tend the plants. 

One day, while working in the royal park, a gardener gasped. "What's that under the mango tree? Why, it's a lady! What an extra-ordinary beauty!"
He went up to her. "Who are you, fair lady? What is your name? Where have you come from? And what is your destination?"
"I don't have a name. I come from nowhere and have nowhere to go," the mysterious maiden replied softly. 
"Are you going to live in this garden?"
"Yes. If you do not mind, I would like to live here."
"I certainly don't mind. But this garden belongs to the nobles," the gardener thought. "They should be informed."

Leaving her in the garden, he rushed to the council of nobles. "A strange lady has appeared in the state garden! In all Vaishali there is no one as beautiful as she is!"
"Who could she be? Why has she come here?" the nobles wondered, and immediately rushed out to the garden. 

When they reached the garden, they were stunned. "The gardener knew what he was talking about! She is the most enchanting lady I have ever seen!"
Immediately, a fierce fight broke out among the proud nobles. Each wanted the maiden for himself! 
"Amrapali shall be mine!" one noble declared, drawing his sword. (*Amrapali* meant the 'mango-girl', as she was found near the mango tree). 
"You'll have to take her from me first!" another shouted, attacking him. 

Suddenly, Amrapali stepped between them. "Stop! Do I have no choice in the matter? Can I not have my say?"
The nobles lowered their swords in shame. "We have been selfish, thoughtless! You choose one among us, Amrapali. The one you choose shall marry you."
"If I choose one of you, I will no doubt be making him very happy," Amrapali said wisely. "But I will be making all the others extremely jealous and angry. So I will not marry any of you."

The elders of Vaishali, who had hurried to the garden on hearing the clash, supported her. "A wise decision, indeed! Amrapali cannot belong to one person. She shall belong to Vaishali."
"We agree," the young nobles nodded. 
"Amrapali has no place to live in. Let us present this garden to her," one elder suggested. 
"And we could have a grand house built here for her," another added. 

As soon as the house was ready, the elders handed over the keys. "This garden and the house are yours, Amrapali."
"I shall ever remain indebted to the people of Vaishali for the love they have shown me," she smiled. 

To entertain the nobles of Vaishali in return, Amrapali, who was an incredibly excellent dancer, began to give public dance performances. 
"Such perfect rhythm!" "She is matchless!" "No other kingdom can boast of such an accomplished dancer!" "She is indeed the absolute pride of Vaishali!" the nobles cheered. 

One evening, when the nobles were watching Amrapali perform, a messenger rushed in, panting heavily. "Master! I have bad news! Vaishali is in danger! The Magadhan army is heading towards Vaishali."
"What! That monster King Bimbisara again!" a noble cursed. 
"Duty calls! We will see you later, Amrapali," they said, rushing off to arm themselves. 

As Amrapali watched the young men of Vaishali ride to the bloody battlefield, she felt deeply disturbed. "Why do men fight? What do they gain by killing one another? One moment they appreciate beauty and art; the next, they thirst for blood."

A few days later, as the war raged at the borders, a handsome stranger called on her house. "What a winsome personality he has!" Amrapali thought. 
"I have come a long way to see you dance. Will you dance for me, fair one?" the stranger asked. 
Amrapali danced for him. "No wonder the Licchavis are proud of her," the stranger thought, mesmerized. "We don't have a dancer to equal her in all Magadha."

When the dance was over, Amrapali asked, "Where do you stay in Vaishali?"
"I am a stranger here. I have not yet found a place to stay."
"Then you are welcome to stay at my house."
"You are very generous, Amrapali," he smiled. 

The stranger stayed with her in secret for a week. Then, one evening, he said, "Amrapali, I must take your leave. I have to go back. I love you, Amrapali. Why don't you come away with me?"
Amrapali laughed. "You amuse me. You are a total stranger. I don't even know your name!"
"I am Bimbisara."
Amrapali gasped, stepping back. "What! The dreaded king of Magadha!"
"Yes."
"There is no room for love in the hearts of ambitious men!" Amrapali flared up angrily. "How can you talk of love, when this very moment because of you hundreds of men are dying on the battlefield?"

"If I give up my ambition to conquer Vaishali and cease hostilities forthwith, will you marry me?" Bimbisara pleaded. 
"No! I cannot!"
"Why not, Amrapali? Don't you love me?"
"I belong to Vaishali! I have given my pledge," she replied firmly. 

Without another word, the dejected Bimbisara turned and left into the night. 
Amrapali could not understand her own feelings. "Why do I feel sad to see the dreaded enemy of Vaishali go?" she wept. 

A few days later, the young nobles returned triumphantly. "Amrapali, we are back! That coward, Bimbisara, suddenly called off the battle."
"Why did he do it? Was it for love of me?" Amrapali thought, her heart heavy. 
"Amrapali, we missed you. We would like to see you complete the dance you began when we left," they requested. 
"You shall, in a moment."

But as Amrapali danced, the magic was gone. 
"What is wrong with her today?" "Her movements are lifeless," the nobles muttered. As they left for their homes, they agreed, "Amrapali's performance was a failure today. It was evident that her heart was not in it!"

Meanwhile, Amrapali sat brooding alone. "What is the matter with me? Why did I find no joy in dancing for the nobles today? Why do the presents that once pleased me, now disgust me? Why do I feel lonely... lost... afraid...?"

The next morning, as she walked in her garden to clear her head, she saw a young monk carrying a bright pink flower. 
"What a beautiful lotus! How fresh it is!" she exclaimed. "Will you sell that lotus to me, my lad?"
"Pardon me, lady. This lotus is not for sale," the young monk replied politely. "Haven't you heard? Lord Buddha has come to Vaishali and is camping at Kotigrama. I am taking this flower to him."

As the boy went his way, Amrapali felt a sudden surge of hope. "Lord Buddha! The enlightened one who knows the cause of suffering! I will go to him."

She immediately went to Kotigrama and sat among the crowd, listening to Lord Buddha with rapt attention. 
"Out of desire is born grief. Out of desire alone is born fear," Buddha preached calmly. "Free yourself from desire and the grief and the fear will wither away."
Amrapali began to experience a new kind of joy. "His words fill me with peace! His voice is so soft! His words so soothing!"

She went to Kotigrama daily to listen to his sermons. One day, she thought, "His very presence stills my restless mind. How I wish he would visit my home and fill it with his being! But will the lord visit the house of an unworthy person like me?"

Soon it was time for Lord Buddha to leave Vaishali. "When will I see you again, master?" Amrapali wept silently as he walked away. 
Even after Lord Buddha had left, Amrapali continued to visit Kotigrama every single evening. "This is where the master used to sit. I can still feel his presence here."
Many months passed, and Amrapali patiently waited for the return of Lord Buddha. "My master will come to Vaishali again. Perhaps today, perhaps tomorrow, but he will come."

One evening, she arrived at Kotigrama and gasped. "My master is back! I can't believe my eyes."
She ran up to Lord Buddha and fell weeping at his feet. 
"Arise, my child," Buddha smiled gently. 
And before she could stop herself, the words poured out. "Master, will you honour me by eating at my house tomorrow?"
"I will come, my child," Buddha agreed. 

Amrapali sped homewards to Vaishali in her chariot to prepare for the coming of her master. Suddenly, a few chariots carrying the nobles of Vaishali came charging from the opposite direction, blocking her path. 
"Where are you speeding to?" a noble asked. "We heard that Lord Buddha has arrived. We are off to invite him to eat with us at the palace tomorrow."
"But the lord has already agreed to dine at my house tomorrow," Amrapali declared proudly. 
"We have been forestalled!" a noble frowned. "Not all is lost. Not yet. She can easily be bought off."
He turned to her. "Amrapali, if you let Lord Buddha have his first meal at Vaishali with us, we will give you all the gold you want."
"No. Even if you offer me the entire kingdom of Vaishali, I won't give up the honour of serving the lord!" Amrapali retorted, driving away. 

"What shall we do?" the nobles grumbled. 
"We will go to the lord. He cannot refuse the nobles of Vaishali."
Later, at Kotigrama, the nobles bowed to the Buddha. "Lord, please honour us by dining with us tomorrow."
"I am sorry, I can't," Buddha replied peacefully. "I have already agreed to go to Amrapali's house tomorrow."

The next day, Lord Buddha went to Amrapali's house. 
"I have come, Amrapali."
"Lord, you do me great honour," she bowed. 

Later, as she served him the meal, she asked, "Lord, why do I feel such immense joy as I serve you?"
"Amrapali, you have finally begun to know the joy of giving," Buddha smiled. 

After the meal, Amrapali knelt before him. "Lord, permit me to grant my garden and this house to the Sangha."
"So be it, child. It could be used as a monastery."
Then, leaving behind her the worldly life, her riches, and its heartaches, Amrapali became a devoted follower of the Buddha. She began to lead a life of renunciation, finding at last the eternal peace of mind she had craved.


# The Acrobat and Other Buddhist Tales

## Upagupta

Long, long ago in ancient Mathura, there lived a dancer called Vasavadatta, who was famed for her extraordinary beauty and her art. She had a perfect figure and graceful movements, but an incredibly hard heart. 

After her performances, wealthy admirers would sigh, "I am prepared to part with all my wealth for a single smile from her." 
But with not so much as a glance at her admirers, Vasavadatta would sit in her chariot and ride home. 
"They are all madly in love with you. Couldn't you see it in their eyes?" her companion asked. 
"I chose not to see."
"Why, Vasavadatta? Why don't you marry one of them? Is there not a single one whom you deem worthy of your love?"
"No! Not one of them possesses what I am looking for," she declared. 

One evening, as Vasavadatta was standing in the balcony of her mansion, she saw a young monk pass by in the street below. She was instantly captivated by his serene face. 
"Quick! Run down and invite that monk in," she ordered her companion. 
"I can't believe it," her companion thought. "Vasavadatta showing some interest in a man!"

Her companion rushed out. "Vasavadatta wishes to see you, holy one. Will you come in?"
"Not now," the monk replied calmly. "I will see her at the proper time."

When Vasavadatta was told of the monk's reaction, she was offended. "Perhaps, he hesitates to visit me because he is poor and can't bring me gifts. Tell him I don't want anything from him."
The monk who spurned the invitation of the dancer was Upagupta, a disciple of Lord Buddha. 

Her friend went back to Upagupta. "O monk, my friend does not crave for gifts or riches. Please visit her."
"No, I cannot. It is not yet time to visit Vasavadatta," Upagupta replied, and walked away. 

Vasavadatta was stunned. "Why should the only man I choose to love, shun me?"
Heartbroken and insulted, she stopped giving dance performances, much to the annoyance of the people of Mathura. "Life in Mathura has lost its charm. How could she be so cruel to us!" they grumbled. 

Meanwhile, her friend was worried. "She sits alone, brooding over that heartless monk. It is not good for her health and looks. I must find something to distract her."
A few days later, she suggested, "A famous sculptor is holding an exhibition of his works. Let us go and see it, Vasavadatta."
"All right, if you insist."

At the show, Vasavadatta forgot her sorrow for a while. "Such exquisite workmanship!" she praised a statue. "Will you sell this to me?"
"You might find the price too heavy," the chief sculptor warned. 
"Whatever it may cost, I am prepared to buy it. Quote your price."
"It's yours, if you agree to dance again."
Vasavadatta hesitated, but her companion pushed her. "You can't go back on your word. You have agreed to pay whatever price he asks." 
Vasavadatta reluctantly agreed. 

The next evening, people flocked to the town hall. "At last we will see Vasavadatta dance again! Thanks to the chief sculptor of our city. It was indeed a clever bargain!"
At the end of the brilliant performance, Vasavadatta received thunderous applause. But instead of making her happy, it only made her brood all the more. "Why did that monk shun me when thousands of people long for a sight of me?"

In the days that followed, the chief sculptor began to visit the dancer to capture her image in stone. "My art will die with me. But yours will last for centuries," she told him. 
"My talent, which brings so much happiness to you, only makes my envious rivals hate me," the sculptor sighed. 

A few days later, the sculptor suddenly vanished. "Why doesn't he come? The work is yet to be completed. It's three days since he last came!" Vasavadatta complained. 
Meanwhile, his friends and relatives too were frantically searching for him. "He was last seen entering the house of Vasavadatta, three days ago."

Later, the body of the missing sculptor was found buried not far from Vasavadatta's house. She was immediately charged with the brutal murder of the sculptor. 
"Do you have anything to say in your defence?" the judge asked. 
Vasavadatta, completely shocked and grieving, had nothing to say. 
"Vasavadatta is guilty. Confiscate her property and have her banished from Mathura," the judge ordered. 

As Vasavadatta was turned out of her house, an angry mob jeered at her and pelted her heavily with stones. "Take this, you murderess. See that you never darken our city again!"
Bleeding profusely and covered in festering wounds, Vasavadatta reached the outskirts of Mathura and found refuge in an abandoned crematorium. 
"Vasavadatta, I know you are innocent! Your rivals have deliberately done this to you," her companion wept. 
"Friend, let us not blame anyone," Vasavadatta sighed, resigned to her fate. 

People coming in and out of Mathura looked upon Vasavadatta, now seriously ill with festering wounds, as an untouchable. "Leave her to die. Why do you waste your time on her? She doesn't deserve any sympathy." They would spit at her and sometimes even stone her as they passed by. 

Then came Upagupta, the Buddhist monk. 
"Friend, cover my body. Let him not see me in this terrible state," Vasavadatta panicked. 
"Vasavadatta, I have come to you," Upagupta said softly. 
"O monk, when all Mathura admired me, you alone spurned me. Why have you chosen to visit me now when I am a mass of festering flesh, shunned by all?" she cried. 
Upagupta smiled gently. "At that time you did not need me, Vasavadatta. You do, now. Come, let us go to my monastery."

Upagupta took her with him and painstakingly nursed her back to health. 
"You seem sad, Vasavadatta," he noticed one day. 
"I am no longer beautiful. No one will look at me now."
"Vasavadatta, you are yet to discover a beauty greater than the one you have lost—the beauty of the self."

Curious to know more, she began to attend the daily discourses of Lord Buddha. 
"You can't call even the body your own. When the body is cast away it becomes food for others," Buddha preached. "Light the lamp within you, and only then will you find peace."
"Light the lamp within you... peace..." Vasavadatta realized the profound truth. 
Vasavadatta fell at the feet of Lord Buddha. "Lord, admit me into your order."
"So be it, my child. May you find peace of mind."

## The Acrobat

The city of Rajagriha was buzzing with excitement. "Have you heard? They are coming!" "Yes. It's been a year since we saw them last. The acrobats are coming!"
A few days later, Rajagriha jubilantly welcomed the traveling troupe of acrobats. 

Crowds gathered in the central square. "Let the show begin," the King ordered. Those at the very back stood on their toes to get a better view. 
A beautiful female acrobat climbed high up a tall pole and performed breathtaking stunts. 
"Superb! She looks just like a bird!" the king cheered. 

The young man staring with absolute fascination at the acrobat was Ugrasena, the son of the king's wealthy treasurer. "Yes. The loveliest bird I ever set eyes on. How graceful and skilful she is! If I can't make her mine, life is not worth living," he decided. 

Ugrasena went home and flung himself on his bed. "Father, mother—I want to marry the acrobat we saw today. If I can't marry her, I shall starve myself to death."
"My dear son, what are you saying? What can you have in common with a lowly acrobat? We will find you a girl... one worthy of you..."
"It's her or none! I shall not even look at another girl," he insisted. 

The helpless treasurer and his wife sent Ugrasena's friend to the acrobat's father with a chest of gold. "My friend wishes to marry your daughter. As a marriage portion, here is all the gold you might desire."
"For shame! Are you asking me to sell my daughter?" the acrobat father scolded. "If your friend is willing to travel with us, wherever we go, he may marry her."

The treasurer and his wife were shocked to hear this. "Son, surely you will not leave us to go travelling with an acrobat!"
"I will, for she means everything to me now."
So Ugrasena married the acrobat, abandoned his immense wealth, and joined the travelling troupe. 

Being the only member of the troupe unskilled in acrobatics, he made himself useful in other menial ways, fetching wood and carrying water, and was content with his simple life. 
Soon, a son was born to them. His wife spent all her time between shows with the baby. 
"I'll rock you in my arms, o son of a wood-fetcher," she would sing mockingly. "Close your eyes and sleep, o son of a water-carrier. Let the jolt of the cart rock you to sleep, o son of a cart-driver."

"Woman, are you referring to me, when you sing those songs?" Ugrasena asked angrily. 
"Yes... I am."
"You are trifling with me. I shall leave you, and go away."
"Do as you think best," she shrugged indifferently. 

"It... it's her skill as an acrobat that makes her so bold... so vain... so indifferent!" Ugrasena realized bitterly. 
Ugrasena went to his father-in-law. "I want to become an acrobat."
"I'll teach you the skills."

Driven by a desperate need to prove himself, Ugrasena made rapid progress in his new profession. Soon, his father-in-law announced, "I present before you a new performer—my son-in-law."
When Ugrasena displayed his feats, the crowd went wild. "Look—he has turned seven somersaults! Unbelievable! His wife can turn only four!"

Soon at Rajagriha, news spread that the acrobats were returning and, with them, the treasurer's son. "It seems Ugrasena will perform the impossible feat of turning fourteen somersaults in the air!" 
On the appointed day, the entire city gathered in the square to watch Ugrasena perform. 

Just as he climbed the tall pole and prepared to turn the first somersault, a murmur rippled through the crowd. "Look! The holy teacher!" 
Lord Buddha and his monks had entered the square. The entire crowd turned their backs to the pole, bowing to the Buddha. 

When Ugrasena came back to position at the top of the pole, he realized no one was watching him. "It... it is Buddha!"
As Ugrasena gazed down at Buddha from the top of the pole, a profound change came over him. He saw the vanity and futility of his lifelong obsession with proving his worth through dangerous physical feats just to earn the fleeting respect of a vain woman and a fickle crowd. 

He smoothly slid down the pole, walked past the cheering crowd, and fell at Buddha's feet. 
"Most venerable teacher, please admit me into your fold," Ugrasena begged, finally finding true peace.

## The Harvest

Once a farmer was hard at work clearing his field when Buddha came walking by. 
"Water my field, o cloud with your gentle rain; ripen my crops, o sun, into golden grain..." the farmer sang happily. "Ah! What a fine harvest I will reap!"
"What are you doing, o farmer?" Buddha asked. 
"I am clearing my field, sir."

The next day, Buddha came that way again. "What are you busy with now, o farmer?"
"I am ploughing my field, sir."
On every succeeding day after this, Buddha came and inquired about the farmer's work. 

Then one day, the farmer said, "Sir, I find that from the day I began to clear my field, you have been showing a great deal of interest in my work. So when my crop is harvested, I am going to share it with you. For, you are now my partner."
The days passed and the farmer's crops ripened beautifully. "I shall call in the reapers tomorrow," he thought joyfully. 

But that night, terrible black clouds gathered. Thunder and lightning rent the sky, and a raging, violent storm broke out. 
The next morning, when the farmer hurried to his field, he collapsed in despair. His entire crop was utterly destroyed. "Oh, God—no! I am ruined! And I have promised a share to my partner!"

The grief-stricken farmer returned home and took to his bed, refusing to eat or speak. 
Just then, Buddha arrived there. "Master, just a mouthful of food..." his wife begged him. 
"No—take it away. I will not eat," the farmer wept. 

"O farmer, I wish to speak to you," Buddha said, entering the room. 
The farmer slowly rose and came to sit by Buddha. "Tell me why you grieve, o farmer."
"Last night's storm destroyed my crop... and I cannot keep my word to you."

"You would not grieve thus, if you knew what your sorrow springs from. It is from attachment, o farmer," Buddha explained softly. "Attachment to your crops, to your word, and to me! From attachment springs sorrow, from attachment springs fear. He who is free from attachment is free of the burden of both sorrow and fear."
The farmer bowed. "I have understood, o venerable one."

## The Golden Maiden

In the town of Sravasti lived young Kumara. He was the son of rich parents and he had come of age. 
"Son, you are old enough to be married. We shall choose a good, beautiful girl for you and..." his father began. 
"Father, I don't want to get married," Kumara replied. 

But the old couple did not give up. Every once in a way, they put the question to him again. At last, Kumara thought, "They will not take 'no' for an answer. The only way out is to agree but make sure that they won't find the girl for me."
The young man got skilled artisans to carve a golden image of absolute, matchless beauty. Then, he presented it to his parents. 
"Mother! Father! I will marry but... only one such as this! If you find a woman exactly as beautiful as this image, I will marry her."

His father sighed. "We will find her. This image... will be taken... from town to town... till such a girl is found."
"You'll never find her," Kumara smirked to himself. 

Months rolled by. Then at last one day, a messenger returned. "The maiden is found, sir—in the city of Sagala."
"What? Can it be so?" the father gasped. The delighted old couple sent forth a messenger with rich gifts for the maiden and her parents. "Let her be brought here with all speed."

Kumara received the news with mixed feelings. "When I got the statue made, I never imagined that such a girl could actually be found. But now that she is found, I can hardly wait to see her! They say that compared with the image... she is far, far lovelier! I cannot even imagine the extent of her beauty!"

He impatiently awaited the arrival of the girl. He stood on his balcony for days. "Is that her carriage? No! It is not. Oh, how slowly the hours crawl by! It is night already, the tenth night since she set out. Where is she?"
He ran to his parents. "Father, mother—she has still not come. What could delay her so?"

"She... she must be on her way, son!" his mother stammered. 
"Mother, are you hiding something from me? Is something wrong? Have they refused the proposal?"
"No, my son."
"Then why hasn't she come yet? Why? Why?"

His father sighed heavily. "We must not keep it from him any longer. Yes. He will have to be told. Son... she will never come."
"But... why...?"
"She is dead, son. On her way here, she fell ill and died."

The shock was too much for Kumara. He fainted on the spot. 
Just then, there on the doorstep, stood Buddha. "Arise, youthful one. What is this sorrow that afflicts you?"
"She was the most beautiful woman on earth," Kumara wept. "She was the woman I was to marry."

"O Kumara, you do not grieve because a woman died," Buddha corrected him gently. 
"Un...?"
"You grieve because the woman you *desired* died. From desire springs sorrow; from desire springs fear. He that is free from desire neither sorrows nor fears."
Kumara prostrated himself before Buddha, as the teacher's profound calmness enveloped his own broken heart.

## Buddha and Krisha Gautami

Once, in the town of Sravasti, a woman named Krisha Gautami was sitting desperately by her child's bed. 
"He has not woken up for hours. He must be very ill indeed," she wept. 
Her husband checked the boy's pulse. "Oh, god! No! He is dead."
"Go, son, make arrangements to cremate the body," the grandmother said sadly. 

"Cremate the body? Burn my son? No!" Krisha Gautami screamed, grabbing the child. "I will find a remedy for death!"
"Gautami! Wait! Come back! Don't you understand? He's dead," her husband cried as she ran out of the house. 

"Kind sir, do you know of any remedy for my son?" she begged a passerby in the street. 
"Poor woman! She has probably not seen a dead person before. I must help her," the man thought. "I'm afraid I don't. But Buddha can help you. Go to him."

Full of hope, Krisha Gautami at once went to the sacred grove of Buddha. "My name is Krisha Gautami, venerable one. I was told that you could cure my son. So I have come to you."
Buddha looked at the dead child and smiled gently. "Go, fetch me a few mustard seeds from a house that has never known death."

Krisha Gautami eagerly set out on her quest. She knocked on the first door. "Mother, can you give me a few mustard seeds? I trust this house has never known death."
The woman looked at her sadly. "What are you saying, good woman? We that are living are few, compared with those that have died here."
"Then please take back these mustard seeds, for I have no use for them."

Krisha Gautami went on her quest from house to house, but everywhere it was the exact same. Every single house had known death, and every dead body had been burnt to ashes. 
"...I lost my daughter..." "...My brother died last year..." "They have taken my old father to the cremation ground to be burnt..."

Finally, the realization dawned on her. "So those that are dead can never be cured, after all. Ah, what a vain search was mine!"
Krisha Gautami went to the forest, laid her child upon a soft carpet of fallen leaves and flowers... and then went back to Buddha. 

"Did you get the mustard seeds?" Buddha asked. 
"No, holy sir, I could not get them. There is not a house that has not known death."
"O Krisha Gautami, it is not you alone who have lost a child to death."
"I have realized that, o venerable Buddha. Admit me into your order."

Krisha Gautami was admitted into Buddha's fold. One day, when it was her turn, she lit the oil lamp in the monastery and sat down in front of it to meditate. 
"Life is exactly like these flames," she realized, watching the wicks. "Some burn on; some flicker and go out."
Krisha Gautami continued to meditate with her eyes fixed on the flame of the lamp... and finally attained Nirvana, the perfect state of peace.

## The Perfectionist

There was once a carpenter who prided himself deeply on the impeccable quality of his work. 
"Hmm-m. Good. But not good enough. It needs improving. Now let me see..." he would mutter, sanding a piece of wood for the hundredth time. 

Just then a man walked in. "What can I do for you, sir?"
"My wife has been blessed with a child. I need a cradle."
"You have come to the right man. I'll make you the best cradle you have ever seen. A perfect dream," the carpenter promised. 
"When can I come for it?"
"It should be ready in a week from now."

A week later, the father returned. "I've come for the cradle."
"Eh? The cradle? Oh! The cradle! Ah! The cradle!" the carpenter stammered. "I will be honest with you, sir. It is not yet ready."
"After all, it has got to be the best you will agree. Shall I come tomorrow?"
"Give me a week, sir. The cradle has to be a dream, don't forget, sir."

Week after week, the father came, but the answer was always the same. 
"No, it is not yet ready, sir. I am not satisfied with my work. Whatever I do *must* be perfect."

Soon the child was too old to need a cradle, so the father gave up going to the carpenter altogether. By and by the boy grew up, got married and in his turn became the proud father of a child. 
"Go to our old carpenter, today," the grandfather told his son. "Ask him if the cradle I had ordered for you is ready. Your son could use it."

Soon, the young man arrived at the shop. "The cradle my father ordered for me when I was born should be ready by now. So..."
"It isn't. And look, sir!" the old carpenter scowled, pointing a chisel at him. "Let me make one thing clear. I take extreme pride in my work. And I will not permit you or your father to rush me."

The young man was about to protest but realized it would be absolutely futile to argue with the perfectionist. So he quietly left the place.
