    Vivekananda

    In nineteenth-century India, the true spirit of Hinduism lay hidden under a debris of rituals—rituals disowned by Indian intellectuals and scorned by the ruling British. At such a time was born to Vishwanath Datta and Bhuvaneshwari of Calcutta, a son whom they named Narendranath.

    As a boy, Narendranath, whose pet name was Biley, was strong-willed but restless. One day, his sister complained, "Ma, Biley has taken away my doll again." "Biley! Give it back to her!" his mother demanded. "No, Ma, I won't," the boy retorted.

    But his mother knew of a way to control him. She poured cold water on his head, chanting, "Shiva, Shiva." The magic of the name immediately quietened Narendranath. "There! Now you are calm like Shiva in meditation," she smiled.

    Narendranath was a born leader. Playing with his friends, he would declare, "I am the king. You, my prime minister; and you, the jester." He would playfully demand tribute, saying, "A king needs revenue. You bring me sweets. You bring me marbles."

    Though he loved to play king, what fascinated him most were the wandering sanyasis in their saffron robes. Whenever they passed his house, he would run out to give them food and alms. "Why do you give away everything to the sanyasis?" his mother asked. "Because they have nothing, Ma," he replied. "I too shall be a sanyasi."

    He would often go and sit crosslegged in some quiet corner of the house. "Biley, why do you play sanyasi all the time?" his sister asked. "Shh! Close your eyes and meditate," he instructed. One day, while Naren was meditating in a forest, a snake approached. "Biley, run!" his friends screamed, fleeing in terror. But Naren sat exactly where he was. The snake slithered away without touching him. "You are truly a sanyasi," his friends marvelled.

    Forthright like many children, Naren was bold too. One day, his father was shocked to find him smoking from various hookahs. "Biley, what are you doing?" "I was finding out if a Muslim hookah tasted different from a Brahman hookah," Naren replied simply. Even as a child, he could not appreciate communal distinctions.

    As the boy grew up, he took care to develop his body and his mind. His striking personality stood out in school and college. Professor Hastie remarked, "I have yet to come across a more versatile lad than him. Naren has few equals."

    Naren's enquiring mind soon rejected blind idol worship and led him to the Brahmo Samaj. The leader preached, "God is the eternal immutable being." "Can I see God?" Naren interrupted.

    Soon, he was burning with the desire to know and experience God. One day, while alone with Devendranath Tagore, the leader of the Brahmo Samaj, he asked directly, "Sir, have you seen God?" Taken aback, Tagore avoided the question but remarked, "Naren, you have the eyes of a yogi."

    In his ceaseless quest, he approached many a parson and pandit. "Sir, have you been with God?" he asked one. "No, my son, but I hope to," came the reply. "Sir, you have talked of God. Have you seen him?" he asked another. "No, I haven't. Not yet."

    One day, at a religious meeting, he met Surendranath Mitra. "Naren, I know someone who can answer your question—Shri Ramakrishna of Dakshineshwar," Mitra told him. "Has he really seen God?" "You can ask him. He will be giving a discourse at my house tomorrow."

    When Naren went to Dakshineshwar, Ramakrishna greeted him with tears of joy. "Ah, you have come at last! Why did you keep me waiting so long? I know you. You are Narayana, come down to help humanity." Naren thought, "What does he mean? I am Vishwanath Datta's son."

    Later, Naren asked him the question that haunted him day and night: "Sir, have you seen God?" "Of course," Ramakrishna answered without hesitation. "I see him more clearly than I see you. You can also see him."

    Naren, the western-educated rationalist, found it hard to believe this. On his way home, he told Mitra, "I don't know what to think. I like the place. I like the man. I will come again."

    The face of the man who had seen God haunted Naren, and he began visiting Dakshineshwar frequently. Always skeptical, Naren decided to test the saint. Knowing Ramakrishna could not stand the touch of money, Naren slipped a silver coin under his mattress. When Ramakrishna sat on the bed, he jumped up with a start. "Oh, it's burning! My whole body is burning! Who put this silver coin under my mattress?" "I did," Naren confessed, humbled. "You had said you couldn't stand the touch of money. I wanted to test you."

    Vishwanath Datta noticed his son's growing restlessness. "Naren, I want you to marry and settle down. I have chosen a girl for you," he insisted. "Marriage is not yet for me, father!" Naren refused.

    His father passed away unexpectedly, leaving the family in financial ruin. "Now that father is dead, I have to look after the family," Naren realized. "I must have a steady income. What shall I do? I'll go to Dakshineshwar."

    Seeing him pale and stressed, Ramakrishna said, "If you want something, just go up to Mother Kali and ask her." Naren went to the temple with the intent of asking for wealth. But as he stood before the goddess, he prayed, "Mother! I seek true knowledge." When he returned, Ramakrishna asked, "Did you see my Mother? How much money did you ask for?" "I could not ask her for money," Naren admitted. Ramakrishna sent him back twice more, but each time Naren only asked for devotion and renunciation.

    "I do not want money. You know what I want," Naren finally told Ramakrishna. "Yes, I know. Look into my face," the Master replied. Ramakrishna touched him with his foot, and suddenly the whole world seemed to explode into light, illuminating Naren's consciousness to its very depths. When Naren recovered from his daze, Ramakrishna smiled, "Yes, I showed him the whole of creation."

    After his first spiritual experience, Naren was a changed man. "I want to be absorbed in God all the time," he pleaded. "Don't be impatient, Naren," Ramakrishna advised.

    When Ramakrishna's health deteriorated due to throat cancer, Naren devoted himself to his Master's care. Even in excruciating pain, Ramakrishna continued to teach. "The body is subject to the laws of nature, but the mind is ever in bliss. I talk because I have to share my riches with the world."

    Knowing his end was near, Ramakrishna placed his hand on Naren's head. At the touch, Naren felt the divine spirit of the Master entering him. "My time has come. Naren, I give you all that I have," the Master whispered.

    After Ramakrishna passed away, Naren gathered the disciples. "We must carry on the Master's work. We shall all live together at Baranagar." He shaved his head and donned a monk's saffron garb—the color of renunciation. Bidding farewell to Sri Ramakrishna's wife, Sarada Devi, he set out with his staff. "I must travel from North to South, East to West. I must know my land and my people," he resolved.

    Wherever he went, people rushed to hear him speak. His message was a call to action. "In the name of renunciation, you have become lethargic. In the name of ahimsa, you are practising cowardice. Be active; be brave!"

    He slept in the huts of pariahs, refusing to acknowledge untouchability, and conversed on equal terms with Maharajas. In Alwar, a westernised Maharaja mocked idol worship. "How can God be a thing made of stone?" Swamiji turned to the Prime Minister. "Please take down that picture of the Maharaja and give it to me. Now spit on it! It is not the Maharaja." "Oh, no. How can I?" the shocked minister gasped. Swamiji turned to the king. "Do you see my point now? The stone god is like your portrait—a symbol. It helps the common people."

    At Porbandar, the Prime Minister advised him, "Swamiji, you ought to go to the West. Take to them the values of Hinduism which they have missed for its superstitions."

    Swamiji journeyed down to Kanyakumari. "At last! I have seen my country from the Himalayas to Kanyakumari. I knew her riches. I have seen how poor she is." In a vision, Ramakrishna appeared to him and commanded, "Go!"

    With funds collected by disciples in Madras and a humble contribution from the Maharaja of Khetri, who gave him the name 'Vivekananda', Swamiji set sail for America to attend the World Parliament of Religions in Chicago.

    Arriving in Chicago in July 1893, he found himself lost in the bustling city. He was shocked to learn that the Parliament was postponed to September and that he needed an official organization's sponsorship to be a delegate. Almost penniless, he spent a night in an empty railroad freight wagon.

    He faced racism and rejection as he walked the streets begging for food. Finally, Mrs. Hale, a kind old lady, took him in. On a train to Boston, he met Miss Kate Sanborn, who introduced him to Professor J.H. Wright. Astonished by the young monk's brilliance, the professor wrote to the Selection Committee: "Here is a man more learned than all our learned professors put together."

    The credentials were accepted. When the Parliament finally convened, Vivekananda patiently endured the long-winded speeches of others. When his turn came, he prayed silently to Saraswati.

    "Sisters and brothers of America!" he began. The simple, heartfelt greeting electrified the vast audience. Thunderous applause erupted, lasting two full minutes. He spoke of universal tolerance: "As different streams, having their source in different places, all mingle their waters in the sea, so, O Lord, the different paths which men take through different tendencies... all lead to thee."

    Vivekananda was an overnight sensation. Headlines dubbed him the "Cyclonic Hindu Monk." Though lionised in high society, his heart bled for his homeland. "They have given me this luxurious suite. But of what use is all this to me when my country weeps in poverty?"

    He toured America, drawing large crowds, preaching strength and self-belief. Even when pelted with tomatoes by fanatics in Texas, he stood unperturbed, declaring, "The history of the world is the history of a few men who had faith in themselves. That faith calls out the divinity within. You can do anything!"

    He established a Vedanta Society in New York and wrote his famous work, Raja Yoga. He then travelled to England, where he met Margaret Noble, a young teacher who was so moved by his teachings that she dedicated her life to his mission. She would later be known as 'Nivedita'.

    When Vivekananda returned to India in 1897, his arrival was a national event. The Raja of Ramnad and other eager men removed the horses from his carriage and pulled it themselves. Swamiji's words stirred the sleeping nation. "India, this motherland of ours, from deep, long sleep... never is she going to sleep any more! Arise! Awake! And stop not till the goal is reached!"

    Vivekananda organized his fellow sanyasis into a dedicated band of spiritual and social workers. They nursed the sick, fed the poor, and provided relief during famines. He formally consecrated the Ramakrishna Monastery at Belur as the headquarters of the Order.

    "Alas, in our country the poor remain wretched. But they are the backbone of our nation," he reminded his disciples, urging them to serve the Santhal tribals and other marginalised communities as children of God.

    But his relentless work took a toll on his health. "I must hurry... I have work to do before I die! I know I will die before I am forty," he had predicted.

    On July 4, 1902, he partook of the noon meal with his disciples. At seven in the evening, he asked his followers to attend prayers while he retreated to his room to meditate. An hour later, he lay quietly in his bed and passed into eternity. He was thirty-nine years old.

    Today, a magnificent memorial stands at Kanyakumari, marking the very spot where the great monk discovered his life's twin mission: elevating the masses of India and preaching her universal, spiritual message to the world.
