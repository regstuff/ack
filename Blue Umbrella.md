# The Blue Umbrella

*These stories are by Ruskin Bond.*

## 1. The Blue Umbrella

Ten-year-old Binya lived in Garhwal, a part of the Himalayas. The mountains and forests were her home. 
One evening, she realized it was getting late. "I must find the cows and rush home before it gets dark," she thought. "Neelu! Gori! They must have wandered into the pine forest again."
Suddenly, she heard a familiar *Clink Clink*. "That's Neelu's bell! They must be close by." 

As she searched, she came across a group of people having a picnic. "Looks like we have company. People from the plains," she observed. 
Then, she saw it. Lying on the grass was a bright, beautiful blue umbrella. "...What a beautiful umbrella! Why, it's just like a great blue flower! How I wish it were mine," she thought, captivated. 

She moved to take a closer look. The picnickers noticed her. 
"Hello, look who's here! A little village girl," one of the ladies smiled. 
"Isn't she pretty? But how torn and dirty her clothes are!" another said. 
"They're very poor in the hills. Let's give her something to eat. Come here, little girl."

Binya wanted to run, but the blue umbrella had cast a strange spell on her. She slowly approached. 
"What's that on her neck?" one of the men asked. "It's a necklace. See, there's a claw hanging from it."
The leopard's claw pendant was Binya's lucky charm. It was her most prized possession. 

"The people here wear them to keep away evil spirits. Isn't that right, girl?" the man asked. 
"Oh, I want one too," a lady said excitedly. 
"They're not available in shops," the man chuckled. He turned to Binya. "Let's give her two or three rupees for it. She looks like she needs the money. Well... Oh all right. Here, little girl, will you give us your pendant in exchange for two rupees?"
Binya shook her head slowly. 
"Will you give it to us for five rupees then?"
Binya shook her head again. She pointed at the bright blue object. "My umbrella! She wants my umbrella! Can you believe that?" the lady exclaimed. 
"How silly she is!" 
"How much do you want?" the man asked Binya. 

"Well, you want her pendant, don't you?" the man said to the lady. 
"It's not the same thing!"
"Really now? I'll ask her to go away. We're making such fools of ourselves."
"But I really want that pendant!" the lady insisted. 
Binya's eyes lit up. 
"You know what? Just take the umbrella. Here, it's yours," the lady finally sighed. 
Binya happily handed over the pendant to the lady. Enchanted by the blue umbrella, Binya completely forgot about the picnickers and ran off to find her cows. 

From then on, the umbrella was her constant companion. It was always open, even when it was inside the house, often getting in her older brother Bijju's way. 
"Binya, why can't you ever close this silly thing?" Bijju complained. 
"Because it's not beautiful when it's closed, Bijju," she replied happily. 

She took it everywhere she went. Even to old Ram Bharosa's little tea shop on Tehri road. Every day, the Tehri bus would stop near it. 
As Binya walked past the shop one day, twirling her umbrella daintily, Ram Bharosa called out, "What have you got there, Binya? That's a lady's umbrella. It's only for memsahibs. Where did you get it?"
"Someone gave it to me, for my necklace."
"Your lucky claw! The umbrella is no use to you. I'll buy it from you for five rupees."
"It's worth fifteen."
"But what do you need it for? The sun isn't that hot—and it's hardly meant for the rain. It's just a pretty thing for rich ladies to play with," Ram Bharosa argued. 
"You are right. But it's beautiful, isn't it?" Binya smiled. "It's just so beautiful! I have to own it."
"Well, then I'll give you ten... or wait, twelve!"
"No," Binya refused flatly. 

As Binya walked away from the shop, Ram stared hopelessly at the umbrella. Everyone else on that road stared at it too. It was the most beautiful thing in the village. 

A few days later, Bijju passed the tea shop. "What will you have today?" Ram Bharosa asked. 
"No money," Bijju replied. 
"You can pay me later."
"No, thanks." Bijju knew better. Some of his friends had taken sweets on credit from Ram Bharosa. By the time they realized that they had taken more sweets on credit than they could possibly afford, it was too late. Ram Bharosa either kept or sold their things to clear their debts—earrings, hand axes, knives. 

"Bijju and Binya will not take anything from me on credit. Sigh! I'll never get hold of the blue umbrella," Ram Bharosa thought miserably. 

It wasn't just Ram Bharosa who wanted the umbrella. In the schoolmaster's house, his wife complained, "That farmer's daughter has such a gorgeous umbrella while I, a second-class B.A., have to manage with an ordinary one."
"Why don't we get yours dyed blue then?" her husband suggested. 
"Oh, don't be silly! Of all the ideas!"
Even the pujari who took care of the temple wanted it. "I'm going to buy myself a multi-coloured umbrella the next time I go to town," he declared. 

However, the children of the village simply admired it openly. "It's so pretty, Binya! And the blue is so bright... it's such a nice shade!"
"Here, you can hold it for a little while," Binya would generously offer. 

Then the monsoon came, and big black clouds piled up in the sky. *BOOM!*
The drops multiplied, became larger, and started hammering against the blue silk. *PLOP!*
"Hurray! That is the first drop," Binya cheered. 
Even though the umbrella wasn't really a rain-umbrella, it held up bravely. "Feels as if I'm standing under a waterfall!" she laughed. 
"Binya! Run home, child. You'll get soaked," a villager called out. 
"I've waited for the rain for so long. I'm not going home so quickly!" Binya beamed. 

One day, while Binya was out, a few loose pebbles clattering down disturbed a sleeping snake. A cobra! With a loud *HISSSSSSSSSS*, it reared up. 
As the snake lunged at Binya, she quickly thrust the open blue umbrella forward. "Take that!" 
*THUD!* The snake struck the tough silk, bounced off, and slithered away. 

When Binya rushed home and told the story, her mother gasped. "The umbrella saved my life!" Binya said proudly. 
"Oh thank God you're all right. But... where are the cows?" her mother asked. 
"Oh... er, I completely forgot about them," Binya sheepishly admitted. "Ha ha, it's all right, I'll go get them. Will you lend me your umbrella? What if I see any snakes around?" her brother Bijju teased. 

With time, the colour of the umbrella faded, but it was still beautiful. Binya's fondness for it remained the same... and so did Ram Bharosa's deep desire to possess it. 

"Sigh!" Ram Bharosa groaned one afternoon. 
"What is the matter, Babuji? You seem sad," asked Rajaram, a boy from the next village whom Ram Bharosa had hired to run errands. 
"Oh, nothing, Rajaram. It's just a sickness that has come upon me. It's all due to that girl Binya and her wretched umbrella."
"Hmm... What will you give me, Babuji, if I get the umbrella for you?" Rajaram asked slyly. 
"What do you mean?"
"Do you mean to steal it? But won't everyone know that I've stolen it from Binya? How will I show off with it?" Ram Bharosa worried. 
"Take it to Tehri and have it dyed red," Rajaram suggested. 
"You're a sharp boy. I'll give you two rupees."
"Three."
"Two."
"No."
"Oh fine, you wretched boy!" Ram Bharosa agreed, and a dark deal was struck. 

Rajaram waited for the right moment. One afternoon, when Binya was resting, he snatched the umbrella and ran. 
"Hey! What's wrong? Why are you running?" Bijju shouted, seeing Rajaram sprinting away. 
"My umbrella! That boy has stolen it!" Binya cried. 
"I'll catch him," Bijju yelled, sprinting after the thief. 

Bijju easily caught up with Rajaram and tackled him into the stream. 
"Let me go! I don't want the stupid umbrella," Rajaram cried. 
"Then why did you try to steal it?" Bijju demanded angrily. 
"It was that Ram Bharosa. He paid me to steal it," Rajaram confessed. 

The news spread like wildfire. After that incident, everybody entirely avoided Ram Bharosa's shop. Even the children taunted him. "Children, don't you want toffees?" he would ask. 
"Not from Ram, the trusty umbrella thief!" they would reply. 
Lonely and dejected, Ram Bharosa sat alone in his empty shop day after day. "Will anyone ever come in for a glass of tea again?" he wept. 

Kindhearted Binya couldn't help feeling sorry for Ram Bharosa. She almost felt responsible for his misery. "Look at his face! It is a yard long," she thought. From then on, Binya would close her umbrella whenever she passed his shop. "He'll feel bad when he sees my umbrella."

One day, Binya walked into the shop. "A toffee, please."
Ram Bharosa stared at her in shock. "What is she doing here? Has she come to laugh at me?" He handed her the toffee. 
As Binya walked out, she deliberately left her blue umbrella on the counter. 

"The umbrella! She has forgotten it here," Ram Bharosa thought, his heart racing. "Should I hide it quickly? Nobody can prove that she left it in my shop. But... I am never in the sun or rain. Of what use is the umbrella to me?"
His conscience finally won over his greed. He grabbed the umbrella and ran out. "Binya, wait! You left your umbrella behind."

Binya smiled warmly. "I don't need it any more. You keep it."
"Yes, but an umbrella isn't everything," Ram Bharosa protested. 
"Look! Binya has given me her blue umbrella!" Ram Bharosa tearfully told a passing villager later. 
"She has?"
"But it is the prettiest umbrella in the whole village!"

Hearing of Binya's incredible generosity and Ram Bharosa's genuine change of heart, people slowly came back to his shop again. "One glass of hot tea for me, Ram," a villager smiled. 
As for the umbrella, it was kept outside the shop all the time. Anyone could borrow it. "Can I borrow the umbrella for this afternoon, Ram?" 
"Yes, of course!" 
The blue umbrella now belonged to the whole village. 

One night, Ram's shop had a strange visitor. A hungry bear! The bear knocked over some things in search of food and accidentally left something behind. 
The next morning, Ram Bharosa found it. "A bear's claw! That's a lucky find!"
He immediately took the claw to a silversmith in town. "I want a silver locket and chain for this claw."

When it was ready, he called Binya. "Binya, I want to show you something. Take it. You gave me the umbrella—I give you the claw."
"It's beautiful!" Binya gasped. 
Wearing her brand new silver pendant, Binya walked back home with Neelu and Gori, singing a happy song that echoed beautifully through the mountains. 
The radiant smile that Binya gave him was something Ram Bharosa would never forget.

## 2. Angry River

In the middle of the big river—the river that began in the mountains and ended in the sea—was a small island. A grand old peepul tree stood on it. It had stood there for fifty long years, sheltering the small family that lived near it. 

There were many stories about the peepul tree. 
"Don't yawn when you are sitting under the peepul tree, Sita," her grandmother would warn. "Or else a spirit will jump down your throat! And the leaf is like the body of Krishna, broad at the shoulders and narrow at the waist."
"And what will happen if a spirit jumps down my throat?" Sita would ask. 
"It will probably ruin your digestion!" her grandfather would laugh. 

Sita lived quietly with her grandparents. Her father worked far away in the city, and she had lost her mother long ago. 
Grandfather had taught her everything a boy would know. "What have you caught today, grandfather?" 
"Mahseer fish. Beautiful, aren't they?"
And grandmother had taught her everything a girl would know. 

One day, tragedy struck. 
"Grandmother is very ill. She has never been so ill before," grandfather said worriedly. "Is your grandmother asleep?"
"She sleeps, but she will wake soon, for she has a deep pain," Sita replied. 
"Tomorrow, I will take her to the hospital in Shahganj in my boat. I might have to sell the goats to pay the doctor. You will have to be on your own for a few days."
"I'm not afraid, grandfather," Sita said bravely. "But the rains are heavy this time... what will I do if the river rises?"
"Keep to the high ground. If the water reaches there, take the hens into the hut, and stay inside."
"And if the water comes into the hut?"
"Then climb onto the peepul tree."

That night, Sita could not sleep. "Mumta, do you think the river will reach the hut?" she whispered to her rag doll. "I am a little afraid of the river. Aren't you? Don't be afraid. The river has always been good to us."
Sita shared all her secrets with her doll, Mumta. Mumta always answered Sita's questions, even if only Sita could hear her. 

The next day, grandfather carried grandmother to his small boat. "Take care, Sita. We will be back soon."
Sita watched the little boat getting smaller and smaller, until it was just a tiny speck on the broad river. "There's so much work to be done. Let me feed the hens first."
A loud crack of thunder echoed. "Thunder! Do you think the gods are angry with me?" Sita asked her doll. 
"Why should they be angry with you? They don't have to have a reason," Mumta 'replied'. "Will you save me if the river rises?"
"Yes, of course!"

The rain poured heavily for two days. "Will I ever see grandmother again?" Sita worried. "The people across the river are moving to the high land. It isn't safe anywhere."
The mud banks of their tiny island were slowly getting eaten away by the rising water. "I'll get a better view if I climb the tree," Sita thought. 
From the branches, she saw devastation. "There's water everywhere! And... that's a dead buffalo floating away!" She quickly got down from the tree. "Shoo! Quick, get inside!" she yelled, herding the hens into the hut. 

As she cooked herself a meager meal, she realized the water was rising dangerously fast. "I will stuff the trunk with all our valuables. The river won't be able to carry such a heavy thing away. I've put in grandfather's hookah and grandmother's walking stick. Maybe I should put some of the tins from the kitchen shelf in the trunk too."
She was so busy saving their belongings that she hadn't noticed the water rapidly seeping into the hut. 
"The curry must have cooked by now... oh no!" The water was ankle-deep inside! 

Forgetting everything else, she grabbed her doll and ran outside. "The hens will climb on the rock for safety."
Sita watched in absolute horror as her belongings began floating out of the submerged hut. She waded through the rushing water to the peepul tree and climbed high into the branches. "Grandfather's turban and.. oh no, Mumta! She can't swim but she can float," Sita cried, holding her doll tight. 

The hut was completely surrounded by deep, raging water now. Suddenly, the mud walls collapsed. "Our home! The walls are breaking!"
Then, she felt a terrible vibration. "The tree is shaking."
The river was like a wild beast. With a flurry of its beautiful leaves and a massive surge of mud from below, the ancient tree left its place in the earth. The tree groaned loudly as the stones holding it in place were completely washed away. 
"It's going to fall!" Sita screamed. 
*SPLASH!*

The massive tree fell sideways and moved out into the deadly main current of the river, as Sita clung tightly to one of the thick branches. "Any moment now, the tree will turn over and I'll be in the water," she sobbed. "Those boats in the distance are too far away. The people won't be able to hear me."

With aching arms, Sita clung on for hours until, with a heavy jolt, the tree snagged on a submerged obstacle. "We've hit a sand bank. I must try swimming."
Just then, a voice called out over the roaring water. "Hellooooo! Someone's calling!"
A boy rowing a sturdy little boat maneuvered expertly through the dangerous currents towards her. "Here, take my hand."
Sita climbed into the boat, exhausted. The little boat moved swiftly down the river, leaving the fallen tree far behind. "I'm never going to see it again," Sita wept silently. 

"I've seen you on the island. Where are the others?" the boy asked gently. 
"Grandmother was sick, so grandfather took her to the hospital this morning."
"Where have you come from? I've never seen you around," Sita asked. 
"I live near the foothills. I was trying to get across the river with the news that one of the villages was badly flooded, but the current was too strong. So I was swept past your island. That's when I saw you. My name is Krishan. My father has many cows and buffaloes, but several have been lost in the flood."

Krishan took out two sweet, ripe mangoes and handed her one. 
"Thank you! I haven't tasted one in over a year," Sita smiled gratefully. 
"I suppose you go to school," Krishan asked. 
"No, there is too much work at home," Sita said, her heart sinking at the word 'home'. "Oh yes, there is a school not far from our village. Do you have to go to school?"

As the evening approached, Sita pointed to the water. "A python! What if it gets into the boat?"
"Don't worry, it won't bother us. The animals are interested only in reaching dry land. It's getting dark. We'll tie the boat here to these branches and rest for the night. Sleep, I will keep watch," Krishan promised. 

Sita stretched herself out in the boat and drifted off to sleep. "I hope grandmother and grandfather are all right," she prayed. As she drifted off, she looked at the boy playing his flute. "He looks so much like the blue-skinned Krishna. I wonder if he is... No, no, how silly I am! He's just a village boy... but he's different."

After a few hours, a loud trumpeting woke them. "A baby elephant! He's calling for his mother," Sita gasped. 
Soon, a larger elephant came splashing through the shallows. "That must be the mother!" Krishan smiled as the young elephant went stamping excitedly towards the sound. "It's all right now. You can go to sleep again."
"I don't think I will sleep now."
"Then I will play my flute for you, and time will pass quickly."
Sita was enchanted by the sweet music. The sound gently rocked her to sleep, and little Sita was lost in the land of dreams once again. 

At sunbreak, Sita woke up with a start. "It's morning! I must not wake Krishan." But the warm sun soon woke the boy. 
"The last mangoes," Krishan offered. 
"I'm hungry."
"So am I."

After they had eaten, they rowed on. For an hour, they glided through the flooded forest, marveling at the tall, sturdy trees. Soon, the forest cleared. 
"I can see rooftops! It's a village!" Sita cheered. 

The villagers were generous and helpful. "Share our food and rest for a while," they offered. 
Sita found an old farmer who offered to take her to Shahganj on his bullock cart. 
"Will you come with me?" Sita asked Krishan. 
"No, my people will be coming to this village to look for me. I must stay here and wait for them. You will be all right now. Your grandfather will be anxious. You must go to him. The water will soon go down and you can return to the island."

Sita hesitated. Krishan smiled and handed her his beloved wooden flute. "Please keep this flute for me. It is a good flute."

The bullock cart was agonizingly slow and often got stuck in the mud. Finally, after travelling for a day and a night, they reached Shahganj. 
Sita thanked the farmer and walked down the narrow, bustling lanes of the town, searching frantically. Then, sitting outside a small clinic, she saw a familiar turban. 
"There he is! Grandfather!" she cried, running to him. 

"Sita! But how are you here? How did you get off the island? I was so worried—it has been very bad these last two days," the old man wept, holding her tight. 
"Is grandmother all right?" Sita asked. 
The old man was silent. Tears welled in his eyes. 
Sita understood. "Grandmother has left us..." she thought. "But I cannot cry. It will make grandfather more unhappy."

They waited a few days in Shahganj for the river to retreat, and then grandfather rowed them back to their island. 
When they arrived, the island was barren. "I can't believe that the tree is gone. It was a part of the island—and my life," Sita whispered sadly. 
"It was the tree that saved you," grandfather said. "Yes, and the boy."

"Grandfather, look! Our iron box is still here! It didn't wash away!" Sita called out. 
They opened the trunk. "Oh no, there's water inside. The food is completely spoilt, and so are the clothes," Sita sighed. 
"But my hookah is fine!" grandfather smiled through his grief. 

For three days, Sita and her grandfather stayed in a makeshift shelter made of jute bags while they rebuilt their small mud hut. 
Sita found a ripe mango seed in the mud and planted it exactly where the grand old peepul tree had stood. "It will be a long time before it grows into a huge tree, but that doesn't mean I can't dream about feasting on its mangoes."

The rains ceased completely. The river was free of mud again, flowing clear and blue. Grandfather would often catch fish, which Sita would cook for their meal. 
In the quiet evenings, Sita would sometimes try and play the flute that Krishan had given her. "Oh, all right, Mrs. Goat! I know I can't play well," she would laugh when the goat bleated at her off-key notes. 

One bright, sunny day, she heard the unmistakable sound of sweet flute music! "No, it can't be. I'm just imagining it," she thought. 
Then, she heard a soft footfall behind her. It was Krishan!
"I thought you'd never come!" she cried happily. 
"I was waiting for the rains to stop. Did you keep my flute?"
"Yes, but I cannot play it properly."
"I will teach you," Krishan smiled. 

Sita sat with her friend by the riverbank. "Sometimes, the river is angry, and sometimes it is kind," she said softly. "We are part of the river. We cannot live without it."
It was a good river, deep and strong, beginning in the mountains and ending in the sea.
