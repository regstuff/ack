    Tukaram

    Tukaram was the son of Bolhoba, a farmer by birth who had chosen the profession of a grocer. Bolhoba and his devoted wife Kanabai lived in Dehu, a village near Poona in Maharashtra. Their life was simple and peaceful, yet they made regular pilgrimages to Pandharpur to pray for the one blessing they lacked in life—a child.

    "Kana, it is good to be alive," Bolhoba would say. "Our life is simple, yet full of peace."

    "Yes, my Lord. But... but if only we had a child," Kanabai would sigh softly. "My yearning sometimes becomes too much for me."

    One night, Bolhoba's unwavering faith was rewarded. The great gods Shiva, Brahma, and Vishnu came to him in a glorious vision.

    "You and your wife have pleased us by your devotion," the gods smiled. "We have come to grant you a boon. What will you have?"

    "My Lord, if it pleases you, a son! A son who will be a comfort to us in our old age," Bolhoba bowed deeply.

    "Bolhoba, you shall have the son you wish for," the gods promised.

    As each of the three gods had promised, Kanabai in due course was blessed with three sons—Savaji, the gift of Shiva; Tukaram, the gift of Vishnu; and Kanhoba, the gift of Brahma.

    The babies soon grew up into strong, healthy boys. However, as they grew older, Savaji, the eldest, showed deep signs of wanting to become a sanyasi (ascetic).

    "Dear Father, forgive me... but business does not interest me," Savaji said one day. "I would like to be a sanyasi."

    "I understand, Savaji," Bolhoba sighed, having expected this. "Never mind. You may go with my blessings."

    Bolhoba then sent for his second son. "Tukaram, I need help at the shop. Will you come?"

    "I will, Father. When should I begin?" Tukaram agreed readily.

    Tukaram soon mastered the grocery trade and was very helpful to his father. When he came of age, he was married to a good girl named Rakhmabai. They soon had a son whom they called Mahadev.

    But Rakhmabai, unfortunately, suffered from severe asthma and would be bed-ridden for months at a time.

    "Managing both the business and household affairs will prove too much for Tukaram. Poor Rakhma is always ill," Kanabai worried.

    "Why don't we get him married again?" Bolhoba suggested. "When we are dead and gone there will be someone to look after Tukaram, as well as Rakhma and Mahadev."

    So, Tukaram was married once again, this time to Avalibai, the daughter of a wealthy banker from Poona. Tukaram was a good, considerate husband and was miraculously able to keep both wives happy and peaceful under one roof.

    But Tukaram's happiness was short-lived. When he was barely eighteen, both his parents passed away.

    Overwhelmed with grief, Tukaram turned for solace to his favourite deity, Lord Vithoba, spending hours in prayer. He began neglecting his grocery business. Unscrupulous people took advantage of his trusting nature, and soon the once-prosperous family was deep in debt.

    His friends began to counsel him. "Tukaram, you are a householder. Religion is necessary, but mere worship of Vithoba is not going to feed your starving family."

    "All right. If you insist. What should I do?" Tukaram finally relented.

    "We are going to a far-off market to buy grain which we will sell at Dehu. Will you work with us on the deal?"

    Tukaram agreed and went with the traders. They bought the grain and set out on the homeward journey through the dense jungles.

    On the way, towards evening, Tukaram brought his horse to a halt and dismounted.

    "What is the matter?" his friends asked.

    "It's time for my evening prayers."

    His friends were incredibly annoyed. "This is no time for prayers! It will soon be dark and these parts are dangerous. Come on, let us leave him with his prayers and his grain to the mercy of the jungle. Our families have to be fed."

    When Tukaram finished his prayers, it was pitch dark. Suddenly, a streak of lightning lit up the jungle, followed by a deafening clap of thunder. The rain came down in violent torrents. His frightened horse reared up, and the heavy sacks of grain fell off its back into the mud.

    "Lord! Have you too deserted me?" Tukaram called out from the depths of his heart.

    Just then, a stranger rode up in the darkness. "Young man, what are you doing all alone in the jungle at this hour?"

    "I am a trader. My friends deserted me while I was lost in prayer," Tukaram explained.

    "Come. Let us lift these sacks onto your horse. I know my way well through the jungle. I will lead you out," the kind stranger offered, dismounting.

    Because it was dark, Tukaram had not seen the stranger's face. But once they were safely out of the jungles, the storm cleared, and a brilliant, divine light shone from the stranger.

    "Lord Vithoba! You... you... you remembered me, my Lord!" Tukaram gasped, falling to his knees. But before he could say anything more, the Lord vanished.

    Tukaram rode back to Dehu more deeply engrossed in his Lord than ever before.

    The next year, the rains failed completely, and a terrible famine hit the region. At Tukaram's house, things were desperate.

    "Mahadev has been crying for food. I am sinking fast. Please bring us some food," his frail first wife, Rakhmabai, begged.

    "I have tried my best, Avali," Tukaram told his second wife helplessly. "Other families too are suffering like us. It is the will of God."

    The ailing Rakhmabai and little Mahadev could not hold out for long, and soon they tragically perished from starvation.

    "Don't grieve, my Lord! I am still there. I shall always stand by you," Avali comforted her heartbroken husband.

    The next year, the rains finally came, bringing fresh hope. "Dear husband, it is every man's duty to earn a good living," Avali suggested gently. "Why don't you work for a farmer?"

    Tukaram agreed and started working for a local farmer. "The crops are almost ready for harvesting. It will be your duty to shoo away the birds from the fields," the farmer instructed.

    But alas! Tukaram, while sitting on a high platform looking after the crops, suddenly fell into a deep, devotional trance, singing praises of God. The birds swooped down and feasted on the crops.

    When the farmer came on his rounds, he was furious. "What have you been doing, you scoundrel?" he yelled, seeing his ruined field.

    He dragged Tukaram to the village headman. "He must promise to pay me two khandis of grain. That would be the yield if he had not let the birds get at the crops."

    "That's fair enough," the headman nodded. "As for you, Tukaram, whatever is left of the crops is yours."

    When Avali heard the news, she stomped up to the field. "How can you? If we reap what is left, it will at least feed us for a few days."

    So they began to reap the ravaged crops. "Avali, even if we had left this for the birds we would not have starved. Vithoba would have looked after us," Tukaram smiled patiently.

    Pleased with Tukaram's unshakeable faith, Lord Vithoba decided to reward him. When the paddy was fully reaped, Avali and Tukaram were astonished.

    "This is a miracle... Vithoba... There are eighteen khandis of grain in all, my Lord!" Avali gasped. "Nine times the quantity the farmer expected!"

    Avali looked forward to enjoying their massive windfall. "Let us sell this, pay back the farmer and live a life of ease with what's left."

    But Tukaram had different ideas. "The extra belongs to Vithoba and to Him it shall go! I shall use the money to restore our ancestral temple of Vithoba."

    Avali was furiously angry. She raved and ranted. "At this rate, I shall never know ease. I am doomed to live the life of a labourer!"

    But after a while she calmed down. "I'll write to Father and ask him for a loan of two hundred rupees. That should enable my husband to start his trading afresh."

    A few days later, the money arrived. Avali went up to Tukaram. "Dear husband, use this money to buy some goods and start your trade again."

    Tukaram started out with every intention of pleasing Avali. But on the way, he saw a commotion. A group of angry people were belabouring a meek Brahman. "Pay up or else..." they threatened.

    Tukaram ran to the Brahman's aid. "Stop! What has he done to deserve this?"

    "He owes us money and refuses to pay," they yelled.

    "How much does he owe you?"

    "Two hundred rupees in all."

    Tukaram did not hesitate for a single moment. He handed over Avali's entire loan. "Here, take this and leave him alone."

    "You have saved my life. I am grateful to you," the weeping Brahman said.

    "Do not be grateful to me," Tukaram smiled. "It was Lord Vithoba who came to your aid. He guided me to you. Offer your thanks to Him."

    And Tukaram returned home empty-handed. When Avali heard what he had done, she cursed him and created such a massive furore that all the villagers came out.

    "What is the matter? Who has wronged you? We shall deal with him," they demanded.

    When they learnt the whole story of the wasted loan, they were convinced of one thing. "He is a madman! Let us give him his due."

    The angry mob grabbed the astonished Tukaram, put a humiliating string of pungent onions round his neck, placed him backwards on a donkey, and paraded him through the village streets!

    The miserable Tukaram somehow managed to shake off his tormentors. I'd better run away to the hills. They will not follow me there, he thought, escaping into the wilderness. Once in the safety of the hills, Tukaram spent his days in unbroken meditation and prayer.

    Meanwhile, Avali returned home to find Tukaram's brother, Kanhoba, waiting for her. "Why are you weeping? Where is Tuka dada?" he asked.

    Avali broke down and told him everything. "Dear brother, what have they done to you? I shall find you and bring you back in honour," Kanhoba vowed.

    After a long search, Kanhoba found Tukaram in the hills and brought him back. Back at Dehu, Kanhoba, a clever and practical youth, soon revived the family business and became highly prosperous.

    "Tuka dada, you are now a rich man. Here is your share," Kanhoba said, handing him a heavy bag of gold.

    But Tukaram would have absolutely nothing to do with worldly matters anymore. He took the bag of gold and calmly flung it into the deep river!

    "Even Kanhoba will not have any patience with me now. I shall return to the hills," Tukaram sighed.

    Once back in the quiet hills, Tukaram intensified his meditation. At last, he achieved true enlightenment. Lord Vithoba came to him in a radiant dream.

    "Pure saint, wake up. When Namdeo was on earth, he made a vow to write a lakh of devotional verses. He died before he could write all of them. You, Tukaram, have my blessings. You shall have the power to complete the unfinished work."

    Tukaram woke up deeply inspired. He began the grand task of writing his famous poems. "I too like Namdeo shall use the abhang metre," he decided.

    Meanwhile, the loyal Avali made a difficult decision. "I have promised to stand by my husband. If he will not come to Dehu, I shall go up to the hills each day and look after his needs."

    And Avali kept her promise. Every single day, she hiked up the rugged hills carrying fresh food for her husband. "Why do you torment me? Why can't you be like any normal husband?" she would scold him, even as she served him lovingly.

    One day as she was on her way, she stepped on a massive thorn. "Ah... H... H... E... E...!" she gasped in pain. "I will pull it out and walk on. My husband must be hungry."

    When she reached him, Tukaram saw her bleeding foot and was deeply touched by her unspoken devotion. Gently pulling out the remainder of the thorn, he said softly, "No. I cannot let you suffer like this for me. I shall return with you to Dehu."

    But he could no longer return to the grocery business, and this time, Avali understood. He is different from other men. It is best that he choose his own path, she thought proudly.

    Every morning, Tukaram went to the hills and in solitude composed his beautiful abhangs. In the evenings, he sang them in the village. "He is a true saint. Let us listen to his kirtans instead of Mambaji's sermons," the villagers whispered.

    Mambaji, a corrupt and arrogant local preacher, seethed with violent jealousy. "Ever since that upstart Tukaram started his kirtans my earnings have dwindled. I must teach him a lesson."

    He did not have to wait long. One day, a villager told him, "Sir, Tukaram's cow has entered your fields and damaged the crops."

    Mambaji grabbed a heavy, thorny stick and ran to the field. There, he mercilessly beat both the innocent cow and her master. Tukaram did not utter a single cry of pain or raise a hand to defend himself. He had truly become a saint, devoid of anger.

    But Mambaji was not his only enemy. One day, Rameshwar Bhat, an orthodox Hindu scholar, came to him. "Tukaram, you are a low-caste Hindu. You have no right to expound the philosophy of the Gita. Leave that to the qualified Brahmans."

    "I demand that you throw your works into the river Indrayani," the arrogant scholar commanded.

    "I shall obey," Tukaram bowed humbly. He took his precious manuscripts to the river and cast them into the water. "But I shall fast till the gods give me justice," he vowed, sitting on the river bank.

    For twelve long days, Tukaram fasted in deep prayer. On the thirteenth day, the water bubbled and parted. Indrayani, the resplendent Goddess of the river, emerged holding his completely dry manuscripts!

    "Moved by your devotion, I have come to restore your work to you," she smiled.

    When Rameshwar Bhat heard of this absolute miracle, his arrogance shattered. He came running and fell flat at Tukaram's feet. "Master, will you accept me as your humble disciple?"

    And so Tukaram continued teaching and preaching, his fame spreading far and wide. His abhangs became so famous that even the great Maratha King, Shivaji, became his devoted disciple.

    "Will you recite a katha in the temple in Poona, where I used to worship as a child?" Shivaji requested one day.

    "With pleasure, sir," Tukaram agreed.

    But the jealous Mambaji found out. He tipped off the local Mughal commanders. "Shivaji, they say, is at the temple, unarmed and without his guards," the enemy captain grinned. "Good. Surround the temple and search for him in the audience."

    As the armed soldiers burst into the temple searching for Shivaji, panic erupted. "Don't let him go. Catch him!" they yelled.

    They gave chase to a man who looked exactly like Shivaji, but never succeeded in catching him. He led them deep into the dark forests, where he simply vanished into thin air! It was Lord Vithoba himself, who had once again come down to protect his pure devotee and his royal disciple.

    Meanwhile, unaware of the danger outside, Tukaram continued with his peaceful katha. When it was over, Shivaji returned home completely unmolested.

    Tukaram taught and preached for many long, fruitful years. Then suddenly, one fine morning, he turned to his wife.

    "Avali, I have come to bid you farewell. I am going to Vaikuntha."

    He walked calmly to the banks of the sacred river Indrayani, and entering the deep water, he gave up his mortal body.

    As Tukaram's pure spirit rose from his earthly body, the sky blazed with divine light. Shri Vishnu came in person, riding his magnificent eagle Garuda, to transport Tukaram's soul directly to heaven.
