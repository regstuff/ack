# Aruni and Uttanka: Tales of Devotion and Reward

## 1. Aruni

One cold winter day, Aruni of Panchala, a young disciple of Sage Dhoumya, was on his way back to the hermitage carrying a heavy load of firewood. As he passed by a field belonging to his master, he noticed a serious problem: there was a breach in the embankment, and the precious water was rapidly escaping! 

"The master's field will dry up!" Aruni realized. He wanted to stop it, but he hesitated. "If I wait here to try and stop it, I'll be delayed. And there's not a bit of firewood at the hermitage!" 

Meanwhile, at the hermitage, Sage Dhoumya and his disciples had assembled for the day's lessons. "Where is Aruni?" the sage asked.
"He's gone to fetch firewood," a disciple replied. 
Dhoumya was pleased with the boy's dedication. "How responsible and devoted Aruni is! A disciple like him is hard to find."

Just then, Aruni came running in, panting under the heavy load of firewood. "Ah! There you are! Why are you late, Aruni?" the sage asked.
"There's a breach in the embankment," Aruni gasped. "The water is escaping...."
"Oh! Oh! Go and attend to it. Quick!" Dhoumya ordered. 

Running all the way back, a breathless Aruni reached the troubled spot. He gathered heavy logs and laid them across the gap, packing them tightly with wet mud. "There! That should hold the water in," he said, wiping his brow. 
But as Aruni turned to go, the rushing water washed away the mud and logs. The breach opened again! "Oh, no! It's given way! What shall I do? If I go back for help, the field will be drained dry!" 

As he stood gazing helplessly at the breach, a desperate idea occurred to him. For a moment he wavered, knowing he would miss his studies for the day. "But my guru's field! I must save the crops!" Without another thought, Aruni lay down in the freezing mud and water, using his own body to plug the breach. 

Meanwhile, engaged in his daily routine, the sage forgot about the errand he had sent Aruni on. Towards evening, Dhoumya noticed the lamps were not lit. "It's not like Aruni to forget his duties. Where's Aruni?"
"You sent him to the field on an errand, master. He has not yet returned," the other disciples reminded him. 
"Not yet returned? I hope no mishap has befallen him. Come, let us go to the field and see."

When the anxious sage and his disciples reached the dark field, there was no sign of the boy. "Aruni-i-i-! Aruni, my child... Where are you?" Dhoumya called out. 
Suddenly, a feeble, shivering voice called out from the mud. "I am here... Master!" 

Tears filled his eyes as the sage bent down to lift up his half-frozen disciple. "He's been here all day! He stopped the gap with his own body! Bless the child! My child, are you all right?"
"Aruni! You're cold and stiff!" the other disciples cried, helping him up. 
"The water, master! It's flowing out! The crops..." Aruni shivered, still worried about his duty.
"Never mind, my son," Dhoumya said gently, wrapping his shawl around the freezing boy. "Don't worry, Aruni. We'll take care of it."

Later, when the breach had been properly attended to, and they had all returned to the warmth of the hermitage, Sage Dhoumya blessed his devoted pupil. "My son, your devotion and obedience to your guru are unmatched. They shall forever bring you renown."

## 2. Uttanka

Many disciples came to the hermitage of Sage Gautama, but the one who won his special affection was Uttanka. When Gautama worshipped Agni, the God of Fire, he always chose Uttanka to assist him. Uttanka worshipped Agni with deep devotion, not for himself, but for the sake of his beloved guru. 

The years passed. One day, Gautama called his disciples to him and announced, "You have acquired mastery of the Vedas. You may now go and set up ashrams of your own."
One by one, the disciples came to Gautama to be blessed, offered their *guru dakshina* (teacher's fee), and departed. 
Uttanka happily awaited his turn. "What *dakshina* should I offer my guru? I'll leave it to him to decide," he thought. 

But when Uttanka approached the sage, Gautama stopped him. "No, not you, Uttanka. I cannot permit you to go. Not yet. I need you here."
Over the years, Gautama became so dependent on Uttanka that even though hundreds of disciples came, finished their studies, and left, Uttanka had to stay on. "Will I ever set up an ashram of my own? But how can I go when my guru needs me?" Uttanka sighed, accepting his fate. 

So Uttanka, serving Gautama and his family, grew old without either his guru or himself realizing it. One day, returning from the forest completely exhausted under a heavy load of firewood, Uttanka threw the wood on the ground in sheer fatigue. As he did, he noticed something caught in the twigs. "What's that? A lock of silver hair! It is my own! I have grown old! Decrepit!"

Overwhelmed with sorrow, Uttanka went to Gautama and poured out his woes. "Old age has overtaken me without my being aware of it. And I have not yet tasted worldly happiness. Though I have lovingly served you for many years, you have not yet permitted me to go."
Gautama listened patiently and smiled. "My son, I kept you here because of my love for you. But if you wish to go, you have my permission and my blessings. And... if, with the strength of your penances, you become a young man again, I shall give my daughter to you in marriage."

And, lo! Through the immense spiritual power he had accumulated by serving his guru, the very next moment Uttanka regained his lost youth! Gautama placed his daughter's hand in Uttanka's and solemnized their marriage. 

Before leaving, Uttanka asked, "What *guru dakshina* should I give you?"
"Your excellent conduct as my disciple has been fee enough. I need no other," Gautama replied warmly. 
"Then, with your permission, may I bring something for mother?" Uttanka asked. 
"If it pleases you, you may do so."

Uttanka went to Gautama's wife. "What shall I bring for you, mother?"
"If you must, then bring me the celestial earrings worn by the wife of King Saudasa," she requested. 
"My penances will aid me in obtaining the earrings for you," Uttanka promised. He asked his bride to wait at the hermitage and set out. 

When Gautama heard what his wife had asked for, he was alarmed. "It was not wise to demand such a fee! Saudasa has been cursed by Sage Vasishtha and is now a cannibal! He is sure to kill Uttanka."
"Oh, I didn't know that!" she cried in dismay. "But, my lord, surely he can be protected by your grace?"
"And my prayers! He can and he will," Gautama assured her. 

Meanwhile, Uttanka trudged through a remote forest and finally found King Saudasa, who now lived as a wild cannibal. 
"Ah! I am lucky," Saudasa roared. "You have come just in time, for it's the sixth hour of the day and I'm hungry."
"Wait, O King! I have come on an errand for my guru. It's against *dharma* to injure anyone who is on such a mission," Uttanka reasoned. 
"But this is the only hour when I am permitted to eat. So I will not let you go," Saudasa argued. 
"Then let us make a pact. After I have completed my errand, I will return and offer myself to you," Uttanka promised. 

Saudasa agreed. "What is the errand?"
"I have come to beg from you the celestial earrings worn by your wife!" 
Saudasa was taken aback. "They belong to my wife. Please ask for something else. Anything. I'll give it to you."
"It is the earrings I'll have and nothing else. If our pact means anything to you, if you are one who keeps his word..." Uttanka pressed. 
Trapped by his honor, Saudasa relented. "All right! Go to my queen and tell her that I command her to give the earrings to you."

Uttanka found the queen and delivered the message. "If it is the command of my husband, and for his good, I will obey," she sighed. "But take care of them. These earrings free the wearer from hunger, thirst, fear, poison, and fire. The Nagas (serpent demons) are waiting for an opportunity to steal them. Hide them securely in the folds of your deerskin."

Uttanka hid the earrings and returned to Saudasa to fulfill his half of the bargain. "I have got the earrings. I will give them to my guru's wife and then return to you, as I promised," he told the cannibal king. 
"Ah! My friend, you have returned!" Saudasa greeted him. 
"There is, however, one question I'd like to ask you before I go," Uttanka said cleverly. "The learned say that one who does not take the advice of a friend is a fool. And one who wrongs a friend is a villain. And you, O King, have become my friend today. Would you, a friend, advise me to return to you, a cannibal?"
Saudasa laughed, realizing he had been outwitted. "Well, as a friend, I would say you'd be a fool to come back!"
"That's all I wanted to hear, my friend. Good day!" Uttanka smiled, and hurried back toward the hermitage. 

On his way back, exhausted and hungry, Uttanka stopped to pick some ripe *bilva* fruit from a tree. Remembering the queen's warning about the Nagas, he carefully placed his deerskin bundle on a high branch. But as he climbed, a falling fruit knocked the deerskin to the ground! Instantly, a Naga slithered out from the bushes, grabbed the glowing earrings, and slid down into a deep anthill. 

Furious, Uttanka grabbed his wooden staff and began desperately digging into the anthill, trying to dig his way to the netherworld of the Nagas. He dug continuously for thirty-five days! 
On the thirty-sixth day, an old man appeared. "It's a vain pursuit, my son," the old man said gently. "The region of the Nagas is thousands of *yojanas* away. You can't reach it by digging with your staff."
"If I can't recover the earrings, I shall give up my life," Uttanka declared fiercely. 

The old man transformed into his true form—Lord Indra, the King of Gods. "Since you are steadfast in your resolve, I shall aid you," Indra smiled. "Hold up your staff. Now your staff is charged with the force of thunder!"
With a few mighty strokes of his thunder-charged staff, Uttanka tore a massive hole in the earth and descended straight into the magnificent underground realm of the Nagas. 

But he was stopped by a massive, locked gate. Suddenly, a blazing steed appeared before him. "Blow hard at me!" the magical horse instructed. Uttanka hesitated, but the horse urged, "Do not hesitate, my son. You have often done it at your guru's hermitage. I am the fiery Agni; you have often worshipped me with your guru. I have come to help you."

Uttanka blew hard onto the horse. Instantly, the steed blazed into a magnificent inferno, billowing thick clouds of black, choking smoke over the walls and into the palaces of the Nagas. 
"Aa-ah! I am blinded! I can't see anything!" the Nagas choked and panicked. "It must be the curse of an offended rishi! Throw open the gates!"

King Vasuki and the coughing Nagas rushed out, fell at Uttanka's feet, and worshipped him. "O holy one, why have you caused this affliction to befall us?"
"One of you has stolen the earrings I was taking to my guru's wife," Uttanka demanded. 
The guilty Naga immediately came forward, bowed, and returned the celestial earrings. "Take the earrings, but please rid us of this scourge."

Uttanka thanked Agni, and the smoke instantly dispersed. Triumphant, the devoted disciple returned to the ashram, presented the celestial earrings to his guru's wife, and finally lived happily with his own beautiful bride.
