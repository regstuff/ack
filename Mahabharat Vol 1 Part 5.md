# The Pandavas Recalled to Hastinapura

News reached the blind King Dhritarashtra of Hastinapura that the beautiful princess Draupadi had been wedded—not to an ordinary prince, but to the Pandavas, who were thought to have perished in the treacherous house of lacquer. Spies reported that the five brothers were alive, well, and now powerfully allied with King Drupada of Panchala. 

"Good fortune brings prosperity to the Kurus!" exclaimed Vidura, the wise and righteous minister, upon hearing the news. 

Dhritarashtra, misunderstanding the report, rejoiced, assuming that his own eldest son, Duryodhana, had been chosen by Draupadi. When Vidura corrected him, explaining that the Pandavas were the chosen ones and were being celebrated by Drupada's powerful kinsmen, Dhritarashtra maintained a façade of joy. However, in the privacy of his chambers, he revealed his true, malicious intentions to Duryodhana and Karna. 

"My intentions are the same as yours," Dhritarashtra whispered to his son. "But I would not, even by my demeanour, reveal it to Vidura."

Duryodhana proposed a series of deceitful plots. "Let us try to separate the sons of Madri and Kunti. Or with ample wealth, let us lure Drupada, his son, and his ministers away from Yudhishthira. Or turn Draupadi against them by enticing them with beautiful women. Or let Bheema be killed secretly."

Karna rejected these cowardly tactics. "The King of Panchala is scrupulous and not greedy. He will not sacrifice the Pandavas for all your kingdom," Karna argued. "Nor can Draupadi be separated from them. Time is running out. We must strike before their faith in Drupada becomes firm. Attack them, triumph over them, and make the whole world yours. Force is the prime virtue of the brave."

Dhritarashtra applauded Karna's aggressive stance but decided to summon the elders—Bheeshma, Drona, and Vidura—for their counsel. 

Bheeshma spoke with the wisdom of a patriarch. "I do not like any collision with the Pandavas. Pandu was to me as you are. So are the sons of Kunti and Gandhari to me. Let half the kingdom be given to them. It is, indeed, the inheritance of those noble Kuru scions."

Drona concurred. "I concur with Bheeshma. Let the sons of Kunti be given an equal division. Send a courteous messenger to Drupada with precious gifts for the Pandavas and their bride, and invite them back."

Karna angrily accused the elders of malice and poor counsel, but Drona warned him sharply. "If you go against my counsel, which is all for prosperity, the Kauravas will soon come to ruin."

Vidura passionately supported Bheeshma and Drona, emphasizing that no one was a better friend to the king than those two. He urged Dhritarashtra to send a speedy messenger to invite the Pandavas back. Yielding to their wisdom, Dhritarashtra agreed and sent Vidura himself to Panchala. 

Upon receiving the invitation, Yudhishthira looked to Drupada and Krishna for guidance. Drupada stated, "I abide by what Krishna decides, because the Pandavas are as dear to him as to me."

"O King, we submit to your will," Krishna advised. With their blessings, the Pandavas set out for Hastinapura. 

Their return was a moment of immense celebration. The citizens of Hastinapura uttered exclamations loud enough to gladden their hearts, feeling as if Pandu himself had returned. After paying their respects, Dhritarashtra addressed the brothers. 

"Take possession of half the kingdom and rule over it from Khandavaprastha," the king ordered, hoping to avoid further dissension. "No harm will come to you there, with Arjuna to protect you."

The Pandavas departed for the wilderness of Khandavaprastha. Under the guidance of Sage Vyasa, they measured out a plot and consecrated it for building a new city. The city that arose was magnificent, surrounded by a deep moat and sky-high walls equipped with formidable defenses. The streets were wide and safe, adorned with beautiful gardens, orchards, lakes, and pleasure houses. This heavenly city came to be known as Indraprastha. 

Having successfully settled the Pandavas, Krishna and Balarama returned to Dwaraka. The Pandavas lived in peace and prosperity, growing their kingdom day by day. 

One day, the celestial Sage Narada visited their court. After blessing them, he recounted the cautionary tale of two Asura brothers, Sunda and Upasunda. Though they shared one soul and were inseparable in all things, they eventually killed each other out of jealousy over the beautiful Apsara, Tilottama. 

"Therefore, in my affection, I tell you to devise some order lest you should quarrel among yourselves over Draupadi," Narada warned them. 

Heeding his advice, the affectionate brothers established a strict rule: if any brother intruded upon another while he was alone with Draupadi, the intruder must retire to the forest and live in celibacy for twelve years. 

Some days later, a desperate Brahmana rushed to the palace gates. "Thieves are driving away my cows!" he wailed. "Take up arms, O Pandavas! The whole world reviles a king who levies a sixth of the produce and yet fails to protect his subjects!"

Arjuna heard the man's grief and stepped out. "Fear not," he promised. 

But a terrible dilemma faced him. His weapons were kept in a chamber where Yudhishthira and Draupadi were currently together. He could not go out unarmed to face the thieves, but entering the chamber meant breaking the sacred vow. 

*If I do not go to the aid of this Brahmana, the king will be tainted by the sin of omission,* Arjuna reasoned. *If I go in, disregard of the king is a trifle compared to my duty. Be it breaking the rule, be it death in the forest, it is better to lay down one's life in doing one's duty.*

Arjuna boldly entered the chamber, took his bow, and rode out after the thieves, successfully recovering the stolen cattle. 

Upon his return, he approached Yudhishthira. "I have broken the rule by beholding you with Draupadi. I shall therefore go into the forest as agreed."

Stung by grief, Yudhishthira's voice trembled. "Why? I know very well the reason for your entry. A younger brother may, without blemish, go in where the elder brother sits with his wife. I bear no ill will in my mind. Therefore, turn away."

But Arjuna was resolute. "I have heard you say that Dharma is not to be observed with prevarication. I cannot waver from truth. I say this under the oath of my weapons."

True to his word, Arjuna left his brothers and proceeded to the forest, embarking on a twelve-year exile that would lead him on a grand journey across the land.


# Arjuna's 12-Year Exile

Having accepted a twelve-year exile to honor a broken vow, Arjuna journeyed across the land, accompanied by Brahmanas, musicians, and ascetics. Their travels eventually brought them to the pristine source of the Ganga, where the mighty hero decided to camp. 

One day, as Arjuna was finishing his bath in the sacred river, he was suddenly pulled down into the depths. Uloopi, the beautiful daughter of the Naga King, dragged him down to the bed of the river and into her father's magnificent underwater mansion. 

There, Arjuna saw a sacrificial fire already ablaze and completed his daily sacrifice to Agni. Only then did he turn to his captor. "O impetuous girl, your action was rash. Whose daughter are you?"

"O Prince, when I saw you, I came under the spell of Kamadeva," Uloopi confessed. "I am still unwed. Give yourself up to me."

"I am undergoing the vow of Brahmacharya for twelve years. I am not free to act as I like," Arjuna replied gently. 

Uloopi smiled. "I know of your brother's command and of your exile. But the order of exile has reference to Draupadi, and therefore here there is no moral lapse."

Convinced by her logic and wishing to please her, Arjuna accepted her advances and stayed in the underwater mansion for a day and a night. When it was time to leave, Uloopi conferred a powerful boon upon him. "Denizens of the water will always obey you," she promised. "You will be invincible in water."

Arjuna continued his journey, ascending the Himalayas before descending to walk leisurely along the eastern seashore. He visited sacred spots in Anga, Vanga, and Kalinga, eventually reaching the kingdom of Manipura. 

There, he accidentally saw Princess Chitrangada, the beautiful daughter of the ruler, and was instantly filled with desire. He approached the king and boldly asked for her hand. "Give away your daughter to me, O King. I am a noble Kshatriya."

The king agreed, but with one absolute condition. "That son shall be the bride-price you pay. On this condition, O Pandava, you may wed her. The son begotten of you on her shall be the progeny of my line."

"So be it," Arjuna accepted. He spent three idyllic years with Chitrangada in Manipura. After a son, Babhruvahana, was born to her, Arjuna ceremonially presented the child to the king and resumed his wanderings. 

Traveling further south, Arjuna reached regions adorned by holy rishis, but he noticed that five specific water-spots were entirely shunned. When he asked the sages why, they warned him, "Each of these water-spots has a crocodile which drags down the rishis who enter it."

Undeterred, Arjuna went straight to the nearest water-spot and plunged in. Immediately, a massive crocodile seized his leg. Utilizing his immense strength, Arjuna grappled with the beast and dragged the writhing crocodile onto the land. 

The moment the beast touched the earth, it miraculously transformed into a breathtakingly beautiful woman adorned with jewels. 

"Who are you, O pretty woman?" an amazed Arjuna asked. 

"I am an Apsara," she explained. "My four companions and I were cursed by a bright Brahmana when we vainly tried to divert him from his steadfast meditation. He cursed us to become crocodiles for a hundred years, but promised that a great man would eventually lift us from the water, allowing us to regain our forms."

Moved by her story, Arjuna systematically visited the other four water-spots, liberating all five Apsaras from their aquatic curse. 

His journey eventually took him westward to Gokarna, Prabhasa, and near the territories of Dwaraka. When Lord Krishna heard of his dear friend's arrival, he rushed out to greet him. The citizens of Dwaraka thronged the royal path to catch a glimpse of the legendary Pandava, and he was welcomed with grand festivities. 

During a grand festival held on the Raivataka mountain, amidst the music and celebration, Arjuna's eyes fell upon Subhadra, the beautiful sister of Krishna. Arjuna was instantly smitten. 

Noticing his friend's distraction, Krishna smiled. "A recluse of the forest agitated by Kamadeva! That is my sister, and she is the favourite of our father."

"She is irresistibly beautiful!" Arjuna confessed. "If she becomes my wife, all prosperity will be mine. Tell me, O Krishna, the means by which I may win her."

"Swayamvara is the marriage ordained for Kshatriyas, but there is uncertainty in it," Krishna advised shrewdly. "Therefore, Arjuna, carry away my sister by force. Who knows what her choice will be at a Swayamvara?"

Following Krishna's advice, when Subhadra was returning to Dwaraka after worshipping at the mountain, Arjuna rushed out in his golden chariot and forcibly abducted her. 

The news sent the Yadava warriors into a frenzy. Agitated, they gathered in the court, blowing trumpets and preparing for war. Balarama, furious, thundered, "That treacherous wretch deserved not the honour! He has insulted us by abducting Subhadra. I shall exterminate him!"

Krishna sat silently through the uproar until Balarama demanded his opinion. Then, Krishna spoke with calm authority. "This alliance is right. Subhadra is noble and so is Arjuna. His action bespeaks not so much of insult as appreciation of our race. Who would not be honoured by kinship with the descendant of Bharata?"

He looked at the angry warriors. "Besides, if Arjuna goes back to his city after defeating you by force, you will all lose your reputation. There is no disgrace, however, in peaceful accord. Go after him and bring him back in peace."

The Yadavas, respecting Krishna's wisdom, intercepted Arjuna and brought him back with honor. Arjuna and Subhadra were joyously united in marriage. 

After spending a final year in Dwaraka, the twelve years of Arjuna's exile were complete. He returned to Indraprastha with his new bride. However, knowing Draupadi's fiery temper, Arjuna sent Subhadra into the women's apartments dressed simply as a cowherdess. 

Subhadra bowed deeply to Draupadi. "I am your maid," she said humbly. Pleased by her modesty and respect, Draupadi lovingly embraced her, accepting her without rivalry. 

When Krishna heard that Arjuna was safely home, he arrived in Indraprastha with Balarama and the Yadava noblemen, bringing magnificent wedding gifts of unimaginable wealth. While the other Yadavas eventually returned to Dwaraka, Krishna remained in Indraprastha, roaming the banks of the Yamuna and hunting with his dear friend Arjuna. 

In due course, Subhadra gave birth to a mighty son named Abhimanyu, who would grow to equal his father and Krishna in courage and strength. Draupadi also bore Yudhishthira and his brothers five valiant sons. Surrounded by their celestial children and united in brotherhood, the Pandavas lived in supreme happiness and unparalleled valor.
