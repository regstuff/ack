    King Kusha: A Jataka Story About Inner Beauty

    Sheelavati, the chief queen of the heirless King Okkaka of Kushavati, was offered a boon by Indra, king of the gods. "You shall have two sons," Indra told her. "One wise but ugly, the other handsome but a fool. Which will you have first?" "The wise one, my lord," Sheelavati chose without hesitation.

    In due course, Sheelavati gave birth to a son. "He shall be called Kusha," King Okkaka declared. Two years later, she gave birth to the second son, Jayampati. "What a beautiful baby!" the maids cooed over the younger prince.

    Even as a child, Kusha was deeply conscious of his unappealing appearance. "I must excel in all the arts to make up for my ugly looks," he resolved. In the years that followed, Kusha mastered the arts of music, painting, and sculpture, becoming a brilliant artisan and scholar.

    One day, the King told his wife, "Sheelavati, Kusha is almost sixteen years old. I would like to place him on the throne while I am yet alive. But before I do, I'd like to see him married." "I too would love to have a daughter in the palace!" the Queen agreed.

    When Kusha learnt of his parents' wishes, he was sad. "Would any princess marry an ugly fellow like me?" he worried. "But I cannot tell my parents this. It would hurt them. I must find a way out." After giving the matter a great deal of thought, he hit upon a plan. "I will create a beautiful image and ask for the impossible—someone exactly like it for a wife."

    Kusha locked himself in his workshop. When the golden image was completed, Kusha could not help admiring his own work. "They will never be able to find a princess half as beautiful as you!" he thought confidently. He then brought his mother to see it. "If you can find me a princess as beautiful as this, I'll marry her."

    Queen Sheelavati was astounded by the sculpture. She told the king, "Prince Kusha, the gift of Indra, is highly talented. He will have none but a princess worthy of him." King Okkaka summoned his royal councillors. "Take this figure he has created and tour the country exhibiting it. When you come across its living likeness, present the image to her father and request him for her hand on behalf of your king."

    The councillors travelled from kingdom to kingdom, exhibiting the golden image in town squares, till at last they arrived at Sagala, the capital of the kingdom of Madda. As usual, they placed the image at a conspicuous spot. "Now let us stand away from it and overhear the comments of those who pass by," a councillor whispered.

    After a while, a hunchbacked royal nurse followed by seven or eight maids came by. "As soon as we've bathed we will..." one maid began. Suddenly, the old nurse marched up to the golden statue and hit it angrily. "Prabhavati! What on earth are you doing alone here at this hour? Should your father hear of this it will be the end of us! ...Oo-oo-oo! My hand! It's a metal image!"

    "Madam! What's the matter? Your hand! It's bruised and broken!" the maids cried. But the old hunchback soon recovered her composure and began laughing. "Ha! Ha! Ha! What a fool I've been. I mistook this cheap image to be the princess." "Ha! Ha! How could you?" "I have hurt my hands for insulting her. What is this worthless image compared to my darling Prabhavati!" the nurse scoffed.

    The hidden councillors were overjoyed. They stepped out. "What do you mean?" "What I say," the nurse declared. "This image would appear ugly before her. And I am her nurse who says so."

    The delighted councillors immediately sought an audience with Princess Prabhavati's father, the King of Madda. "Your majesty, our king, Okkaka of Kushavati, is anxious to place his son, the brave Prince Kusha, on the throne. We request you on his behalf for the hand of Princess Prabhavati." "We gladly agree," the King of Madda smiled. "We would be honoured by the alliance."

    The councillors rushed back to Kushavati. "Your majesty, we have found her—a princess more beautiful than the image!" When King Okkaka and Queen Sheelavati heard the whole story, they were thrilled. "Let us set out for Sagala at once with a large retinue." "Yes! Let us not waste a moment."

    When they reached Sagala, they were received graciously by the king, the queen, Princess Prabhavati and her seven younger sisters. Later, when Prabhavati came to pay her respects to Queen Sheelavati, the Queen was stunned by her absolute perfection. "Should this dazzling beauty see my son's face, she will run away from him that very moment," Sheelavati worried. "Yet I must not fail my son. Let me see what I can do."

    When Prabhavati had left, Sheelavati spoke to the king of Madda. "Your daughter is worthy of my son. But we have an unusual tradition in our royal family. If she will agree to observe it, we will take her as our daughter-in-law." "What is the tradition?" "A wife is not permitted to see her husband's face in daylight till she has conceived. Will she agree?"

    Prabhavati agreed to respect the royal tradition. After much giving and receiving of gifts, Okkaka and Sheelavati escorted their new daughter-in-law, along with her nurse and a vast retinue, back to Kushavati.

    Back at Kushavati, the King rejoiced. "Have the city decorated. Release all prisoners. The wedding and the coronation of Prince Kusha shall be celebrated today!"

    Following the tradition, neither Kusha nor Prabhavati saw each other's face during the day. In the dark, Prabhavati would listen to him play music. "How well he plays the veena!" she thought lovingly. "He must have the sensitive, handsome face of a musician." Meanwhile, Kusha pined for her. "Ah! I can only imagine how she looks by thinking of my golden image."

    A few days after the wedding, King Kusha came to Sheelavati. "Mother, please permit me to look at my wife, but once." "You will have to wait until she has conceived," his mother reminded him. "I cannot wait till then, mother." Sheelavati relented. "All right. Go to the elephant stalls disguised as a keeper. I will bring Prabhavati there. But see that you do not make yourself known to her."

    Sheelavati then went to Prabhavati. "You have not yet seen your lord's royal elephants. Come, I will show them to you." When Kusha saw Prabhavati as she walked behind his mother, he was utterly enchanted. "Is she a princess or a goddess?" he marvelled. Overwhelmed by her beauty and determined to find out if she was real, he playfully picked up a handful of elephant dung and flung it at her dress.

    Prabhavati was enraged. "How dare you! You ugly fellow," she screamed at the disguised keeper. "I will get the king, my husband, to cut off your hands." Kusha grinned to himself. "She is a true fierce princess!" As Sheelavati pacified her and hurried her away, Prabhavati demanded, "Mother, I must see my husband's face—just once." "No! Please don't ask for that. It would be inauspicious."

    But Prabhavati went on pleading. At last, Sheelavati compromised. "Well, tomorrow my son will be riding through the city in the royal parade. You can open your window and see him." Prabhavati thought happily, "I will finally see him." Meanwhile, Sheelavati secretly arranged for Kusha's handsome younger brother to lead the parade. "I will tell Kusha to send Jayampati in a procession through the streets. He can wear Kusha's royal clothes and ride on his elephant."

    Poor Kusha was not going to miss the chance of seeing his wife again. "Dressed as an elephant-keeper, I will ride behind Jayampati," he decided. The next day, when Prabhavati saw Jayampati on the royal elephant, she was thrilled. "How handsome my husband is! Indeed! I am proud of him." Then, she noticed the keeper sitting behind him. "But what an impudent, ugly elephant-keeper he has! How dare he wave to me!"

    When the procession had passed by, Prabhavati turned away from the window. "Did you see your lord?" Sheelavati asked. "I did, mother. But why do they let such an ugly, impudent man ride behind him?" "Don't be misled by looks," Sheelavati said carefully. "That man is capable, and the king must have a highly capable man sitting behind him to guide the elephant."

    But Prabhavati was not convinced. "No man would be allowed to behave like that, however excellent his services might be. Can it be that he is King Kusha and they do not let me see him because he is so ugly?" She turned to her nurse. "Run, my dear. Find out who was the king—the man in front or the one behind." "How am I to find out?" "The king will be the first to alight from the elephant when it returns to the palace. Go. Don't waste any more time."

    When the nurse hurried to the courtyard and saw the ugly 'keeper' alight first and be treated like royalty, she was shocked. "What! Is that hideous one King Kusha!" Kusha spotted her. "Ah! Isn't that Prabhavati's nurse? She is here to spy on me." He immediately sent for her. "I charge you on pain of death not to reveal my secret to Prabhavati," he commanded the nurse.

    The nurse went back to Prabhavati, who eagerly awaited her return. Fearing for her life, she lied, "The handsome one that sat in front was the first to alight." "Thank God!" Prabhavati sighed in relief.

    The second sight of Prabhavati made Kusha want to see her yet again. He pleaded with his mother. "Well then, if you must, enter the lotus pool in the gardens, conceal yourself under the large lily pads, and wait," Sheelavati suggested. A little later, Sheelavati brought Prabhavati to the garden. "Oh! What a beautiful lotus pond. Mother, may I bathe in it?" "Certainly. I'll go back to the palace, and send some maids with a change of clothes for you."

    Prabhavati waded into the water. "Ah! That lotus is larger and more beautiful than the rest. I'll pluck it for myself." As she stretched out her hand to pluck it, the lily pad suddenly rose. "Ee-ee-ah! A goblin has caught hold of me!" Prabhavati screamed. "I am King Kusha!" Kusha announced, grinning. Seeing his hideous face, Prabhavati swooned and collapsed in the water.

    When she regained consciousness, she found herself in her chamber surrounded by her nurse and her maids. "I was right. It was him all the time," she wept angrily. "Why should I live with such an ugly, hideous man, powerful king though he be." She summoned the councillors who had arranged her marriage. "Have my chariot ready. I will return this very day to my parents." "B-but... yes, your majesty," the terrified councillors stuttered.

    Not knowing what to do, they went and consulted King Kusha. Kusha sighed sadly. "Do not stop her. She would be unhappy here. I will go after her later and bring her back with her willing consent."

    Though Kusha so kind-heartedly let Prabhavati go, he was desolate. Two days later, he went to his mother. "Mother, I will go and bring Prabhavati back, with her consent." "Go, my son. But take care of yourself." Equipping himself with all that he would need, Kusha set out for Sagala. "I will not rest till I win her love," he vowed.

    When he reached Sagala, he entered the palace and found his way to the elephant stalls. "Who are you? What do you want?" the keeper asked. "Let me stay here for the night and I will play the veena for you," Kusha offered. They agreed. Kusha tuned his veena and began to play a hauntingly beautiful melody.

    In the palace, Prabhavati suddenly woke up. "Those pure notes. They can only come from his veena," she realized, her heart aching. "How I loved to hear them till I saw his face. He has followed me here."

    Early next morning, Kusha woke up. "I must now try to meet Prabhavati without letting anyone know who I am." He went to the royal potter and became his apprentice. A few days later, the potter was amazed. "Why, my son, you are incredibly talented! These vases are beautiful! I shall take them to the palace right away." "Master, do not forget. This one with these specific figures is for Princess Prabhavati. I made it specially for her," Kusha instructed.

    At the palace, the King of Madda examined the pottery. "Who has made these exquisite vases?" "I have, your majesty," the potter lied nervously. "This is not your handiwork. Tell me the truth, who made them?" "M-my apprentice, your majesty." "Not your apprentice but your master," the King proclaimed. "Henceforth he shall make all the pottery for my daughters. You may take the vases to them. Ah! You may give him these gold coins."

    When the princesses saw the vases, they were thrilled. "How delightful they are!" "Look at this one. I'll keep it." "Such exquisite workmanship!" The potter came up to Prabhavati. "This he made specially for you, O Princess Prabhavati." Prabhavati looked at the carvings. "Why, these figures...! They resemble me and my nurse perfectly... only he could have made these!" She became terribly angry and flung the vase to the floor, smashing it. "I don't want it. It's hideous." "Ha! Ha! Our sister behaves as if it were a gift from King Kusha himself," her sisters laughed.

    The potter returned and told Kusha all that had happened at the palace. "...But the king certainly liked your work. Here, these gold coins are a gift to you from him." "You may keep the money," Kusha sighed. "It's no use my staying here. I'd better try from elsewhere."

    Kusha left the potter and apprenticed himself to the royal wicker-worker. A few days later, the master worker watched him in awe. "Whoever he is, he is the best worker I've had so far. How deftly his fingers move. What are you making?" "A palm leaf fan for the eldest princess. See, here she is, standing by this pillar," Kusha said, weaving a perfect portrait into the fan. "Remarkable workmanship! I shall take it to the king right away."

    At the palace, the King was once again amazed. "Whose work is this? Not yours, I'm certain." "It's the creation of my new apprentice, sir." "Not your apprentice but your master. Give him these gold coins."

    The moment Prabhavati saw the beautifully woven fan, however, she recognized the style. "This, too, is his handiwork. Why does he pursue me?" She flung the fan on the floor. "Don't you dare show me such inferior work ever again." "Ha! Ha! It was not King Kusha who made that fan. So why do you disown it, Prabhavati?" her sisters teased.

    The wicker-worker returned and told Kusha all that had happened at the palace. "...Women are strange. I don't know why she threw it on the floor in a rage." Kusha knew. "I will have to try some other means to meet her." He pondered for a while. "I know what. I shall seek service in the royal kitchen. Perhaps..."

    Kusha was lucky. The royal cook had sacked one of his assistants that very day. When Kusha approached him, the cook agreed. "In fact, I need a help. But you will have to cook your own meal in the kitchen meant for menials." "That is no problem, sir."

    Kusha soon became an expert cook—better even than his master. One day, the cook gave him some meat. "Here, you may cook this chop for yourself." An hour later, as the king was being served his royal feast, he caught a scent. "Mm-m-m! What a delicious aroma. Is that dish yet to come?" "N-no, your majesty," the cook stuttered. "What you smell is the chop my new assistant is cooking for himself." "Then let me taste it." After the king had tasted it, he declared, "Henceforth your assistant shall cook for me and my daughters. Ah! And give him these gold coins."

    Kusha was extremely happy when he heard about the king's order. "Please keep the coins for yourself, master. I have no use for them. At last I shall be able to see my wife."

    The next day, as Kusha carried the food to the royal chambers, Prabhavati peeked from behind a curtain. "I feel sorry for him," she thought, seeing him doing menial work. "Yet I must not let him know that I do, or he will stay on, thinking I've yielded." She stepped out. "Kusha, I can't bear the sight of your ugly face. Go back to Kushavati. It is not right for you to waste your time here." "I am not going back without you," Kusha replied gently. "I want your love and not the throne. I would gladly give up crown and throne to live near you."

    When Prabhavati heard this she was alarmed. "Suppose he declares 'I am King Kusha' right here and seizes my hand. No one will stop him. Besides, someone might overhear our talk." To discourage any further dialogue, she quickly closed her door and bolted it. Kusha gazed for a moment at the closed door, and then went down. "She is bound to relent. I will wait patiently."

    But seven long months passed and Kusha toiled on in the hot kitchens. At last, his resolve broke. "In seven months, she has not so much as let me have a glimpse of her. Why do I pine for her? She is harsh and cruel. I will return to my kingdom and my parents."

    At that moment, realizing how disappointed Kusha was, Indra, king of the gods, decided to intervene and help him. Using his divine powers, Indra magically sent identical messages to seven different powerful kings in the region. The messages read: "Prabhavati has left King Kusha and returned home. I offer her hand to you." He made sure the messengers claimed to come from the court of Madda.

    When the seven kings arrived at Sagala with their massive armies and discovered that they were all there for the exact same purpose, they were furious. "He has deliberately humiliated us!" "He will have to keep his word to each one of us!" They jointly sent an ultimatum to the King of Madda: "Give Prabhavati to all seven of us or prepare for war."

    The King was utterly terrified and alarmed. "I will have to cut her up into seven pieces and send one to each monarch," he despaired. "How can you do such a cruel thing to your own daughter?" his queen cried. "If I send her to any one of them, it will be war with the other six. My kingdom will be destroyed!"

    When Prabhavati heard of her father's desperate decision, she was terrified. "Why did she reject a husband as powerful as King Kusha?" her angry father yelled. "Let her suffer for it." She ran to her mother's chamber. "Mother! O Mother, I am helpless. If only King Kusha were here, his armies could protect us." "He is here, my child," the queen revealed. "Come, I'll take you to him."

    Prabhavati led her mother to the kitchens. "Look! There he is—in the guise of a cook. He came here and suffered for my sake." She ran forward and fell at Kusha's feet in tears. "My lord, forgive me. I have done you great wrong. I am to pay for it with my life. Yes. This beautiful body of mine, of which I was so vain, is to be cut into pieces, to be given to seven kings, to prevent a war."

    Kusha dropped his ladles, his royal bearing returning instantly. "What! Will anyone dare touch my dear wife when I am yet alive!" "B-but! Wh-what about the seven kings and their armies?" Prabhavati trembled. "Leave that to me," Kusha smiled confidently. "Go. Bathe and adorn yourself in your royal jewels, and wait for me in your palace."

    King Kusha stepped out, revealed his true identity to the stunned court of Madda, and took command. Utilizing his immense wealth, power, and unmatched wisdom, he settled matters peacefully by arranging royal alliances, offering his seven beautiful sisters-in-law to the seven kings.

    With peace restored and Prabhavati's vanity completely humbled by his unwavering love and capability, he and Prabhavati returned to Kushavati in a grand procession, where they lived happily ever after.
