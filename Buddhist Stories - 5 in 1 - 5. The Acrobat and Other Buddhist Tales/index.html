    The Acrobat and Other Buddhist Tales

    Upagupta

    Long, long ago in ancient Mathura, there lived a dancer called Vasavadatta, who was famed for her extraordinary beauty and her art. She had a perfect figure and graceful movements, but an incredibly hard heart.

    After her performances, wealthy admirers would sigh, "I am prepared to part with all my wealth for a single smile from her." But with not so much as a glance at her admirers, Vasavadatta would sit in her chariot and ride home. "They are all madly in love with you. Couldn't you see it in their eyes?" her companion asked. "I chose not to see." "Why, Vasavadatta? Why don't you marry one of them? Is there not a single one whom you deem worthy of your love?" "No! Not one of them possesses what I am looking for," she declared.

    One evening, as Vasavadatta was standing in the balcony of her mansion, she saw a young monk pass by in the street below. She was instantly captivated by his serene face. "Quick! Run down and invite that monk in," she ordered her companion. "I can't believe it," her companion thought. "Vasavadatta showing some interest in a man!"

    Her companion rushed out. "Vasavadatta wishes to see you, holy one. Will you come in?" "Not now," the monk replied calmly. "I will see her at the proper time."

    When Vasavadatta was told of the monk's reaction, she was offended. "Perhaps, he hesitates to visit me because he is poor and can't bring me gifts. Tell him I don't want anything from him." The monk who spurned the invitation of the dancer was Upagupta, a disciple of Lord Buddha.

    Her friend went back to Upagupta. "O monk, my friend does not crave for gifts or riches. Please visit her." "No, I cannot. It is not yet time to visit Vasavadatta," Upagupta replied, and walked away.

    Vasavadatta was stunned. "Why should the only man I choose to love, shun me?" Heartbroken and insulted, she stopped giving dance performances, much to the annoyance of the people of Mathura. "Life in Mathura has lost its charm. How could she be so cruel to us!" they grumbled.

    Meanwhile, her friend was worried. "She sits alone, brooding over that heartless monk. It is not good for her health and looks. I must find something to distract her." A few days later, she suggested, "A famous sculptor is holding an exhibition of his works. Let us go and see it, Vasavadatta." "All right, if you insist."

    At the show, Vasavadatta forgot her sorrow for a while. "Such exquisite workmanship!" she praised a statue. "Will you sell this to me?" "You might find the price too heavy," the chief sculptor warned. "Whatever it may cost, I am prepared to buy it. Quote your price." "It's yours, if you agree to dance again." Vasavadatta hesitated, but her companion pushed her. "You can't go back on your word. You have agreed to pay whatever price he asks." Vasavadatta reluctantly agreed.

    The next evening, people flocked to the town hall. "At last we will see Vasavadatta dance again! Thanks to the chief sculptor of our city. It was indeed a clever bargain!" At the end of the brilliant performance, Vasavadatta received thunderous applause. But instead of making her happy, it only made her brood all the more. "Why did that monk shun me when thousands of people long for a sight of me?"

    In the days that followed, the chief sculptor began to visit the dancer to capture her image in stone. "My art will die with me. But yours will last for centuries," she told him. "My talent, which brings so much happiness to you, only makes my envious rivals hate me," the sculptor sighed.

    A few days later, the sculptor suddenly vanished. "Why doesn't he come? The work is yet to be completed. It's three days since he last came!" Vasavadatta complained. Meanwhile, his friends and relatives too were frantically searching for him. "He was last seen entering the house of Vasavadatta, three days ago."

    Later, the body of the missing sculptor was found buried not far from Vasavadatta's house. She was immediately charged with the brutal murder of the sculptor. "Do you have anything to say in your defence?" the judge asked. Vasavadatta, completely shocked and grieving, had nothing to say. "Vasavadatta is guilty. Confiscate her property and have her banished from Mathura," the judge ordered.

    As Vasavadatta was turned out of her house, an angry mob jeered at her and pelted her heavily with stones. "Take this, you murderess. See that you never darken our city again!" Bleeding profusely and covered in festering wounds, Vasavadatta reached the outskirts of Mathura and found refuge in an abandoned crematorium. "Vasavadatta, I know you are innocent! Your rivals have deliberately done this to you," her companion wept. "Friend, let us not blame anyone," Vasavadatta sighed, resigned to her fate.

    People coming in and out of Mathura looked upon Vasavadatta, now seriously ill with festering wounds, as an untouchable. "Leave her to die. Why do you waste your time on her? She doesn't deserve any sympathy." They would spit at her and sometimes even stone her as they passed by.

    Then came Upagupta, the Buddhist monk. "Friend, cover my body. Let him not see me in this terrible state," Vasavadatta panicked. "Vasavadatta, I have come to you," Upagupta said softly. "O monk, when all Mathura admired me, you alone spurned me. Why have you chosen to visit me now when I am a mass of festering flesh, shunned by all?" she cried. Upagupta smiled gently. "At that time you did not need me, Vasavadatta. You do, now. Come, let us go to my monastery."

    Upagupta took her with him and painstakingly nursed her back to health. "You seem sad, Vasavadatta," he noticed one day. "I am no longer beautiful. No one will look at me now." "Vasavadatta, you are yet to discover a beauty greater than the one you have lost—the beauty of the self."

    Curious to know more, she began to attend the daily discourses of Lord Buddha. "You can't call even the body your own. When the body is cast away it becomes food for others," Buddha preached. "Light the lamp within you, and only then will you find peace." "Light the lamp within you... peace..." Vasavadatta realized the profound truth. Vasavadatta fell at the feet of Lord Buddha. "Lord, admit me into your order." "So be it, my child. May you find peace of mind."

    The Acrobat

    The city of Rajagriha was buzzing with excitement. "Have you heard? They are coming!" "Yes. It's been a year since we saw them last. The acrobats are coming!" A few days later, Rajagriha jubilantly welcomed the traveling troupe of acrobats.

    Crowds gathered in the central square. "Let the show begin," the King ordered. Those at the very back stood on their toes to get a better view. A beautiful female acrobat climbed high up a tall pole and performed breathtaking stunts. "Superb! She looks just like a bird!" the king cheered.

    The young man staring with absolute fascination at the acrobat was Ugrasena, the son of the king's wealthy treasurer. "Yes. The loveliest bird I ever set eyes on. How graceful and skilful she is! If I can't make her mine, life is not worth living," he decided.

    Ugrasena went home and flung himself on his bed. "Father, mother—I want to marry the acrobat we saw today. If I can't marry her, I shall starve myself to death." "My dear son, what are you saying? What can you have in common with a lowly acrobat? We will find you a girl... one worthy of you..." "It's her or none! I shall not even look at another girl," he insisted.

    The helpless treasurer and his wife sent Ugrasena's friend to the acrobat's father with a chest of gold. "My friend wishes to marry your daughter. As a marriage portion, here is all the gold you might desire." "For shame! Are you asking me to sell my daughter?" the acrobat father scolded. "If your friend is willing to travel with us, wherever we go, he may marry her."

    The treasurer and his wife were shocked to hear this. "Son, surely you will not leave us to go travelling with an acrobat!" "I will, for she means everything to me now." So Ugrasena married the acrobat, abandoned his immense wealth, and joined the travelling troupe.

    Being the only member of the troupe unskilled in acrobatics, he made himself useful in other menial ways, fetching wood and carrying water, and was content with his simple life. Soon, a son was born to them. His wife spent all her time between shows with the baby. "I'll rock you in my arms, o son of a wood-fetcher," she would sing mockingly. "Close your eyes and sleep, o son of a water-carrier. Let the jolt of the cart rock you to sleep, o son of a cart-driver."

    "Woman, are you referring to me, when you sing those songs?" Ugrasena asked angrily. "Yes... I am." "You are trifling with me. I shall leave you, and go away." "Do as you think best," she shrugged indifferently.

    "It... it's her skill as an acrobat that makes her so bold... so vain... so indifferent!" Ugrasena realized bitterly. Ugrasena went to his father-in-law. "I want to become an acrobat." "I'll teach you the skills."

    Driven by a desperate need to prove himself, Ugrasena made rapid progress in his new profession. Soon, his father-in-law announced, "I present before you a new performer—my son-in-law." When Ugrasena displayed his feats, the crowd went wild. "Look—he has turned seven somersaults! Unbelievable! His wife can turn only four!"

    Soon at Rajagriha, news spread that the acrobats were returning and, with them, the treasurer's son. "It seems Ugrasena will perform the impossible feat of turning fourteen somersaults in the air!" On the appointed day, the entire city gathered in the square to watch Ugrasena perform.

    Just as he climbed the tall pole and prepared to turn the first somersault, a murmur rippled through the crowd. "Look! The holy teacher!" Lord Buddha and his monks had entered the square. The entire crowd turned their backs to the pole, bowing to the Buddha.

    When Ugrasena came back to position at the top of the pole, he realized no one was watching him. "It... it is Buddha!" As Ugrasena gazed down at Buddha from the top of the pole, a profound change came over him. He saw the vanity and futility of his lifelong obsession with proving his worth through dangerous physical feats just to earn the fleeting respect of a vain woman and a fickle crowd.

    He smoothly slid down the pole, walked past the cheering crowd, and fell at Buddha's feet. "Most venerable teacher, please admit me into your fold," Ugrasena begged, finally finding true peace.

    The Harvest

    Once a farmer was hard at work clearing his field when Buddha came walking by. "Water my field, o cloud with your gentle rain; ripen my crops, o sun, into golden grain..." the farmer sang happily. "Ah! What a fine harvest I will reap!" "What are you doing, o farmer?" Buddha asked. "I am clearing my field, sir."

    The next day, Buddha came that way again. "What are you busy with now, o farmer?" "I am ploughing my field, sir." On every succeeding day after this, Buddha came and inquired about the farmer's work.

    Then one day, the farmer said, "Sir, I find that from the day I began to clear my field, you have been showing a great deal of interest in my work. So when my crop is harvested, I am going to share it with you. For, you are now my partner." The days passed and the farmer's crops ripened beautifully. "I shall call in the reapers tomorrow," he thought joyfully.

    But that night, terrible black clouds gathered. Thunder and lightning rent the sky, and a raging, violent storm broke out. The next morning, when the farmer hurried to his field, he collapsed in despair. His entire crop was utterly destroyed. "Oh, God—no! I am ruined! And I have promised a share to my partner!"

    The grief-stricken farmer returned home and took to his bed, refusing to eat or speak. Just then, Buddha arrived there. "Master, just a mouthful of food..." his wife begged him. "No—take it away. I will not eat," the farmer wept.

    "O farmer, I wish to speak to you," Buddha said, entering the room. The farmer slowly rose and came to sit by Buddha. "Tell me why you grieve, o farmer." "Last night's storm destroyed my crop... and I cannot keep my word to you."

    "You would not grieve thus, if you knew what your sorrow springs from. It is from attachment, o farmer," Buddha explained softly. "Attachment to your crops, to your word, and to me! From attachment springs sorrow, from attachment springs fear. He who is free from attachment is free of the burden of both sorrow and fear." The farmer bowed. "I have understood, o venerable one."

    The Golden Maiden

    In the town of Sravasti lived young Kumara. He was the son of rich parents and he had come of age. "Son, you are old enough to be married. We shall choose a good, beautiful girl for you and..." his father began. "Father, I don't want to get married," Kumara replied.

    But the old couple did not give up. Every once in a way, they put the question to him again. At last, Kumara thought, "They will not take 'no' for an answer. The only way out is to agree but make sure that they won't find the girl for me." The young man got skilled artisans to carve a golden image of absolute, matchless beauty. Then, he presented it to his parents. "Mother! Father! I will marry but... only one such as this! If you find a woman exactly as beautiful as this image, I will marry her."

    His father sighed. "We will find her. This image... will be taken... from town to town... till such a girl is found." "You'll never find her," Kumara smirked to himself.

    Months rolled by. Then at last one day, a messenger returned. "The maiden is found, sir—in the city of Sagala." "What? Can it be so?" the father gasped. The delighted old couple sent forth a messenger with rich gifts for the maiden and her parents. "Let her be brought here with all speed."

    Kumara received the news with mixed feelings. "When I got the statue made, I never imagined that such a girl could actually be found. But now that she is found, I can hardly wait to see her! They say that compared with the image... she is far, far lovelier! I cannot even imagine the extent of her beauty!"

    He impatiently awaited the arrival of the girl. He stood on his balcony for days. "Is that her carriage? No! It is not. Oh, how slowly the hours crawl by! It is night already, the tenth night since she set out. Where is she?" He ran to his parents. "Father, mother—she has still not come. What could delay her so?"

    "She... she must be on her way, son!" his mother stammered. "Mother, are you hiding something from me? Is something wrong? Have they refused the proposal?" "No, my son." "Then why hasn't she come yet? Why? Why?"

    His father sighed heavily. "We must not keep it from him any longer. Yes. He will have to be told. Son... she will never come." "But... why...?" "She is dead, son. On her way here, she fell ill and died."

    The shock was too much for Kumara. He fainted on the spot. Just then, there on the doorstep, stood Buddha. "Arise, youthful one. What is this sorrow that afflicts you?" "She was the most beautiful woman on earth," Kumara wept. "She was the woman I was to marry."

    "O Kumara, you do not grieve because a woman died," Buddha corrected him gently. "Un...?" "You grieve because the woman you desired died. From desire springs sorrow; from desire springs fear. He that is free from desire neither sorrows nor fears." Kumara prostrated himself before Buddha, as the teacher's profound calmness enveloped his own broken heart.

    Buddha and Krisha Gautami

    Once, in the town of Sravasti, a woman named Krisha Gautami was sitting desperately by her child's bed. "He has not woken up for hours. He must be very ill indeed," she wept. Her husband checked the boy's pulse. "Oh, god! No! He is dead." "Go, son, make arrangements to cremate the body," the grandmother said sadly.

    "Cremate the body? Burn my son? No!" Krisha Gautami screamed, grabbing the child. "I will find a remedy for death!" "Gautami! Wait! Come back! Don't you understand? He's dead," her husband cried as she ran out of the house.

    "Kind sir, do you know of any remedy for my son?" she begged a passerby in the street. "Poor woman! She has probably not seen a dead person before. I must help her," the man thought. "I'm afraid I don't. But Buddha can help you. Go to him."

    Full of hope, Krisha Gautami at once went to the sacred grove of Buddha. "My name is Krisha Gautami, venerable one. I was told that you could cure my son. So I have come to you." Buddha looked at the dead child and smiled gently. "Go, fetch me a few mustard seeds from a house that has never known death."

    Krisha Gautami eagerly set out on her quest. She knocked on the first door. "Mother, can you give me a few mustard seeds? I trust this house has never known death." The woman looked at her sadly. "What are you saying, good woman? We that are living are few, compared with those that have died here." "Then please take back these mustard seeds, for I have no use for them."

    Krisha Gautami went on her quest from house to house, but everywhere it was the exact same. Every single house had known death, and every dead body had been burnt to ashes. "...I lost my daughter..." "...My brother died last year..." "They have taken my old father to the cremation ground to be burnt..."

    Finally, the realization dawned on her. "So those that are dead can never be cured, after all. Ah, what a vain search was mine!" Krisha Gautami went to the forest, laid her child upon a soft carpet of fallen leaves and flowers... and then went back to Buddha.

    "Did you get the mustard seeds?" Buddha asked. "No, holy sir, I could not get them. There is not a house that has not known death." "O Krisha Gautami, it is not you alone who have lost a child to death." "I have realized that, o venerable Buddha. Admit me into your order."

    Krisha Gautami was admitted into Buddha's fold. One day, when it was her turn, she lit the oil lamp in the monastery and sat down in front of it to meditate. "Life is exactly like these flames," she realized, watching the wicks. "Some burn on; some flicker and go out." Krisha Gautami continued to meditate with her eyes fixed on the flame of the lamp... and finally attained Nirvana, the perfect state of peace.

    The Perfectionist

    There was once a carpenter who prided himself deeply on the impeccable quality of his work. "Hmm-m. Good. But not good enough. It needs improving. Now let me see..." he would mutter, sanding a piece of wood for the hundredth time.

    Just then a man walked in. "What can I do for you, sir?" "My wife has been blessed with a child. I need a cradle." "You have come to the right man. I'll make you the best cradle you have ever seen. A perfect dream," the carpenter promised. "When can I come for it?" "It should be ready in a week from now."

    A week later, the father returned. "I've come for the cradle." "Eh? The cradle? Oh! The cradle! Ah! The cradle!" the carpenter stammered. "I will be honest with you, sir. It is not yet ready." "After all, it has got to be the best you will agree. Shall I come tomorrow?" "Give me a week, sir. The cradle has to be a dream, don't forget, sir."

    Week after week, the father came, but the answer was always the same. "No, it is not yet ready, sir. I am not satisfied with my work. Whatever I do must be perfect."

    Soon the child was too old to need a cradle, so the father gave up going to the carpenter altogether. By and by the boy grew up, got married and in his turn became the proud father of a child. "Go to our old carpenter, today," the grandfather told his son. "Ask him if the cradle I had ordered for you is ready. Your son could use it."

    Soon, the young man arrived at the shop. "The cradle my father ordered for me when I was born should be ready by now. So..." "It isn't. And look, sir!" the old carpenter scowled, pointing a chisel at him. "Let me make one thing clear. I take extreme pride in my work. And I will not permit you or your father to rush me."

    The young man was about to protest but realized it would be absolutely futile to argue with the perfectionist. So he quietly left the place.
