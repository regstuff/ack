# Indraprastha Lost

Following the successful completion of the Rajasooya Yajna, the jealous Duryodhana and his cunning uncle Shakuni remained at Indraprastha for some time. As they explored the magnificent palace built by the Asura architect Maya, Duryodhana's envy only deepened. 

The palace was filled with wondrous illusions. In one hall, Duryodhana mistook a polished crystal floor for a lake and nervously tucked up his garments, evoking laughter from the onlookers. Later, he mistook a real lotus pond for a solid floor and fell straight into the water, prompting hearty laughter from Bheema, Nakula, Sahadeva, and the other Pandavas, though Yudhishthira remained respectful. Adding to his humiliation, Duryodhana banged his head against a solid wall he mistook for an open door. 

Duryodhana returned to Hastinapura burning with resentment. "The prosperity of the Pandavas has completely vitiated my mind," he told Shakuni. "I am burning with resentment. Who among the mighty will tolerate an enemy prospering?"

"The Pandavas cannot be vanquished even by the celestials," Shakuni advised shrewdly. "But, O Prince, I know a stratagem to obtain their immense riches. Yudhishthira is very fond of gambling but he is not adept at it. When invited, he never refuses to play. Invite Yudhishthira to a game of dice. I shall win their kingdom and its glittering wealth for you."

Duryodhana approached his blind father, King Dhritarashtra, looking weak and pale. When his anxious father inquired about his health, Duryodhana explained his jealousy and Shakuni's plot. Despite the wise warnings of his minister Vidura, who cautioned that gambling would bring the destruction of their clan, Dhritarashtra's blind love for his son prevailed. Believing that fate could not be changed, he ordered Vidura to invite Yudhishthira. 

Vidura reluctantly journeyed to Indraprastha. "Gambling is the root cause of misfortune," Vidura warned Yudhishthira. "I made an effort to prevent it. But still the king has sent me to invite you. Do what you consider proper."

Bound by his honor as a Kshatriya, Yudhishthira replied, "He who perceives this is not perturbed by the outcome. When invited, I do not decline. Tell me, who has the authority to wager with me?"

Yudhishthira and his brothers arrived at the gambling hall in Hastinapura. When the game commenced, Duryodhana announced that his uncle Shakuni would play and roll the dice on his behalf. 

Using foul and deceitful means, Shakuni tossed the dice and began to win every single round. Yudhishthira first wagered heaps of precious stones, gold, and pearls. He lost. He then staked his glorious chariots, war elephants, maids, slaves, soldiers, and his entire immense treasury. Each time, Shakuni cried, "Lo, I have won!"

Despite the protests of the elders, Dhritarashtra, unable to hide his joy, kept asking, "What has been won? What has been won?!"

"You have lost all your wealth," Shakuni taunted. "What will you wager now?"

Driven by a strange compulsion, Yudhishthira staked his brothers—Nakula, Sahadeva, Arjuna, and Bheema. He lost them all. 

"Myself! If I lose now, I shall serve you," Yudhishthira said desperately. He lost again. 

"You still possess your dear wife. Stake Draupadi and win yourself back," Shakuni suggested wickedly. A collective gasp of horror rose from the assembly. Sweat broke out on the bodies of Bheeshma and Drona, but Yudhishthira, trapped by the dice, wagered Draupadi. And Shakuni won. 

Duryodhana gleefully ordered his charioteer, Pratikami, to fetch Draupadi from the women's quarters. When Pratikami conveyed the message, Draupadi asked a sharp, piercing question: "Did Yudhishthira have nothing else to wager? Go back and ask that gambler whom he lost first, himself or me."

When Yudhishthira failed to answer, Duryodhana sent his brutal brother, Duhshasana, to bring her by force. Duhshasana marched into the women's quarters, roared with anger, and caught Draupadi by her unbraided hair. He dragged the weeping, menstruating queen all the way into the open assembly. 

"Fie on the descendants of Bharata!" Draupadi cried angrily, trying to cover herself with her single, half-fallen garment. "They have lost the Dharma of the Kshatriyas! Why do Bheeshma and the others tolerate this act against Dharma?"

Bheeshma hung his head in sorrow. "O chaste woman, Dharma is very subtle. It requires that a woman must obey the commands of her husband... but one who does not possess himself cannot wager what is not his. So, I am unable to decide what is proper."

Vikarna, a younger brother of Duryodhana, boldly stood up and declared the wager invalid, as Yudhishthira had already lost his own freedom before staking Draupadi. But Karna insulted Vikarna, calling him a boy who did not understand the rules of gambling. 

"Duhshasana," Karna ordered cruelly, "disrobe the Pandavas and Draupadi."

The Pandavas silently removed their upper garments. Duhshasana stepped forward and grabbed Draupadi's sari. Helpless, Draupadi closed her eyes and meditated on Lord Krishna. "O Govinda... O Creator of all, save me from this disgrace!"

A miracle occurred. As Duhshasana pulled off Draupadi's garment, another garment instantly appeared in its place. He pulled and pulled, tearing away colorful silks, but Draupadi remained fully clothed. Soon, a massive, mountainous heap of garments formed on the floor, and Duhshasana collapsed, exhausted and filled with shame. 

Watching the horrific scene, Bheema's eyes filled with blood. He took a terrifying vow. "May I not attain the heavenly abode if I do not tear Duhshasana's chest and drink his blood!"

Unmoved, Duryodhana lewdly patted his thigh, gesturing for Draupadi to come sit on it. Bheema took a second vow. "If I do not break your thigh in a great war, then may I not attain the world of my ancestors!"

Suddenly, a jackal howled in the sacrificial hall, accompanied by the braying of asses and the screeching of carrion birds. Terrified by the ominous signs, Dhritarashtra finally intervened. He rebuked Duryodhana and granted Draupadi three boons. With her first two boons, she secured the freedom of Yudhishthira, and the freedom and weapons of her other husbands. She refused a third boon, and the Pandavas departed for Indraprastha. 

However, Duryodhana and Shakuni could not let their enemies leave. They convinced Dhritarashtra to summon the Pandavas back for one final, fateful game of dice. The terms were absolute: the loser would be forced into exile in the forest for twelve years, and must spend the thirteenth year incognito. If discovered during the thirteenth year, they would have to repeat the entire twelve-year exile. 

Bound by his Kshatriya dharma, Yudhishthira played once more. And once again, Shakuni used foul means to win. 

As the Pandavas prepared to leave for the forest, dressed in humble deerskins, Duhshasana danced for joy. Bheema turned back, his voice thick with fury. "I will kill Duryodhana with my mace. Arjuna will kill Karna, and Sahadeva will kill the wily Shakuni. If our kingdom is not returned to us respectfully after thirteen years, I will surely accomplish my vow."

And so, the proud Pandavas, deprived of their wealth and kingdom, set out on their long and arduous exile into the deep forest.


# The Pandavas in the Forest

Having been defeated by the wicked sons of Dhritarashtra in the deceitful game of dice, the Pandavas walked out of Hastinapura in anger, accompanied by Draupadi. As they marched northward, fourteen of their loyal attendants and their wives followed them, along with thousands of sorrowful citizens. 

"We are distressed that you are being banished through the treachery of your cruel enemies," the citizens lamented, refusing to turn back. "We will willingly accept your way of life whatever the hardship. We are devoted to you and will make ourselves useful."

Yudhishthira, touched but firm, addressed them. "You have attributed to us qualities we do not possess. We are sure we will perish under the rule of this evil king, but we cannot take you with us. Please do not desert us. Return to your homes." Reluctantly, the citizens returned, though a few dedicated Brahmanas remained with the Pandavas. 

They reached the bank of the Ganga and decided to spend the night under a massive banyan tree. As night fell, the Brahmanas lit a sacrificial fire and entertained Yudhishthira by chanting Vedic hymns. 

The next morning, Yudhishthira was plagued with worry. "We have lost all our wealth and are going to live in the forest sustaining ourselves on fruits and roots. How can I then look after you?" he asked the Brahmanas. "Nor can I ask my brothers to acquire food for you; they are all upset over Draupadi's humiliation."

"Do not turn us away," the Brahmanas pleaded. "We will obtain our food ourselves, look after your well-being with our prayers, and entertain you with interesting tales."

Yudhishthira sought the advice of Sage Dhaumya. "O King! Surya, the Sun, is the provider of food for all creatures," Dhaumya advised. "Worship him for the means to maintain the Brahmanas."

Following the sage's instructions, Yudhishthira stood in the freezing waters of the Ganga and performed intense austerities to propitiate Surya. Pleased by his prayers, the Sun God appeared before him in a radiant chariot. 

"Your desire shall be fulfilled," Surya promised. "I shall provide you with food during all these twelve years." Surya handed Yudhishthira a glowing copper plate. "Take this copper plate. Every day, this plate will remain full with the choicest food till Panchali eats."

With the help of the Sun God's wondrous boon, Yudhishthira was able to offer food regularly to the Brahmanas. Every day, the meager food Draupadi cooked would miraculously multiply to fill the plate, allowing her to serve the Brahmanas, then her brothers-in-law, and finally her husband. Only when Draupadi herself finished her meal did the plate become empty. 

With their sustenance secured, the Pandavas proceeded to the Kamyaka forest. 

Back in Hastinapura, Dhritarashtra felt a brief pang of sadness for the Pandavas and asked Vidura for advice. Vidura spoke truthfully: "Restore to the Pandavas the kingdom originally given to them by you. Then alone can you avert a loss of reputation and dispute within the family. Otherwise, utter ruin will destroy the family of the Kurus."

"What you say is to the advantage of only the Pandavas," Dhritarashtra replied, displeased. "I cannot shun my son Duryodhana for the sake of the Pandavas! You are giving me wrong advice. You may go wherever you like."

Vidura muttered that the destruction of the family was inevitable and went away to join the Pandavas in the Kamyaka forest. When Duryodhana learned that Vidura had joined his enemies, he was delighted, thinking they were now truly rid of him. However, Dhritarashtra soon repented his harsh words and sent a messenger to call Vidura back. Vidura, forgiving his brother, returned to Hastinapura. 

Meanwhile, word of the Pandavas' exile reached their allies. Lord Krishna, King Drupada, Dhrishtadyumna, and other valiant princes arrived in the forest, cursing the sons of Dhritarashtra. 

Krishna was visibly enraged. "The earth will be drenched with the blood of this evil quartet of Duryodhana, Karna, Shakuni, and Duhshasana!" he declared. "We are ready to fight and inflict defeat on them."

Yudhishthira, committed to his vow of exile, gently pacified Krishna. Arjuna, stepping forward, recounted Krishna's glorious past incarnations to calm him. "You are the Almighty. You are the truth, the Lord of Lords and the King of the Universe. Do not grieve. I promise you this: you will become the king of kings."

Krishna's anger subsided. "I will not grieve. But you are young and appear to have been brought up in luxury. Why are you living alone in the forest?"

After Krishna and the other allies departed, the Pandavas moved to the Dvaitavana forest on the banks of the Saraswati river. 

One evening, while discussing their unfortunate state, Draupadi angrily addressed Yudhishthira. "The wicked Duryodhana has made us homeless and reduced us into inhabitants of the forest. How is it that you do not nurse any anger against him? It is contrary to the nature of a Kshatriya."

Yudhishthira calmly replied, "One who can control his anger ensures his own well-being. Forgiveness and non-cruelty are the qualities of great men. So I adopt the path of forgiveness."

Bheema, aroused to anger by Draupadi's words, supported her. "I too do not understand this inaction. Why don't we attack the Kauravas and acquire what is rightfully ours? Unless we match their might, we cannot hope to win."

"Do not underestimate the might of our enemies," Yudhishthira cautioned. "The Kauravas have Bheeshma, Drona, Kripa, Karna, and Ashwatthama on their side. They all have celestial weapons with them."

While they were conversing, the great Sage Vyasa appeared before them. Having divined their thoughts through meditation, he took Yudhishthira aside. 

"Let Arjuna have this secret knowledge named Pratismriti," Vyasa instructed. "Obtaining this, the mighty Arjuna will achieve success. With this knowledge, he can please Shiva and Indra and get divine weapons from them."

Vyasa taught the secret mantras to Yudhishthira, and Yudhishthira in turn imparted them to Arjuna, setting the stage for the greatest warrior of his age to embark upon his legendary quest for weapons.


# Arjuna's Quest for Weapons

Bidden by Yudhishthira, Arjuna prepared to leave on his quest to acquire celestial weapons. Empowered with the secret knowledge of *Pratismriti*, he burned with the desire to obtain the legendary arms from Lord Indra and Lord Shiva, knowing he would need them to slay the sons of Dhritarashtra in the inevitable war. 

He equipped himself with the Gandeeva bow, two inexhaustible quivers, formidable armor, finger-guards, and a leather arm-band. After offering oblations to the Fire God and receiving the blessings of the Brahmanas and Draupadi, he set out on his solitary journey. 

"I pray to Vasu, Rudra, Aditya, the Maruts, and all other Gods," Draupadi prayed as he left, "May they look after your well-being."

Using the yogic knowledge of *Pratismriti*, Arjuna journeyed as fast as thought itself. In a single day, he reached the holy Himalayas, the abode of the gods. Walking incessantly over the mountains and the Gandhamadana peaks, he finally arrived at Indrakeela. 

Suddenly, he heard a booming voice: "Stop!" 

Arjuna looked around and saw an ascetic with matted hair sitting under a tree. His thin, tawny-colored body was glowing with a heavenly radiance. 

"Who are you?" the ascetic asked. "Why have you come here following the Dharma of a Kshatriya, equipped with bow and arrows, sword and armour? This place is the hermitage of ascetic Brahmanas who are peace-loving and devoid of all passions. You have attained your final goal by coming here. So throw away your arms."

Thus the great sage, with a smile on his face, repeatedly asked Arjuna to discard his weapons. But Arjuna, steadfast in his purpose, was not persuaded to do so. 

Pleased and satisfied by Arjuna's unyielding determination, the Brahmana revealed his true identity. "O destroyer of enemies, I am Indra! You may ask me a boon."

Arjuna, recognizing the king of the gods, bowed with folded arms. "O Lord! I have come to acquire the knowledge of all the weapons from you. Please grant me this wish."

Indra smiled. "Now that you have reached here, what do you want with weapons? You may as well ask for the best life in the heavens."

"O King of Devas!" Arjuna replied firmly. "I do not have any greed or lust. Neither do I desire to attain divinity. Leaving my brothers behind in the forest, I have come to learn the means of taking revenge on our enemies. And if I deviate from it, it will be perpetual infamy for me."

Comforted by his son's resolve, Indra spoke in a pleasing voice. "My child! When you have pleased and beheld Shiva, the wielder of the trident, I shall give you the celestial weapons. Strive to behold Shiva. After that, you shall arrive in heaven." Saying this, Indra disappeared. 

Following his father's advice, Arjuna embarked upon an incredibly severe penance to propitiate Lord Shiva. Adorning himself in tree-bark and deer skin, and eating only dried leaves, he sat in deep meditation. In the first month, he ate only once in three days; in the second, only once in six days; and then once a fortnight. By the fourth month, he consumed nothing but air. Standing on his toes with his arms raised, he continued his intense meditation. 

His austerities were so fierce that the earth began to smoke, and the sages of the Himalayas grew alarmed. They approached Shiva and bowed to him. "O Lord of Lords! The splendid Arjuna has been performing Tapas... The entire region has become cloudy as a result of his intense meditations. We do not know what he is after, but we have been much perturbed. Pray, stop him!"

Shiva smiled reassuringly at the pure-hearted sages. "I know what he has come for. Today I will fulfill his desire. You need not worry about Arjuna. He does not desire heaven, wealth, or eternal life."

Assuming the form of a golden-skinned Kirata (hunter), accompanied by his consort Uma disguised as a huntress and surrounded by thousands of fierce women, Shiva approached the spot where Arjuna was meditating. 

Just as Shiva arrived, a massive Danava named Mooka, having assumed the form of a wild boar, rushed at Arjuna with the intent to kill him. Seeing the beast, Arjuna quickly picked up his Gandeeva, mounted a snake-like arrow, and prepared to strike. 

"Stop!" shouted Shiva, in the guise of the hunter. "O ascetic! I'm the one who first spotted this boar, luminous like Indrakeel. I am going to send you to the abode of death for trying to steal my prey."

Without paying heed, Arjuna loosed his arrow at the boar. At the exact same moment, the hunter dispatched his own arrow. Both shafts hit the mountain-like Mooka simultaneously with a deafening crack. The boar immediately died, reverting to its true, monstrous Danava form. 

Arjuna turned to the hunter with a furious smile. "Who are you, roaming with women in this deadly forest? Are you not afraid? And why did you shoot the beast after I had shot it? You have acted against the established rules of hunting, and you deserve to die at my hands."

The hunter laughed loudly. "You are young and appear to have been brought up in luxury. Why are you living alone in the forest? This creature came to kill me, and in turn, got himself killed by me. You imbecile! You are trying to glorify yourself through the achievement of others. So I will not spare your life. Shoot as you please."

Angered, Arjuna unleashed a rain of his sharpest arrows at the hunter. The hunter, pleased at heart, easily absorbed them all. 

"Try again!" the hunter taunted. "Try again! The sharpest of your arrows."

Lord Shiva easily absorbed the relentless shower of arrows, remaining unhurt and unmoved like a mountain. Awed by the prowess of his adversary, Arjuna muttered in appreciation, "Bravo! Bravo! Who could he be? Could he be Rudra himself, or some other god, or a Yaksha? I have heard that gods often visit this mountain. Shiva alone, and none other, can contain my shower of arrows."

Determined to win, Arjuna fiercely attacked the hunter with his bow itself, striking him with thunderous blows. But the hunter casually snatched away Arjuna's celestial bow. Arjuna then drew his sword and struck the hunter on his head, but the mighty blade shattered into pieces against the hunter's skull. 

Resorting to physical combat, Arjuna hurled trees and massive rocks, but the hunter absorbed them all. Finally, the two formidable warriors wrestled bare-handed, trading crushing blows. But the mighty Partha was no match for the Lord of the Universe. Smothered in a powerful hug by Shiva, Arjuna fell to the ground like a lifeless lump. 

After regaining consciousness, his body smeared with blood and his mind filled with despair, Arjuna hastily molded a mud-image of a Shiva-Linga and worshipped it, placing a beautiful garland of flowers upon it. 

When he looked up, he gasped in amazement. The garland he had placed on the Linga now adorned the head of the hunter! 

"Ah! It is Lord Shiva himself," Arjuna realized. Overwhelmed with devotion, he fell at the feet of the Lord. "I am blessed."

Shiva's voice rolled like majestic thunder. "I am pleased with your gallant effort. There is no Kshatriya equal to you in valour. You have proved today that you are equal to me in power. I grant you divine vision."

Suddenly, Arjuna could see the true, radiant form of Mahadeva and Uma. He knelt before them, his hands folded in prayer. 

"O Lord!" Arjuna begged. "I have come to acquire the knowledge of all the weapons from you. Please grant me this wish."

"O Pandava, I am giving you my dearest weapon, Pashupata," Shiva said, handing him the terrible, world-destroying missile. "But do not use it on an impulse. If used on anyone inferior in strength, the weapon will destroy the entire universe." 

Shiva then imparted the deadly knowledge of the weapon's application and retrieval. As soon as Arjuna took possession of the Pashupata, the earth, oceans, and villages trembled, and the sky vibrated with the sound of thousands of conch-shells and drums. 

"O Monarch! As Shiva left, Arjuna remained overwhelmed," Yudhishthira later learned. 

Following Shiva's departure, the Lokapalas—the guardians of the universe—descended from the heavens on radiant chariots to present Arjuna with their own divine weapons. 

First came Varuna, the Lord of the Waters, glowing with a gem-like complexion. "I am giving you Varunapasha, this inescapable noose of mine," he said. "I had enchained thousands of Danavas with this during our battle with the Asura Taraka."

Next came Kubera, the God of Wealth, on a chariot as golden as the sun. "Take this powerful and burning Antardhana, the all-effacing destroyer of enemies," Kubera declared. "With this, you will be able to destroy the entire army of Duryodhana."

Then came Yama, the God of Death, in his flying Vimana. "O Arjuna! Look at us. We, the rulers of different worlds, are here. Take this irrepressible weapon, the Danda."

Finally, Indra arrived on his magnificent white elephant, Airavata. He presented Arjuna with a multitude of celestial weapons. "O mighty son of Kunti," Indra said proudly. "You have now attained divinity and must proceed to Swargaloka, the abode of the Devas."

Matali, Indra's celestial charioteer, arrived in a divine chariot to carry Arjuna away. Awestruck by the sight of all the Lokapalas gathered to bless him, Arjuna devotedly worshipped them with fruits and water. 

Fully armed with the mightiest weapons in the universe, Arjuna stepped into the golden chariot, ready to ascend to the heavens and prepare for the great war that would decide the fate of the world.
