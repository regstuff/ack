# Ganesha and the Moon and Other Stories

## Ganesha and the Moon
Once on his birthday, Ganesha was invited to Chandralok, the abode of the moon, for a feast. "Everything is splendid but I like these modaks best," Ganesha said, eating all the sweet dumplings filled with coconut and jaggery. 

Very soon, he had eaten too much. "I feel so uncomfortable. I need to get some fresh air. I'll go for a ride on my mouse," he decided. 
It was a beautiful night. The moon shone so brightly, it was almost like day. Suddenly, a snake crossed their path with a loud hiss. The mouse squeaked in terror and jumped, throwing Ganesha off his back. 

The moon, watching from the sky, burst out laughing. "Ha ha ha! You look so funny! What a fall that was!"
Ganesha was very angry. But first, he put all the spilled modaks back into his stomach and used the snake as a belt to tie his stomach up. 
The moon just would not stop laughing. Infuriated, Ganesha broke off a piece of his tusk and hurled it furiously, slashing the moon's luminous face. 
"O foolish moon! Your unblemished appearance has made you vain! I command you to vanish from the universe!" Ganesha cursed. 

The moon disappeared and there was chaos in heaven and on earth. "I cannot see anything," people cried. "It is so dark!"
Realising his mistake, the moon came to Ganesha with the other gods to apologize. "I have learned my lesson. I will never mock you again," the moon pleaded. 
"Take back your curse, O Lord! The world is plunged in darkness," the gods requested. 

The ever-forgiving Ganesha thought for a while. "You were wrong to laugh at my discomfort. I cannot take back my curse but I can soften it. From now on, you will not glow brightly every night. You will wax and wane from one bright fortnight to one dark fortnight alternately. You will be seen as a full moon, in all your splendour, only once a month. Also, you will be invisible for one night, every month."
The moon bowed before Ganesha and went back to his place in the sky.

## Ganesha and Ravana
Ravana, the demon king of Lanka, observed severe penance to please Shiva. Pleased with his austerities, Shiva appeared before him. "Ask for a boon, Ravana. I will grant you anything you wish for."
"Lord, I want the Atmalinga," Ravana demanded. 

Shiva was dismayed. The Atmalinga had the power to make anyone invincible. But having promised, he gave it to Ravana with a condition. "You must walk all the way to Lanka holding the Atmalinga in your hands. Don't put the Atmalinga down. If you do, even for a moment, it will stay rooted to that spot for ever."
Ravana was jubilant and started his journey back home. 

The gods watched, aghast. "Ravana is our enemy! With the Atmalinga, his power will be greater than ever!" They sought Lord Ganesha's help. 
Ganesha followed Ravana. When Ravana reached the west coast, Ganesha invoked the power of Lord Vishnu to temporarily block out the sun. 
"Strange how the sun set so suddenly!" Ravana was puzzled. "It is time for me to say my evening prayers." 

Since Ravana was a stickler for rituals, he found himself in a dilemma. "I cannot put the linga down and I cannot pray with it in my hands." 
Just then, he saw a young brahmin boy. "Here, boy, hold this for me," Ravana offered the Atmalinga. 
"No, I can't! It's too heavy for me," the boy (who was actually Ganesha in disguise) refused. 
"I will return soon. Please hold it till then," Ravana begged. 
"Well... all right," the boy agreed reluctantly. 

The wily Ganesha waited until Ravana was in the middle of his prayers. Then he shouted, "This is too heavy for me! I am going to put it down!"
Ravana was alarmed. "No, don't! I'll come as soon as I finish!"
But Ganesha shouted again and placed the Atmalinga on the ground. 
Ravana rushed back, but it was too late. Suddenly, the sun was back in the sky as Vishnu removed the illusion. Ravana tried to lift the linga, but it wouldn't budge. "I have been tricked!" 

An enraged Ravana chased the boy and hit him thrice on his head with his fists. Then Ganesha assumed his true form. Realising what he had done, Ravana bowed before him. 
The place where Ganesha placed the Atmalinga is in Gokarna, Karnataka. The great force exerted by Ravana on the linga changed its shape to resemble the ear of a cow (Go-karna).

## Ganesha Releases Kaveri
At the wedding of Shiva and Parvati at Mount Kailash, the ground started to sink under the weight of all the gathered gods. Shiva summoned Sage Agastya. "If you travel south to the Podhigai hills, you will act as a counter-balance."
Shiva poured some sacred water from his matted locks into Agastya's water pot. "The water pot now contains the sacred river, Kaveri. Find a suitable spot in the mountains to set it down. The river will then flow out in whichever direction you point."

Time passed and Agastya wandered in the southern lands. In the meantime, Indra, king of the gods, was attacked by the demon Surapadman. Indra fled to earth, created a garden in Sirgazhi, and decided to propitiate Shiva with flowers from his garden to ask for help. 
But a terrible drought struck, and the flowers wilted. Indra pleaded with Varuna, the rain god, but Varuna was too terrified of Surapadman to help. 
Narada advised Indra, "Agastya has the river Kaveri. Pray to Lord Ganesha. He will find a way to help you."

Ganesha answered Indra's prayers. Taking the form of a crow, he spotted Agastya meditating. The crow flew down and perched on Agastya's water pot. Agastya chased the crow away, but as it flew off, it knocked the pot over. The river Kaveri flowed out! 
Suddenly, the crow took the form of a small boy. Agastya caught him, about to rap him on the head, when the boy changed into Lord Ganesha. 
"O Vighneshwara, forgive me!" Agastya repented, striking his own forehead with his knuckles. 
"Stop, Agastya... you are truly repentant. This is a sign of true wisdom," Ganesha smiled. The drought ended, Indra's garden flourished, and the river Kaveri began its eternal flow across South India.

## Ganesha and Gana
King Abhishek and Queen Gunavati performed severe penances on a sage's advice and were blessed with a son they named Gana. The young prince grew up to be a great devotee of Shiva. Shiva granted him boons that made Gana powerful but arrogant. 

One day, while hunting, Gana visited the ashram of Sage Kapila. The sage hosted a grand feast using the Chintamani gem, given to him by Indra, which could grant any desire. 
Gana's selfish mind lusted after the gem. "I must have this gem," he demanded. When Kapila refused, Gana flew into a rage and forcefully took it away. 

An anguished Sage Kapila prayed to Ganesha. Ganesha appeared in Gana's dream and warned him, but Gana arrogantly gathered his army and attacked Sage Kapila's ashram. A fierce battle ensued. Ganesha killed Gana and retrieved the gem, earning the name Gananayaka ('one who defeated Gana'). 

When Ganesha returned the gem to Kapila, the sage refused it. "This gem has brought nothing but misery. Will you keep it?" 
Ganesha agreed. Sage Kapila then installed an idol of Ganesha. "This form of you, O benevolent Lord, shall be called Chintamani Vinayaka." Even today, devotees flock to the Chintamani Vinayaka temple at Theur (near Pune) to get rid of their worries ('chinta').

## The Legend of Shami and Mandar
Shami, the beautiful daughter of Sage Aurava, was married as a child to Mandar, son of Rishi Dhaumya. Years later, when it was time for Shami to go to her husband's home, they passed by the ashram of Sage Bhrushundi. 

Bhrushundi was a former thief who had repented and worshipped Ganesha with such single-minded devotion that Ganesha bestowed a singular honour on him - an elephant trunk growing from his forehead! 
When Shami and Mandar saw him meditating, they burst into uncontrollable laughter. "Look at that! A man with an elephant trunk! Ha ha ha!"

Bhrushundi's eyes blazed with anger. "Who dares laugh at me? You will become trees! Even animals will turn away from you! No birds shall eat the leaves of the Mandar tree or rest on the thorns of the Shami tree."
Instantly, Mandar turned into an Indian Coral Tree and Shami into an Indian Mesquite Tree. 

When their fathers found out, they were heartbroken and performed terrible austerities to appeal to Ganesha. 
"I cannot bring your children back to life," Ganesha told them, as he could not completely undo his great devotee Bhrushundi's curse. "But they will be honoured in the three worlds. No worship of me or Shiva will be complete without Shami leaves being placed on the altar. Shiva will be pleased if worshipped with the flowers of the Mandar tree. The 'Panchanga' (five parts) of the Shami tree will have medicinal value."

Sage Aurava, still inconsolable with grief, became the fire within the trunk of the Shami tree. To this day, when sacrificial fires are lit, priests rub pieces of Shami wood together to bring out the hidden fire. True to Ganesha's words, the Shami and Mandar trees remain forever auspicious.
