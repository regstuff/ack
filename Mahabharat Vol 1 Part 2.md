# The Pandavas at Hastinapura

Following the tragic deaths of King Pandu and Queen Madri in the Himalayan forests, the sages of Shatashringa escorted the grieving Queen Kunti and the five young Pandava princes back to the royal capital of Hastinapura. 

The entire city turned out to welcome them. Bheeshma, the blind King Dhritarashtra, and the wise Vidura received the children with deep affection and sorrow. Following the majestic state funeral of Pandu and Madri, the five Pandava brothers—Yudhishthira, Bheema, Arjuna, Nakula, and Sahadeva—began their new life in the royal palace alongside their hundred cousins, the Kauravas (the sons of Dhritarashtra).

The princes grew up together, playing and studying in the palace. However, the immense, god-given strength of the second Pandava, Bheema, quickly became a source of intense friction. Whether in running, wrestling, or swimming, Bheema effortlessly overpowered all hundred Kauravas. He would playfully but roughly pull them down from trees, outwrestle them in the dirt, and hold them underwater during swimming games until they gasped for air. 

While Bheema’s actions were born of childish exuberance rather than malice, they deeply humiliated the eldest Kaurava, Duryodhana. Consumed by toxic jealousy and hatred, Duryodhana decided that Bheema had to be eliminated. 

"I shall throw him into the Ganga when he is asleep," Duryodhana plotted darkly. "I shall then imprison Yudhishthira and Arjuna, and reign as the undisputed king!"

To execute his wicked plan, Duryodhana constructed a magnificent water-sports camp on the banks of the river Ganga at Pramanakoti. He invited the Pandavas for a grand picnic and feast. 

During the feast, Duryodhana served Bheema a plate of choice delicacies that had been heavily laced with a potent, lethal poison. Unsuspecting, Bheema ate the food heartily. After hours of exhausting water sports, the poison took effect, and Bheema collapsed into a deep, unnaturally heavy slumber on the riverbank. 

Seizing the opportunity, Duryodhana bound the unconscious Bheema tightly with strong river reeds and ruthlessly rolled him into the deep, swirling waters of the Ganga. 

Bheema sank like a stone. Down, down he went, passing through the mystical waters until he crashed into the subterranean realm of the Nagas (serpents). The fierce, venomous Nagas immediately swarmed the intruder, biting him furiously with their fangs. 

Miraculously, instead of killing him, the highly toxic snake venom acted as a perfect antidote to Duryodhana's poison! Bheema instantly snapped out of his coma. Bursting his bonds with a mighty roar, he grabbed a snake by the tail and began smashing the other serpents against the ground. 

The surviving Nagas fled in terror to their king, Vasuki. Accompanied by his courtiers, Vasuki investigated the commotion. Among his retinue was an ancient Naga named Aryaka, who recognized the boy. 
"This is the son of Kunti, my own great-granddaughter!" Aryaka exclaimed joyfully. 

Delighted, King Vasuki offered to grant Bheema any wealth or gems he desired. But Aryaka had a better idea. "Let this prince imbibe the essence in our possession," he suggested. "Each draught confers the strength of a thousand elephants!"
King Vasuki agreed. Bheema sat facing the east and drank eight massive bowls of the divine nectar in one sitting. Exhausted but empowered, he was laid on a special bed to digest the nectar, falling into a deep sleep that lasted for eight days. 

Meanwhile, back in Hastinapura, Kunti was frantic with worry when the other princes returned without Bheema. She feared Duryodhana had murdered him. The wise Vidura comforted her, assuring her that prophecies predicted a long life for all her sons. 

On the eighth day, Bheema awoke, feeling the astronomical power of the nectar surging through his veins. The Nagas blessed him, bathed him, adorned him with divine garments, and safely transported him back to the surface of the Ganga. 

Bheema rushed back to the palace, where he was joyously embraced by his mother and brothers. When Bheema recounted Duryodhana's treachery and his miraculous survival in the Naga kingdom, the righteous Yudhishthira grew grave. 
"Let it be confined to ourselves," Yudhishthira advised wisely. "If Duryodhana knows you survived his poison, he will only plot something worse. We must be vigilant."

From that day on, the Pandavas remained fiercely protective of one another. Shortly after, the Kuru elders placed all the princes—the Pandavas and the Kauravas—under the strict tutelage of the great master Kripacharya, to formally begin their martial education.


# Enter Drona

The great sage Bharadwaja had a son named Drona, who was born miraculously in a wooden vessel. Drona grew up studying the scriptures and the science of weapons alongside Prince Drupada of Panchala. The two boys became inseparable friends, and in a moment of youthful affection, Drupada made a solemn promise: "When I succeed my father to the throne, my wealth shall be yours as well."

Years passed. Drupada became the King of Panchala, while Drona married Kripi, the sister of Kripacharya, and lived in abject poverty. When his young son Ashwatthama cried for milk, Drona's poverty was so severe that other children mocked the boy by giving him water mixed with flour. Deeply humiliated, Drona decided to seek wealth. 

Hearing that the great warrior-sage Parashurama was giving away his wealth to Brahmanas, Drona traveled to the Mahendra mountains. But he arrived too late. "I have already donated all my gold and land," Parashurama told him. "Now left are my weapons and my body."
"O Sage, give me the weapons and the secrets of wielding them," Drona requested promptly. Parashurama agreed, bestowing upon Drona all his divine, fiery missiles and making him a supreme master of warfare. 

Exulting in his newly acquired martial wealth, Drona went to Panchala, confident that his childhood friend Drupada would welcome him. "Know me for your friend," Drona smiled as he stood before the king. 

But King Drupada, intoxicated by wealth and power, scowled. "O Brahmana, your intelligence seems crude and stunted! Friendship never endures forever. A king can never have a beggar for his friend. It only exists between equals!"
Furious and deeply insulted, Drona stormed out of Panchala, silently vowing to avenge this humiliation. 

Drona traveled to Hastinapura, where he lived unannounced in the house of his brother-in-law, Kripacharya. One day, the Kuru princes—both Pandavas and Kauravas—were playing outside the city when their wooden ball (vita) fell into a deep, dry well. They tried desperately to retrieve it but failed. 

Spotting a dark-complexioned, ascetic-looking man sitting nearby, they approached him. Drona smiled and said, "Fie on your Kshatriya valour, and fie on your archery! I shall recover the vita." 
Taking a handful of long reed grass, Drona threw one reed like a dart into the ball, piercing it. He then pierced the first reed with a second, the second with a third, creating a long chain of reeds, and effortlessly pulled the ball out. He also retrieved a ring that Yudhishthira had dropped into the well using a perfectly aimed arrow. 

Astounded, the princes narrated the miracle to their grandfather, Bheeshma. Realizing instantly that this master archer was none other than Drona, Bheeshma welcomed him to the palace with immense respect and appointed him as the supreme preceptor to train all the Kuru princes. 
Drona accepted, but made the princes promise him a specific *Guru Dakshina* (fee). Only Arjuna stepped forward and promised to accomplish whatever the Guru demanded. 

Under Drona's expert tutelage, the princes quickly mastered various weapons. However, Arjuna’s dedication was unparalleled. Once, the wind blew out Arjuna's lamp while he was eating dinner, but he noticed his hand flawlessly found his mouth in the dark. Realizing the power of muscle memory, Arjuna began practicing his archery in the dead of night. Hearing the twang of Arjuna's bowstring in the darkness, Drona was deeply moved by his pupil's devotion and promised to make him the greatest archer in the world. 

One day, a tribal prince named Ekalavya, son of the Nishada king, came to Drona seeking instruction. Bound by the strict rules of his royal employment, Drona refused to teach him. Undeterred, Ekalavya went deep into the forest, built a clay idol of Drona, worshipped it as his Guru, and practiced with such intense devotion that he soon excelled all others in archery. 

Sometime later, the Kuru princes were hunting in the very same forest. Their hunting dog strayed away and encountered Ekalavya. As the dog barked loudly at the dark-skinned youth, Ekalavya swiftly fired seven arrows into the dog’s mouth in quick succession, silencing the animal without killing it. 

When the dog ran back to the princes with its mouth pinned open by arrows, they were astonished by this incredible display of marksmanship. They soon found Ekalavya, who proudly declared himself a pupil of Drona. 

Arjuna, feeling deeply threatened and betrayed, confronted Drona. "You promised to make me the foremost of your disciples! How is this Nishada superior to me?"
Troubled, Drona accompanied Arjuna to the forest. Upon seeing Drona, Ekalavya prostrated himself respectfully.
"If you are really my pupil, give me my Guru Dakshina," Drona commanded softly. "Give me the thumb of your right hand."

Without a moment of hesitation or a flicker of regret, Ekalavya drew his blade, severed his right thumb, and cheerfully laid it at Drona's feet. Though he continued to shoot with his remaining fingers, his legendary agility was gone forever, securing Arjuna's position as the greatest archer.

As the final test of their training approached, Drona placed an artificial wooden bird high atop a tree and gathered his disciples. He called Yudhishthira first. "Take aim. Now, what do you see?"
"I see you, the tree, my brothers, and the bird," Yudhishthira answered. 
"Stand back, you will not hit the target," Drona sighed. He asked Duryodhana and the others the same question, and all gave similar answers, earning disqualification. 

Finally, Drona called Arjuna. "What do you see?"
"I see only the head of the bird. I see neither the tree nor you," Arjuna replied with unwavering focus. 
"Shoot!" Drona commanded. The arrow flew true, severing the wooden bird's head cleanly. 

Overjoyed by Arjuna's unmatched concentration, Drona embraced him and bestowed upon him the supreme, irresistible *Brahmashira* weapon, cementing Arjuna's destiny as a warrior without equal.


# Enter Karna

When Drona was satisfied that the princes of Hastinapura had mastered the martial arts, he approached the blind King Dhritarashtra and the royal elders, suggesting a grand public tournament to showcase their phenomenal skills. The king happily agreed, and a magnificent arena was constructed, complete with opulent pavilions for the royalty and vast seating for the citizens. 

On the day of the tournament, the entire city gathered in an atmosphere of festive excitement. The princes entered the arena, showcasing breathtaking feats of weaponry. They performed dazzling maneuvers in archery, chariot driving, and horseback riding. However, when the time came for the mace fighting display, the deep-seated rivalry between Bheema and Duryodhana erupted into genuine violence. The two princes clashed furiously, fighting like enraged elephants, until Drona was forced to send his son Ashwatthama to separate them before blood was spilled.

After order was restored, Drona called his favorite pupil to the center of the arena. "Here comes Arjuna, who is dearer to me than my own son!" Drona announced proudly. 

Arjuna, clad in golden armor that shimmered in the evening sun, stepped forward. He put on a spectacular, almost magical display of archery. With the *Agneya* missile, he created a raging fire; with the *Varuna* missile, he extinguished it with a massive column of water. He conjured howling winds, heavy clouds, and soaring mountains from thin air, leaving the vast assembly completely spellbound. 

Just as the sun began to dip and the tournament seemed over, a deafening sound echoed from the gates, striking the crowd like a thunderbolt. The gates swung open, and a majestic, towering warrior strode into the arena like a mountain on the move. He was born with divine armor grafted to his chest and golden earrings glowing by his face. It was Karna.

Striding directly toward Drona and the stunned Kuru princes, Karna bowed with scant respect. "O Arjuna!" his voice boomed like thunder across the silent arena. "Do not exult over your feats. Whatever you have done today, I shall perform each one of them with a fine excess!"

The crowd gasped. Duryodhana, watching from the royal pavilion, was instantly thrilled at the prospect of a rival who could finally match Arjuna.

True to his word, Karna effortlessly replicated every single complex and magical feat Arjuna had just performed. The spectators erupted into wild cheers. Delighted, Duryodhana rushed down from the pavilion, embraced the strange warrior, and said, "Welcome, O mighty warrior! It is my good fortune that you are with me. I am at your command, and bring down your foot on your enemies!"

Feeling intensely disgraced by this upstart intruder, Arjuna stepped forward, his eyes blazing. "O Karna, slain by me in combat, you shall gain the realm reserved for the braggart!" he threatened, challenging Karna to a duel to the death. 

As the two warriors readied their bows, Kripacharya—who knew the strict rules of duels—intervened. 
"This is Arjuna, a prince of the Kuru race," Kripacharya announced. "You must tell us of your royal line, your father, and your mother. Princes never fight with men of inferior descent."

Hearing these words, Karna’s face fell, wilting like a lotus under pounding rain. He had been raised by Adhiratha, a humble charioteer, and had no royal title to claim. 

Seeing Karna’s humiliation, Duryodhana immediately stepped forward. "It is averred that men of noble descent, men of valour, and men who command armies can aspire to kingship," he declared boldly. "If Arjuna will not fight one who is not a king, I shall crown Karna the King of Anga right now!"

Right there in the arena, with ceremonial grains, flowers, and chants, Duryodhana had Karna installed and crowned as the King of the province of Anga. 
Overwhelmed by this incredible gesture, Karna turned to Duryodhana with tears in his eyes. "What matching gift can I give you in return for the kingdom donated to me?"
Duryodhana smiled broadly and embraced him. "I look forward to your staunch friendship."

Just then, an old man leaning heavily on a staff hobbled into the arena. It was Adhiratha, the charioteer who had raised Karna. Upon seeing his foster-father, the newly crowned King of Anga immediately lowered his bow and respectfully bowed his head, which was still wet with the anointing waters of the coronation.

Watching this tender reunion, Bheema burst into mocking laughter. "You, O son of a charioteer, do not deserve death at the hands of Arjuna! Go and take the whip in keeping with your station!"

Duryodhana furiously leapt to Karna’s defense, angrily roaring at Bheema like a rutting elephant. "Such words do not become you, Bheema! Might is the prime virtue of a Kshatriya. The origin of heroes and rivers remains mysterious!"

Before the situation could escalate into a full-scale battle, the sun finally set. The tournament was officially over. Duryodhana, taking Karna's hand, led him away under the glow of countless lamps, profoundly pleased to have found a warrior capable of challenging Arjuna. Meanwhile, the Pandavas retired, uneasy with the sudden, powerful threat that had entered their lives.

Shortly after the tournament, Drona summoned his pupils to demand his *Guru Dakshina*. "Acceding to my demand," Drona instructed, "seize King Drupada of Panchala in battle and bring him to me."

The pugnacious Kaurava princes, eager to prove their strength, marched out first. However, the mighty Drupada met them on the battlefield like a whirlwind of fire. Firing sharp arrows with terrifying speed, Drupada easily scattered the Kaurava forces, sending them wailing back to the Pandavas.

Seeing the Kauravas defeated, Arjuna turned to his brothers. "We shall move in now that they have expended their energy," he said. The Pandavas launched a fierce, devastating attack. While Yudhishthira, Nakula, and Sahadeva protected his flanks, Bheema plunged into the Panchala ranks like a leviathan, smashing elephants and chariots with his heavy mace. 

Arjuna, his chariot thundering through the chaos, covered King Drupada with a relentless shower of arrows. Finally, leaping gracefully from his own chariot onto Drupada's, Arjuna swiftly disarmed the king and took him captive, sparing his life. 

When Arjuna presented the bound and humiliated King Drupada to Drona, the teacher smiled slightly at his former friend. 
"I have laid desolate your kingdom and capital," Drona said, remembering Drupada's arrogant words from years ago. "But your life is at my mercy. Would you revive an old friendship?"

Drona, true to his word, proved to be forgiving. "I am now giving half your kingdom back to you as a gift," he offered. "Since you said a king could only be friends with another king, you shall rule the southern half, and I shall rule the northern half."

The crushed King Drupada accepted the terms, but the humiliation burned deeply in his soul. He retreated to the southern province of Kampilya, brooding over his defeat, obsessively hoping to obtain a mighty son who could one day destroy Drona and avenge his shattered pride.
