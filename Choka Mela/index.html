    Chokha Mela: The Boy Who Heard God

    One evening, towards the close of the thirteenth century, a few Mahar boys were playfully playing Hututu outside their huts at Mangalvedhe in Solapur district... ...When the slow, steady clang of temple bells rang in the twilight hour. Clang! Clang! Clang!

    As the clang of the bells became louder, clearer, and more insistent, one of the boys suddenly stood still and listened. 'Come, Chokha, come,' they seemed to call to him. The bells had clanged every day at this holy hour, but never had they spoken to him so clearly and with such absolute insistence. Come Chokha! Come! Chokha! Come! "I'm coming! I'm coming!" Chokha answered.

    "Chokha! Where are you going?" his friend yelled. "To the temple! Into the temple!" "You can't! You mustn't! You are a Mahar, an untouchable! Come back, Chokha, come back..." his friends shouted in terror.

    But Chokha was completely possessed by the call of the bells. He ran on. At the temple, the evening arati was about to begin. The tempo of the clanging bells quickened, and their volume rose to a crescendo. Something stirred in the very depths of the little boy's soul. He began to push his way through the crowds. Absorbed in their prayers, the lesser ones hardly noticed him and mechanically made way.

    When he reached the sanctum sanctorum, the undisputed preserve of the highest caste and class... he was suddenly recognised. "Chhi! Chhi! It's Chokha Mela, that son of a Mahar!" the priest gasped. "How dare you come here! And at this auspicious hour! Get out of here, you dirty fellow!" "I - I am not dirty," Chokha pleaded. "I bathe every day and... and my clothes are washed every day. Look! They are clean, I am clean!" "Not another word from you, you insolent wretch!" the head priest yelled. "Throw him out! The fellow has already touched you," another priest yelled. "Yes! Yes, I'll do that. I'll go home, bathe and come back for the prasad," the head priest sneered. "Make way! Make way! It's a Mahar!" "How dare you push me!" Chokha cried as they shoved him. "Let me see the Lord! Who are you to..." "One word more and I'll beat the life out of you," the priest threatened him.

    Later, when the arati was over and people were making their way homeward, they saw a little boy huddled up by the wayside. His body heaved as if he were sobbing. "Who is that boy? Why is he sobbing?" "That boy? Oh, don't worry yourself over his tears. He's only a Mahar. And an impudent one at that!"

    As the last of them trickled away, the boy stood up. Indignation had given way to deep bewilderment. "Why did they call me dirty? I bathe every day and my clothes are as clean as theirs. I even told them in case they didn't know. Then why did they..." Chokha continued to respond to the call of the bells, but with the passage of years his awareness of his social status grew. "I am a Mahar, an untouchable," he sighed by the river. "Scrub my body as I might, I cannot wash this fact away. They will never let me enter this temple. Then why do I yearn to get a glimpse of you in all your glory? Hah! So what? I'll go to other temples. I'll leave home and see you in some temple outside this village." And Chokha left on his quest.

    Outside the first temple he went to, a priest stopped him. "Hey! Aren't you a Mahar?" "Please let me pass. I too want to go in and see the Lord," Chokha begged. "Er... yes... but I have..." "You scoundrel! How dare you talk of passing beyond the main gate!" "What's the matter?" another priest asked. "That lout of a Mahar was trying to slip past the main gate into the temple!" "Really? How did you find him out?" "The moment he spoke. His vulgar speech - his words, his accent - gave him away." "Get out before I have you beaten up!" they shouted.

    "Never mind," Chokha thought. "I will seek you in another village, in another temple." But everywhere it was the same. He was not allowed entry into any temple.

    One day, overcome by despair and hunger, he sat under a tree in a village. A trader noticed him. "You seem to be a stranger in these parts, my good man," the trader said. "I am. I come from Mangalvedhe." "Oh! Have you had anything to eat? Obviously not. You look weak and tired. You seem too weak even to speak. Come. Let me help you."

    Chokha was stunned. He had not known such kindness before. He could not say a word. He could not move. The kind trader took Chokha to his home. "I'll go in and tell my wife to make some warm gruel for you." "Who is that man? What is his caste?" his wife asked. "I don't know. I saw that he was hungry and weak, so I brought him home. Now be a good woman and make some gruel for him." "What about you? Will you eat right away?" "What! Without knowing his caste? What if he is a Shudra? Or worse still, an untouchable?" his wife exclaimed in shock. "What does it matter?" "If it doesn't, why don't the Brahmans let us eat with them?" she argued fiercely. "No. I'll not eat with him." "No! I'll make the gruel but he will eat it out there!" she declared. "And you will eat in here!" For the sake of domestic peace, the trader gave in.

    Later when he came out with the gruel, the trader gasped. "Oh, God! He's gone!" Chokha had overheard the argument and returned to his village a broken man. The temple bells no longer spoke to him. "One by one the blows fall," he wept. "One by one I face them. Yet there is no end to them. My body is impure. My character is despicable. My thoughts vile. My speech vulgar." "He will eat it out there!" the wife's voice echoed in his mind. "Little wonder I am humiliated by everyone..."

    Then one day, a group of Varkaris passed by his village singing the glory of Lord Vithoba and Pandharpur. "Vithala... Vithala... Vithala..." they chanted. The fervor of their song and the intensity of their devotion cast a spell on him. As one in a trance, he followed them. After days of travelling they reached Pandharpur. "Vithala! Vithala! Vithala! Vithala! Vithala!" the pilgrims cried out.

    "Ah! The place resounds with the Lord's name!" Chokha smiled. He bathed and washed his clothes in the river, circumambulated the temple from the outside, and prostrated himself at the main gate. "Strange is the power of this place! I have seen many images, heard many stories of the Lord, but never have I known such peace," he realized. He wandered back to the bank of the river. "Vithala! Vithala! Vithala! Vithala! Vithala!" he chanted joyfully. "Why, this place is a veritable treasury of the Lord's name! Now I see the path of salvation for me. I will follow it, come what may."

    Towards evening the temple bells began to ring. Clang! "Vithala!" Chokha smiled. Chokha walked up to the main gate of the temple. As the bells rang faster and louder, he closed his eyes in pure devotion. Only long after the arati was over did he open them. "I am grateful to you that I can stand at this gate and look towards you, O Lord," he prayed. "And well you should be, you lowly Mahar," a temple priest scoffed from the gate. Strangely, the cruel words did not hurt Chokha.

    "The sun does not forget the lotus raised in filth; nor the moon that sheds its light over the earth, the simple chakora bird," Chokha replied calmly. "Would then the Lord forget even the lowly Mahar?" "How dare you twist the images of learned poets, you crooked Mahar!" the priest yelled. "The sugar-cane is crooked but its juice is sweet," Chokha answered. "Chokha may be crooked but his faith is not."

    For some reason, the simple words stung the priest. "You lowly, illiterate rogue! How dare you talk to me, a priest, about the Lord, about faith!" "If your faith is so great and if the Lord had even an iota of love for you... he would have taken you into the temple, wouldn't he?" Chokha smiled softly. Having no answer to his own taunt, the priest stood silently as Chokha turned and slowly walked away. The devotees were chanting loudly, but he heard them not. "I am a lowly illiterate fool. I have not read the Vedas or the Puranas!" the priest fumed internally. He tried to chant the Lord's name, but repeated Chokha's words instead. "If he had an iota of love for you he would have taken you into his temple, wouldn't he?"

    "Why are you silent? Why don't you answer that question?" the priest mentally challenged his God. "Whatever I try is a waste. I only suffer in return. Why do I run after you?" Meanwhile, Chokha sat by the river. "No, no Chokha! Don't let your faith be shaken by the words of the priest," he told himself. "If you so desire, the Lord will take you into his temple. Now! It was the chanting of his name that gave you peace, remember!" "What peace? Peace that could be taken away by any priest?" a darker voice whispered. "No! A peace because of which I am with you, Chokha," a divine, inner voice spoke. "Come to me. Now if you will."

    Chokha rose and found himself walking towards the temple. In the dark night, however, he stood rooted to the ground outside the main gate, the absolute limits man had set for him. "Enter, Chokha. Enter. Why do you hesitate? Have you no faith in me?" the divine voice called from inside. "But I'll pollute you. I am a Mahar." "If I could be polluted by you, I wouldn't be God. Why, I wouldn't have given you life and the power of yearning for me!"

    Chokha felt himself being irresistibly drawn to the sanctum sanctorum. "Open the door! Come on! Open it! Your faith has brought you thus far. Draw the latch and fling the doors open." His hands pulled away the heavy bolt, and he was inside. "Come, Chokha come..." He ran up to the beautiful idol and hugging its feet, fell in a divine swoon.

    When Chokha came to, it was morning. It did not surprise or shock him in the least to find himself where he was. Just then, the head temple priest burst in. "Who dared open these doors before I..." When he saw Chokha, he was outraged, shocked beyond reason. "Y-you, you filthy Mahar! How dare you come in here? H-have you forgotten your Dharma? A-aren't you afraid of your Karma?" "I know nothing of Dharma and Karma, Veda or Shastra," Chokha replied serenely. "H-how dare you defile the temple! The Lord!"

    "How can I defile God?" Chokha smiled. "Can an untouchable pollute the Ganga? Can a sinner walking over mother earth destroy her sanctity? It's so simple, O priest. Don't you understand? God is above pollution. I have seen him, spoken with him." "Quiet! You madman!" "How did you enter this place? Answer me!" the priest demanded. "Why, God led me in." "You shameless liar! You have the audacity to accuse the Lord after defiling him! Get out of here! At once! Before the devotees come and stone you to death! In fact you should no longer remain in Pandharpur, lest the Lord take you into the temple again!" Without a word, Chokha left the temple and went away from Pandharpur to the opposite bank of the river.

    Meanwhile the priest, even though he had exiled Chokha, could not forget him, his words, or the episode in the temple. "God is above pollution. I have seen him. Spoken with him! Why, God led me in!" the words haunted him. "Were they the words of a madman? Or did the Lord really appear before him? If so, why hasn't he appeared before me? I have served him all these years and after all, I belong to the highest caste. No. It can't be. The man is a liar, a cheat. But would any man dare lie about the Lord? O Lord, what am I to believe?" The smug priest had completely lost his complacency! He was racked by doubts and could not concentrate on his duties in the temple.

    At last, one day, he decided, "I'll go across the river and meet the man again. I must for my own sake." As he approached Chokha's hut on the other side of the river, he heard Chokha praying. "Why are you silent when I call you so piteously?" Chokha wept. "Chokha's voice! He's calling the Lord! Will the Lord appear? Will he speak to him? Let's see," the priest thought, hiding behind the wall.

    "You took me to your house only to have me thrown out," Chokha wept softly. "I found you only to lose you again." Suddenly, Chokha smiled radiantly. He spoke again, his voice filled with divine authority. "Chokha! Can I throw you out when I am within you? Have you lost yourself, Chokha, that you talk of losing me?" "No Vithala! I have always had you and always will." "You dwelt in me all along and I like a fool looked for you outside," Chokha beamed. "The universe is your house! My heart, your temple!"

    "Liar! Impostor! Cheat!" the angry priest burst in, hitting Chokha with his staff. "So that was your trick! Talking to yourself and telling us that you spoke to God!" He neither winced nor cowered from the painful blow. Chokha simply stood there and smiled at the priest with absolute, radiant peace. "Why does he stand there and smile? Why does his smile disturb me?" the priest staggered back. "It's familiar! I've seen it somewhere." "Why did I hit him? Mahar he may be, but... he did obey my orders and leave Pandharpur."

    The priest crossed the river and made his way back to the temple, more disturbed than ever. "I have acted despicably. What right did I have to spy on his innocent games? He did not come in my way. Instead I came here and spied on him." He entered the sanctum sanctorum and closed his eyes. "O Lord, forgive me for my low behaviour." Then he opened his eyes and gazed up at the face of the idol. For the first time, he really saw the deity. "That smile! The Mahar's smile! It's the exact same..." he gasped.

    Then his gaze fell on the right cheek of the idol. Was he imagining things? He rubbed his eyes and looked again. "It is swollen! Unmistakably swollen! The Mahar... I hit the Mahar on his cheek!" The priest collapsed at the idol's feet. "O God, forgive me! Chokha, forgive me. What a fool! What an ignorant fool I have been! Oh, what a sinner!" When he rose from the foot of the idol, he was a completely different man. "By persecuting Chokha, I have persecuted God himself."

    Full of deep remorse, he went back across the river to Chokha's hut and fell at his feet. "Chokha, I have wronged you. Come, I beg you, for my sake, come back to the temple." "Arise, O priest," Chokha smiled gently. Chokha went with the priest and when they entered the sanctum, the priest pointed. "Look! See what I have done to the Lord. To his cheek..." But as Chokha smiled at the idol, the swelling magically disappeared. "The swelling has gone! I am forgiven!" the priest wept in joy.

    Chokha stayed on at Pandharpur, accepted by all, composing abhangs (devotional poems) on his experiences in the search for truth and for God. His beautiful abhangs are sung to this very day in Maharashtra.

    Pandharpur: The City of Vithala

    When Lord Panduranga came to him, Pundalika, his great devotee, was extremely busy looking after his aged parents. Not wanting to be disturbed from his filial duties, he pushed a brick towards the Lord—the only seat he could offer to the divine guest... and the Lord patiently stood on the brick.

    It is in this classic posture that we find the famous image of Panduranga today, in the great temple of Pandharpur on the bank of the river Chandrabhaga. When Bittideva Vishnuvardhana, the Hoysala king from Karnataka, built a grand house for Pundalika Varada Panduranga, the deity came to be known as 'Vithala'—a derivative of Bittideva.

    Later, Sant Namadeva sang: "The Kanada Vithala is standing on the banks of Bhivara (Bhima). The 'darshan' of the 'Kanadia' is good, his looks are pleasing." His looks were so pleasing that Krishnadeva Raya, the mighty Vijayanagara emperor, took the Vithala idol away to his own capital, Hampi. But some devoted Varkaris led by Bhanudas walked all the way to Hampi singing the praises of the Lord... and triumphantly brought him back home to Pandharpur.

    Jnaneshwar, Namadeva, Ekanath, Tukaram, Purandara Dasa, Chokha Mela, and many other saints from Maharashtra and Karnataka looked to Pundalika-Vithala-Panduranga-Vithala for immense spiritual inspiration. And to this day, Varkaris, or pilgrims to Pandharpur, undertake Vari—journeys at regular intervals—to the great temple of Vithala.
