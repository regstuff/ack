# The Fall of Bheeshma

On the ninth day of the great war, with Bheeshma in command, the Kaurava army fell with unprecedented fury upon the Pandava forces. Evil omens appeared; the sun seemed blotted out, and a rain of meteors fell from the sky, foreboding disaster. 

The young hero Abhimanyu led a fierce counter-attack. Chariots, cavalrymen, and elephants fell to his sure arrows. The Kaurava soldiers fled in terror, prompting Duryodhana to send the magical Rakshasa, Alambusha, against him. Alambusha used his magical powers to envelop the Pandava army in absolute darkness. But Abhimanyu released the *Bhaskara* (Sun) weapon, dispelling the darkness and illuminating the battlefield. Having destroyed the illusion, Abhimanyu tormented the Rakshasa with arrows until Alambusha fled in mortal fear. 

Meanwhile, the Kauravas set an entire battalion of elephant-warriors upon Bheema. Descending from his chariot, Bheema attacked them with his massive mace, crushing skulls and shattering bones. The frightened elephants retreated in panic, trampling their own Kaurava soldiers underfoot. 

As the battle raged, Bheeshma continued his relentless slaughter of the Pandava army. Virata and Drupada confronted him, but Bheeshma easily destroyed Virata's banner and bow, and killed his horses and charioteer. The Pandavas were taking heavy losses. 

At the end of the day, a deeply distressed Yudhishthira called a conference. "Only you can help us out of this dilemma, O Krishna," Yudhishthira pleaded. "We do not wish to kill Bheeshma, but if we do not, he may bring about the annihilation of our army."

"Do not grieve," Krishna consoled him. "At your command, I am ready to kill Bheeshma. But Arjuna has vowed to kill him. And as your friend, it is my duty to ensure that his vow does not go unfulfilled."

Together, the Pandavas and Krishna went unarmed to Bheeshma's tent in the dead of night. They paid obeisance to the Grandsire and asked him the fateful question: "O omniscient one! Tell us how you can be killed on the battlefield so as to save our army from slaughter."

Bheeshma smiled, knowing his time had come. "It is true that you cannot win the war as long as I am alive. The Pandavas, along with Krishna, must attack me. But I have taken a vow not to fight against a woman, or one who was formerly a woman. Let Arjuna shoot his arrows at me standing behind Shikhandi. Since I will not attack Shikhandi, I will be vulnerable."

Arjuna was horrified. "How could I bring myself to kill this grand old man in whose lap I have played as a child? How can I attack him when he chooses to be unarmed?"

Krishna reminded Arjuna of his Kshatriya duty. "Remember your Dharma. You vowed to kill him. You cannot win the war without killing him. Moreover, he chose to oppose you in war."

The next morning, the tenth day of the battle, the Pandava army set out with Shikhandi at its head. When the two armies clashed, Shikhandi confronted Bheeshma. 

"I shall kill you today, O Bheeshma," Shikhandi challenged. 

"I shall not take up arms against you," Bheeshma replied calmly, lowering his Gandeeva. 

Seeing this, Arjuna, shielding himself behind Shikhandi, rained an incessant shower of arrows on Bheeshma. The great patriarch was pierced by countless arrows from head to toe. With a few hours to go before sunset, Bheeshma looked to the sky in the east and fell from his chariot. 

He did not touch the ground. His body was suspended in the air, resting entirely on the bed of arrows that pierced his flesh. 

The battle immediately stopped. The gods in heaven lamented the fall of the mighty hero. Holy sages from Mansarovar took the form of swans and came to visit him. Bheeshma assured them, "I shall not die till the sun travels northward (Uttarayan)."

Warriors from both armies, overcome with grief, gathered around the fallen Grandsire. "My head hangs loose," Bheeshma said. "One of you must give me a head-rest."

Several soft and excellent pillows were brought, but Bheeshma refused them all. "These are not fit for a soldier." He looked at Arjuna. "O Arjuna! You have given me the most appropriate bed. Now, give me a head-rest befitting my bravery and courage."

Understanding his Grandsire's wish, Arjuna set three arrows on his Gandeeva and shot them into the ground directly under Bheeshma's head, supporting it perfectly. Bheeshma was pleased. "Give up strife now, O Kings," he urged Duryodhana and the others. "Let my death be the harbinger of peace." But his words of wisdom did not appeal to Duryodhana.

Faint with exhaustion and pain, Bheeshma uttered one word: "W-A-T-E-R!"

The kings rushed to bring pitchers brimming with cool water, but Bheeshma would not accept them. "I do not desire these. I wish to see Arjuna."

As Arjuna arrived, Bheeshma said, "O Arjuna! You alone can provide me with the divine water I wish to drink."

Arjuna climbed up his chariot, invoked the *Parjanya* weapon, and shot an arrow deep into the earth. Instantly, a jet of pure, sweet water sprang up directly into Bheeshma's mouth, quenching his thirst. The people watched in sheer amazement. 

Under the cover of night, Karna, who had abstained from fighting while Bheeshma was commander, approached the fallen patriarch. He bowed to him, tears in his eyes. 

"O Karna," Bheeshma said affectionately, "you are not Radha's son, but Kunti's son by the God Surya. I do not despise you. I spoke to you harshly only to diminish your energy and morale, and to prevent a schism in the family. The Pandavas are your brothers. Join hands with them. This is the favor I ask of you at this hour."

"O Bheeshma!" Karna replied. "I know that I am the son of Kunti. But my mother abandoned me at birth. I will sacrifice my wealth, my family, my very life for Duryodhana. I wish to follow my Dharma and continue fighting Arjuna. Bless me, O Grandsire, that I may fight this war."

Seeing his unwavering resolve, Bheeshma blessed him. "Very well, O Karna, if you are so resolved. Fight the war and attain heaven." 

With Bheeshma's blessing, Karna finally entered the great war.


# Drona's Vow

The fall of Bheeshma plunged the Kaurava army into profound grief. The hapless warriors turned to Karna to save them from what seemed like certain doom. Karna, having received Bheeshma's blessing, finally entered the battlefield, filling the Kaurava ranks with renewed hope. 

Duryodhana approached Karna for advice on who should be the next Commander-in-Chief. "I regard you as the man who can save us," Duryodhana said. 

"I would advise you to appoint the venerable warrior Drona as our next Commander-in-Chief," Karna replied. "He is the seniormost among us and has been a teacher to most of us."

Duryodhana formally requested Drona to lead the forces. Drona accepted the honor, but laid down a chilling condition. "I shall fight unto death all the Pandava warriors, except Dhrishtadyumna, for he is destined to kill me."

The next morning, Duryodhana came to Drona with a specific, strategic request. "O King! By giving me the command of the forces, you have bestowed an honor on me," Drona said. "Tell me, what may I do to please you?"

"O revered one! If you wish to please me, seize Yudhishthira alive and bring him before me," Duryodhana asked. "Because if Yudhishthira is killed, the rest of the Pandavas will never spare our lives. Instead, if Yudhishthira is captured, he will be persuaded to play dice again, and the Pandavas will once again be banished into the forest."

Drona was thoughtful. *I should have suspected his wicked motive,* he thought. Aloud, he laid down a counter-condition. "So long as Yudhishthira is under the protection of Arjuna, I cannot capture him. You must separate Arjuna from Yudhishthira."

Duryodhana agreed, and Drona made a public announcement of his vow to capture Yudhishthira, ensuring he could not go back on his word. 

When Yudhishthira learned of Drona's resolve, he spoke to Arjuna. "Think of a strategy that will ensure that I will be under your immediate protection," he asked. 

"O King, it is as reprehensible for me to kill my teacher as it is to abandon you," Arjuna replied. "Hence I shall not let either happen. The sky may fall to pieces, but Drona will not take you captive as long as I am alive."

The war resumed with explosive fury. Drona attacked and scattered Dhrishtadyumna's forces. Abhimanyu fought brilliantly, engaging in a spectacular duel with King Paurava, breaking his bow and sending him flying from his chariot. Jayadratha also fought fiercely, clashing with Abhimanyu and enduring the young prince's relentless assault. 

The next day, Duryodhana put his plan into action. The Samshaptakas (the Trigarta warriors led by King Susharma) challenged Arjuna to battle at the southern end of the battlefield, deliberately drawing him away from Yudhishthira. Bound by his Kshatriya honor to answer a challenge, Arjuna had no choice but to follow them. 

With Arjuna drawn away, Drona unleashed a ferocious attack on the center of the Pandava army, aiming straight for Yudhishthira. Dhrishtadyumna, Bheema, and Shikhandi fought desperately to hold him back, but Drona was an unstoppable force. 

Suddenly, a massive threat appeared from another quarter. King Bhagadatta, riding his gargantuan and invincible elephant Suprateeka, charged into the Pandava lines. The beast trampled chariots, horses, and men underfoot. 

Bheema rushed to confront the monstrous elephant. As the beast charged menacingly, Bheema ducked under its belly, a known strategy to bring raging elephants under control. He stroked its belly, briefly quieting the beast, before slipping out. However, the elephant quickly resumed its rampage, catching Satyaki's chariot in its trunk and hurling it far away. 

Far to the south, Arjuna, having devastated the Samshaptakas, heard the terrifying roar of Bhagadatta's elephant. "O Krishna, Bhagadatta's elephant is playing havoc with our army," Arjuna said, alarmed. "Take the chariot to him. I shall kill him."

As Arjuna arrived, Bhagadatta hurled his deadliest weapon at him—the Vaishnava *Astra*, an all-destroying, unconquerable missile. 

Arjuna prepared to counter it, but Lord Krishna suddenly stood up in the chariot, shielding Arjuna with his own body. The terrifying weapon struck Krishna squarely on the chest... and instantly transformed into a resplendent garland of flowers adorning the Lord's bosom. 

Arjuna was pained and confused. "O Krishna! It is not proper that you break your vow of not taking part in the war. You should let me do the fighting while I can."

Krishna smiled gently. "Many years ago, Prithvi, the Goddess Earth, approached me when I was in the form of Vishnu. She asked for my most powerful weapon to protect her son, Narakasura. I gave her this Vaishnava weapon, making Narakasura unconquerable. Bhagadatta obtained it from him. No one can endure the terrible impact of this weapon but me. O Arjuna, I have protected you by depriving him of the weapon. Proceed now to kill him."

With the deadly threat neutralized, Arjuna did not hesitate. With one shot, he sent Bhagadatta's mighty elephant to its death. With his next arrow, he pierced Bhagadatta's chest, ending the life of the great warrior. 

The day ended with Drona's vow unfulfilled. Yudhishthira was safe, but the cost had been immense, and the Kauravas, desperate for victory, were preparing even more treacherous strategies for the days to come.


# The Slaying of Abhimanyu

On the thirteenth day of the war, determined to fulfill his vow to capture Yudhishthira, Drona organized the Kaurava forces into an incredibly complex and virtually impenetrable formation known as the *Chakravyuha* (the Wheel Formation). 

Simultaneously, the Samshaptakas once again challenged Arjuna, drawing the invincible warrior and Lord Krishna to the far south of the battlefield, far away from the main conflict. 

Yudhishthira surveyed the massive, swirling Chakravyuha with growing despair. "Is there none in our army who can face Drona's wrath?" he asked his generals. "None of us know how to get across this Chakravyuha."

He turned to young Abhimanyu, the sixteen-year-old son of Arjuna and Subhadra. "O Abhimanyu, do something to save us the embarrassment when Arjuna returns. Besides Krishna, Arjuna, and Pradyumna, only you know how to break through the Chakravyuha."

"I know only how to penetrate the Chakravyuha," Abhimanyu replied respectfully. "O great King! For the glory of my race, I shall certainly force my way through this formidable formation. But... if I am trapped inside, I won't know how to find my way out. My father has not yet taught me how to come out of the Chakra formation."

"O Abhimanyu! Make an inroad into this impenetrable formation," Yudhishthira promised. "We will all follow you inside and protect you thereafter."

Bheema agreed vehemently. "At every point where you break through the formation, we will kill the mighty warriors of the Kauravas and continue to shatter the Chakra."

Assured of their backing, Abhimanyu ordered his charioteer to drive straight toward Drona. With breathtaking speed and skill, the young prince shattered the outer defenses and pierced deep into the Chakravyuha. 

However, as Bheema, Yudhishthira, Nakula, and Sahadeva attempted to follow him through the breach, they were suddenly blocked by a lone, immovable figure: King Jayadratha of Sindhu. 

Empowered by the boon he had received from Lord Shiva—that he could defeat all the Pandavas in battle for one day, except Arjuna—Jayadratha stood firm like Mount Meru. He unleashed an incessant fusillade of arrows, single-handedly holding back the four Pandava brothers and their entire army. Despite their desperate efforts, the breach closed, and they were shut out. 

Inside the Chakravyuha, Abhimanyu was completely isolated. Yet, the young lion fought with the ferocity of a seasoned veteran. He created illusions, making it seem as if there were thousands of Abhimanyus attacking the Kauravas. He effortlessly slew Duryodhana's brave son, Lakshmana, sending shockwaves through the Kaurava ranks. 

"Kill him!" an enraged Duryodhana screamed at his generals. "This son of a charioteer... I mean, this boy is destroying our army!"

Drona, Kripa, Karna, Ashwatthama, Brihadbala, and Kritavarma—six of the greatest Maharathis of the Kaurava army—surrounded the lone, sixteen-year-old warrior. It was a gross violation of the established rules of war, which dictated that multiple warriors should not attack a single combatant simultaneously. 

"How can we kill Abhimanyu, O Grandsire?" Karna asked Drona, seeing the boy's impenetrable defense. 

"His armor is impenetrable," Drona analyzed coldly. "Try, if you can, to cut his bow string from behind, and destroy his chariot."

Following this dishonorable advice, Karna sneaked behind Abhimanyu and shot an arrow that severed the young prince's bowstring. Immediately, Kripa killed his rear attendants, and Kritavarma slew his horses. 

Deprived of his chariot and bow, the young warrior remained undaunted. He picked up a sword and shield and jumped into the fray. But Drona cut the sword at the hilt with sharp arrows, and Karna shattered his shield. 

Defenseless, Abhimanyu picked up the heavy wheel of his broken chariot and rushed at Drona, but the Kaurava warriors mercilessly shattered the wheel into pieces with their arrows. 

Finally, Abhimanyu grabbed a mace and rushed at Ashwatthama, who recoiled in fear. Abhimanyu demolished chariots and killed elephants with his bare hands and the mace. 

Duhshasana's son also took up a mace and charged at Abhimanyu. The two exhausted warriors struck each other simultaneously, both collapsing to the ground from the heavy blows. The son of Duhshasana was the first to recover. As Abhimanyu struggled to rise, Duhshasana's son struck a heavy, fatal blow on Abhimanyu's forehead. 

Abhimanyu fell, never to rise again. 

The Kaurava warriors rejoiced and danced around the fallen boy, celebrating a victory achieved through deceit and cowardice. 

When the sun set, the Pandava camp was plunged into an abyss of sorrow. Yudhishthira wept inconsolably, blaming himself for sending the boy to his death. The great Sage Vyasa appeared and consoled him, reminding him that even the most virtuous men are eventually consumed by death. 

Meanwhile, Arjuna, having defeated the Samshaptakas, was returning to the camp. His heart beat unusually fast, and his voice quavered. "A nameless anxiety grips me, O Keshava," he told Krishna. "I hope my brother, King Yudhishthira, is well."

Upon entering the silent, grieving camp, Arjuna guessed the truth. When Yudhishthira narrated the tragic events—how they were blocked by Jayadratha and how the six Maharathis had mercilessly slaughtered Abhimanyu—Arjuna's sorrow turned into an apocalyptic rage. 

"I will kill Jayadratha tomorrow!" Arjuna roared, his eyes burning like fire. "If I fail to kill Jayadratha by sundown tomorrow, I will consign myself to flames!"

He picked up his Gandeeva and twanged the string, the sound echoing like thunder. Lord Krishna blew his conch, Panchajanya, and Arjuna blew Devadatta. 

The terrifying sound reached the Kaurava camp, striking fear into the hearts of the very men who had rejoiced earlier. The stage was set for the fourteenth day of the war—a day that would decide the fate of Jayadratha, Arjuna, and the war itself.
