# Amrapali and Upagupta: Two Buddhist Tales

## 1. Amrapali

The Licchavi nobles, who ruled over ancient Vaishali, were great lovers of beauty and took a keen interest in maintaining magnificent state gardens. They employed the best of gardeners. One day, the chief gardener was shocked to find an extraordinarily beautiful young woman resting under a mango tree. 
"Who are you, fair lady? What is your name? Where have you come from?" he asked. 
"I don't have a name," the lady replied softly. "I come from nowhere and have nowhere to go. If you do not mind, I would like to live here."
The gardener agreed but informed the nobles of the strange, enchanting maiden in their state garden. 

When the nobles rushed to the garden and saw her, they were instantly captivated. A fierce, bloody fight broke out among them, with each noble claiming the "mango-girl" for himself. 
"Stop!" the young woman cried out in distress. "Do I have no choice in the matter? Can I not have my say?"
Realizing their foolishness, the nobles agreed. "You choose one among us, Amrapali. The one you choose shall marry you."

Amrapali shook her head. "If I choose one of you, I will no doubt be making him very happy. But I will be making all the others jealous and angry. So I will not marry any of you."
The wise elders of Vaishali supported her decision. "Amrapali cannot belong to one person. She shall belong to Vaishali." They presented the beautiful garden to her and built her a grand house. 

To entertain the nobles, Amrapali began giving dance performances. She proved to be an excellent dancer with matchless rhythm, and soon became the pride of Vaishali. 
One evening, during her performance, a messenger rushed in with terrible news: the Magadhan army, led by the dreaded King Bimbisara, was marching towards Vaishali! The nobles immediately left for the battlefield. Watching them ride off, Amrapali thought sadly, "Why do men fight? One moment they appreciate beauty and art; the next, they thirst for blood."

A few days later, a handsome stranger called on her. He had come a long way just to see her dance. Captivated by his winsome personality, Amrapali danced for him and, learning he had no place to stay, invited him to stay at her house. 
They spent a wonderful week together. But when the stranger announced he had to leave, he asked, "I love you, Amrapali. Why don't you come away with me?"
"You amuse me. I don't even know your name!" she laughed. 
"I am Bimbisara," he replied. 
Amrapali was shocked. "The dreaded King of Magadha! How can you talk of love when because of you, hundreds of men are dying on the battlefield?"

"If I give up my ambition to conquer Vaishali and cease hostilities forthwith, will you marry me?" Bimbisara pleaded. 
"No! I cannot!" Amrapali refused. "I belong to Vaishali! I have given my pledge."
Dejected, Bimbisara turned and left. Amrapali found herself surprisingly sad to see him go. 

True to his word, Bimbisara called off the battle. The nobles returned and asked Amrapali to resume the dance she had left unfinished. But as she danced, the nobles noticed her movements were lifeless; her heart was clearly not in it. 

Amrapali fell into a deep depression. Her presents disgusted her, and she felt lonely and afraid. One morning, she saw a young monk carrying a fresh, beautiful lotus in her garden. She offered to buy it, but the monk politely refused. "Haven't you heard? Lord Buddha has come to Vaishali and is camping at Kotigrama. I am taking this flower to him."

Intrigued by the enlightened one who knew the cause of suffering, Amrapali visited Kotigrama. She listened to Lord Buddha with rapt attention: "Out of desire is born grief. Out of desire alone is born fear. Free yourself from desire and the grief and the fear will wither away."
His soothing words filled her with a new, profound peace. She began visiting him daily, wishing he would visit her home, but doubting he would visit an "unworthy" person like her. 

As Lord Buddha prepared to leave Vaishali, Amrapali bravely ran up to him and fell at his feet. "Master, will you honor me by eating at my house tomorrow?"
"I will come, my child," the Buddha smiled. 

Overjoyed, Amrapali sped home in her chariot. On the way, she encountered the nobles of Vaishali, who were also rushing to invite the Buddha to dine at their palace. "I have forestalled you!" Amrapali announced. "The Lord has already agreed to dine at my house."
The nobles tried to buy her off, offering her all the gold she wanted. "No. Even if you offer me the kingdom of Vaishali, I won't give up the honor of serving the Lord!" she declared proudly. 
The nobles went to the Buddha to change his mind, but he gently refused them, honoring his promise to Amrapali. 

The next day, Lord Buddha arrived at Amrapali's house. As she served him, she felt immense joy. 
"Amrapali, you have begun to know the joy of giving," the Buddha observed.
"Lord, permit me to grant my garden and house to the Sangha, to be used as a monastery," she requested humbly. 
"So be it, child."

Leaving behind her worldly life and its heartaches, Amrapali entered the Buddhist order, leading a life of renunciation and finally finding the true peace of mind she had craved.

## 2. Upagupta

Long, long ago in ancient Mathura, there lived a dancer named Vasavadatta, famed for her beauty and her art. She was admired by all, yet she was known for her hard heart, spurning the wealthy nobles who threw themselves at her feet. "Not one of them possesses what I am looking for," she told her companion. 

One evening, from her balcony, Vasavadatta saw a young, handsome Buddhist monk passing by in the street below. Struck by his serene appearance, she immediately sent her companion to invite him in. 
But the monk politely declined. "Not now. I will see her at the proper time."
Piqued, Vasavadatta sent her friend back, offering him gifts and riches, thinking he was ashamed of his poverty. 
"I cannot. It is not yet time to visit Vasavadatta," the monk repeated, and walked away. 
This monk was Upagupta, a devoted disciple of Lord Buddha. 

Vasavadatta was stunned. "Why should the only man I choose to love, shun me?" she brooded. She stopped performing, plunging Mathura into gloom. 
Hoping to distract her, her friend took her to an exhibition by the city's chief sculptor. Vasavadatta was captivated by a particular sculpture and offered to buy it at any price. 
"It's yours, if you agree to dance again," the sculptor bargained. 

Vasavadatta reluctantly agreed, and her return to the stage was met with thunderous applause. Yet, the adulation only made her brood more over the monk who had rejected her. 
The chief sculptor soon began visiting her house regularly to capture her image in stone. But a few days later, the sculptor vanished mysteriously. 

Shortly after, the sculptor's murdered body was found buried near Vasavadatta's house. The authorities charged her with the murder. Having no defense, she was found guilty. Her property was confiscated, and she was banished from Mathura. As she was turned out of her house, an angry mob jeered and brutally pelted her with stones. 

Bleeding profusely and suffering from severe, festering wounds, the disgraced Vasavadatta found refuge in a crematorium on the outskirts of the city. People passing by looked upon her as an untouchable, spitting at her and sometimes even throwing stones at her. 

Then came Upagupta, the Buddhist monk. 
As he approached, Vasavadatta tried to hide her ruined face. "Friend, cover my body. Let him not see me in this state," she wept to her companion. 
"Vasavadatta, I have come to you," Upagupta said gently. 

"O monk, when all Mathura admired me, you alone spurned me," she cried. "Why have you chosen to visit me now when I am a mass of festering flesh, shunned by all?"
Upagupta smiled with deep compassion. "At that time you did not need me, Vasavadatta. You do, now. Come, let us go to my monastery."

Upagupta took her in and nursed her back to health. But as she healed, she grew depressed. "You seem sad, Vasavadatta."
"I am no longer beautiful. No one will look at me now," she grieved. 
"Vasavadatta, you are yet to discover a beauty greater than the one you have lost—the beauty of the self," Upagupta taught her. 

Curious to find this inner light, Vasavadatta began attending the discourses of Lord Buddha. 
"You can't call even the body your own," the Buddha taught. "When the body is cast away it becomes food for others. Light the lamp within you, and only then will you find peace."

Moved by his teachings, Vasavadatta fell at the feet of Lord Buddha. "Lord, admit me into your order."
"So be it, my child," the Buddha blessed her. "May you find peace of mind."
